What actually makes modern science different from every other attempt to understand the world? Philosopher of science Michael Strevens argues the answer is not the scientific method as commonly understood, but a single behavioral rule: settle every dispute through empirical evidence alone, and leave everything else at the door. In this conversation, Strevens traces the origins of this "iron rule of explanation," shows why Popper and Kuhn were both right and wrong, explains how ordinary, self-interested scientists working within imperfect institutions nevertheless produce the most powerful knowledge-generating machine in history, and explores why science took so long to emerge if the rule is so simple. The conversation closes on the ontological status of scientific truth and the most pressing challenge science faces today: communicating uncertain knowledge to a public that demands yes-or-no answers.

## Who is Michael Strevens?

Michael Strevens is a philosopher of science at New York University. His research spans theories of complex systems, statistical explanation, and the social structure of science. His book The Knowledge Machine (2020) offers a sweeping account of what makes modern science uniquely powerful,and why its success depends on a rule that forces scientists to behave in ways that are, philosophically speaking, deeply strange.

## Top 3 theses

- Modern science is distinguished not by any particular method or mindset, but by a single institutional rule: all disputes must be settled through empirical evidence alone. Philosophical, theological, and aesthetic arguments are banned from scientific journals,regardless of whether any individual scientist finds them compelling.

- Popper and Kuhn were both right about the most important thing,that small, intractable empirical facts are what ultimately topple great theories,but both wrong about the scientist’s psychology. Real scientists are neither Popper’s hyper-rational falsificationists nor Kuhn’s almost credulous paradigm-followers. They are ordinary people, which is why institutional structure matters more than individual virtue.

- The iron rule works precisely because it forces scientists with completely different background beliefs, aesthetic preferences, and philosophical commitments to do more experiments rather than argue. Disagreement is not an obstacle to scientific progress; it is the engine of it, because the only permitted way to win a disagreement is to produce more data.

## Key Insights

**1. The iron rule is artificial, and that is the point.**

Science’s power does not come from scientists being more rational or objective than other people. It comes from a rule,enforced at the level of publication and peer review,that bans any non-empirical argument from settling a dispute. Philosophers may argue aesthetics, theologians may argue revelation, but in a scientific journal the only currency is evidence. This is not how scientists privately think; it is a constraint on how they publicly argue. Strevens calls this the iron rule of explanation, and he argues it is the single most important institutional innovation in human history.

**2. Popper and Kuhn were both right,about what matters and wrong about how it works.**

Both philosophers agreed that small, stubborn empirical anomalies are what ultimately bring down great theories. Where they differed was on the mechanism. Popper thought scientists were constitutionally devoted to falsification; Kuhn thought they were constitutionally devoted to their paradigm. Both pictures are too extreme. Actual sociological studies of working scientists found people doing their best with the tools at hand,sometimes admirably, sometimes self-servingly, mostly just normally. The institution, not the individual, is what converts ordinary human behavior into reliable theory-testing.

**3. Disagreement produces more data, and more data is how science wins.**

The Eddington eclipse experiments are Strevens’ central example. In 1919, three telescopes gave conflicting results about the bending of light by gravity. Eddington, a committed Einsteinian, dismissed the results that favored Newton. Critics pushed back. But because both sides had agreed to argue only through evidence, the only available move was to run the experiment again. And again. Over the following decades, dozens of telescopes pointed at dozens of eclipses produced a mountain of data that eventually made the Einsteinian answer inescapable. Disagreement, under the iron rule, is a data-generation machine.

**4. Scientists privately believe things science officially forbids.**

Many physicists,including Eddington himself, a devout Quaker,are motivated by aesthetic, theological, or philosophical convictions that never appear in their papers. String theorists speak of mathematical beauty as a guide to truth. The founders of quantum mechanics held views about consciousness and observer-dependence that most of their successors quietly ignore. The iron rule does not require scientists to abandon these motivations; it only requires that they not cite them in print. This is what makes the rule so strange and so effective: it harvests the energy of private conviction while banning it from public argument.

**5. Science’s biggest challenge is not internal but communicative.**

Strevens identifies the most pressing problem facing science today not as the replication crisis or p-value debates, but the gap between how science actually works and how the public is told it works. Institutions issue yes-or-no pronouncements,“masks work”, “alcohol kills the virus”,stripped of the uncertainty, the contested evidence, and the ongoing disagreement that characterize real science in progress. This produces a lose-lose: either the public sees science as mere authority (and finds anti-authority blogs equally authoritative), or nuance is provided and no one can process it. No one has solved this problem.
