Steven Sloman has spent his career studying how thinking actually works, and discovering, repeatedly, that it works far less than we assume. In this conversation, he walks through the Knowledge Illusion: the pervasive human tendency to believe we understand things we cannot begin to explain. He demonstrates the phenomenon across ballpoint pens, political policies, and toilet mechanisms, then traces its deeper cause, we confuse knowledge stored in other people's heads with knowledge in our own. The conversation moves into evolutionary origins, the explosion of complexity from hunter-gatherer bands to internet-age civilisation, the limits of online information access, and what all of this means for expertise, education, and politics. Sloman distinguishes collaboration from outsourcing as separate cognitive skills, engages critically with Hugo Mercier's argumentative theory of reason, and reflects on how short-lived the puncturing of the illusion actually is, and what it takes to make intellectual humility last.

## Who Is Steven Sloman?

Steven Sloman is Professor of Cognitive, Linguistic, and Psychological Sciences at Brown University. He holds a PhD in Psychology from Stanford University and completed postdoctoral work at the University of Michigan. A cognitive scientist by training, he studies how people think, reason, and make decisions, with a particular focus on causal reasoning and the social distribution of knowledge. He is co-author (with Philip Fernbach) of _The Knowledge Illusion: Why We Never Think Alone_ (2017).

## Top 3 theses

- People systematically overestimate how well they understand the world. When asked to explain how ordinary objects or policies work, they discover they cannot, and their confidence drops. This illusion of understanding is not a failure of intelligence but a structural feature of human cognition: we blur the boundary between knowledge in our own heads and knowledge distributed across our community.

- The right response to epistemic humility is not paralysis but better calibration of trust: choose not the expert with the most confident answer, but the expert most responsive to evidence and most willing to revise their position when the data change.

- Education and politics both suffer from treating knowledge as an individual possession. Reforming them requires treating knowledge as what it actually is, a community property, which means designing institutions around collaboration, outsourcing, and the honest acknowledgement of what individuals do not and cannot know.

## Key Insights

**1. The Knowledge Illusion is structural, not accidental.**

We do not merely fail to know things, we confidently believe we understand things we cannot explain. The original research (the “illusion of explanatory depth”) showed that asking people to explain rather than merely evaluate a device or policy reliably punctures their sense of understanding. The cause is deeper than laziness: the mind does not distinguish between knowledge held internally and knowledge accessible in the surrounding community. As long as the knowledge is reachable, in a friend, a plumber, a Wikipedia article, it feels, functionally, like our own.

**2. We live in a community of knowledge, not individual minds.**

The book’s deeper claim is not merely that people are ignorant but that ignorance is the design. Civilisation works because knowledge is distributed: no individual needs to understand how a ballpoint pen or an aircraft works as long as the system as a whole contains people who do. The cognitive architecture that allows us to feel we understand things we cannot explain is the same architecture that makes the division of cognitive labour possible and productive.

**3. The internet has made information easier to find but harder to evaluate.**

Early optimism that universal internet access would equalise knowledge has not been vindicated. For stable, low-stakes topics, how a toilet works, the information is abundant and reliable. For complex, contested domains, economics, public health, political policy, search amplifies the illusion: one easily-found argument produces confidence without the surrounding counter-arguments that would reveal how partial it is. The problem is not access but discrimination: knowing which sources to trust, and recognising how much remains unknown.

**4. Expertise is imperfect but still the least-bad option.**

Sloman takes seriously the objection that experts have been catastrophically wrong, psychiatry is his sharpest example, but argues that the lesson is not scepticism of expertise in general but a more targeted criterion: look for expertise that is governed by data, responsive to evidence, and constrained to falsifiable claims. Psychiatry went wrong when it operated as ideology rather than science; it improved as it submitted to data. The same principle applies to any field. Vote for the politician who tells you whose advice they take, not the one who tells you they already have the answer.

**5. Puncturing the illusion is easy; making the lesson last is hard.**

Asking people to explain their understanding reliably lowers their confidence, but the effect evaporates within minutes. Sloman’s unpublished research suggests that what helps is social proof: learning that this failure to explain is universal, not personal. When participants discovered that other people share the same experience of having their illusion punctured, the humility lasted longer. The implication for education and public discourse is that intellectual humility must be cultivated as a shared norm, not merely demonstrated as an individual exercise.

**6. Collaboration and outsourcing are distinct cognitive skills.**

Sloman distinguishes between working well with people you are explicitly collaborating with, which requires empathy, integration, and interpersonal skill, and the separate capacity to outsource thinking to the wider body of available knowledge. The first is collaboration; the second is intellectual humility in practice. Both are needed, and education tends to under-develop the second. The growing complexity of research (co-authored papers, multi-continent physics experiments) suggests that both will matter more, not less, as fields develop.

**7. Motivated reasoning means the conclusion usually precedes the argument.**

Engaging with Hugo Mercier’s argumentative theory of reason, Sloman endorses the core observation: in domains with enough uncertainty to reason around, people generate arguments to justify positions they already hold rather than to find the truth. The conclusion typically comes first, often inherited from the community, and the reasoning follows as rationalisation. The practical upshot: in contested political domains, it is nearly impossible to change someone’s mind by argument alone, because the argument they deploy is a justification for a prior commitment, not an open inquiry.
