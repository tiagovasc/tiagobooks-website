Nathan Dufour Oglesby is a classicist, rapper, and philosopher who makes songs about critical theory, economic ideology, and the history of ideas. In this conversation, he traces the deep continuity between ancient poetic wisdom and modern philosophy, arguing that the split between artistic expression and philosophical argument is a modern invention that impoverished both. He walks through his own haphazard path from Latin major to PhD in classics, explains how hip-hop functions as a mode of trenchant social discourse rather than simple entertainment, and introduces Alfred North Whitehead’s process philosophy, a system in which the universe is not a collection of static things governed by external laws, but a self-creating organism choosing its own forms in collaboration with God. The conversation closes on the tension between system-building and mystery: whether Whitehead’s refusal of essentialism leaves room for the sacred, or quietly forecloses it.

## Top 3 Theses

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; The division between artistic expression and philosophical argument is a modern invention. In antiquity, wisdom transmission was always performative and quasi-poetic, the split comes from Plato systematizing Socrates and Aristotle systematizing Plato, not from some natural distinction between art and thought.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Hip-hop is not a degraded popular form but one of the healthiest surviving modes of public discourse: a place where ideas are exchanged, institutions are critiqued, and the lived experience of a community is articulated with precision. What reaches mainstream radio is not representative of what the form is capable of.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Whitehead’s process philosophy demands a complete inversion of our ordinary metaphysics: the universe is not made of static things with fixed essences but of events in collaboration, with God functioning not as an external lawgiver but as a repository of possibilities that the universe freely takes up and sets aside.

## Key Insights

1.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; **Philosophy and poetry were once inseparable.** In the ancient Greek world, the distinction between what we now call lyrical verse and philosophical argument was nonexistent. Both moved through the same poetic, performative medium at symposiums and public gatherings. The separation begins with Plato’s systematization of Socratic questioning and accelerates with Aristotle, who first makes philosophy feel like reading a textbook. Prior to that, wisdom transmission was always quasi-poetic in form.

2.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; **The addiction to novelty is the defining pathology of modern art.** When art is commodified, novelty becomes the primary selling mechanism, because you cannot sell someone what they already have. This produces an arms race of iconoclasm in the mainstream. But ancient theater shows a different model: audiences knew the Oedipus story before seeing Sophocles’ version of it. The pleasure came from how the myth was unfolded, not from surprise at the ending. A work that can be ‘spoiled’ by a spoiler is probably not worth your time.

3.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; **Hip-hop is a mode of discourse before it is a genre of entertainment.** From Public Enemy’s Chuck D calling hip-hop ‘the Black CNN’ to Kendrick Lamar receiving a Pulitzer Prize, the form has always functioned as a vehicle for critiquing institutions, articulating lived experience, and conducting public dialogue at a high level. What dominates mainstream radio, party songs, has always been dominant, but it has never been the whole picture. The intellectual tradition of the form runs continuously from the late 1980s to the present.

4.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; **Classics is not about worshipping marble busts, the busts were painted.** The outside perception of classical antiquity as staid, somber, and monochrome is precisely backwards. The Greeks themselves were fully aware of the debt they owed to Egyptian and Near Eastern civilizations, and the early poets and philosophers were openly interested in where they got their ideas from. The myth of the Greeks as sole inventors of reason is a modern construction with no support in the actual ancient literature.

5.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; **Whitehead’s process philosophy inverts the standard metaphysics.** Rather than a universe of static things with fixed essences, Whitehead sees a universe of events in process. An actual entity is not a thing but a happening, a puff of existence defined by its correlations with other entities. The fallacy of misplaced concreteness is to mistake the useful abstractions we impose on this flux (species, essences, human nature) for the actual fabric of reality. Reality is seething flow; the forms we name are temporary gatherings within it.

6.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; **God in Whitehead’s system is not a lawgiver but a collaborator.** Rather than imposing order from outside, God in process philosophy functions as a storehouse of possibilities, forms the universe can take up and set aside as it creates itself from the inside. The universe is not an instance of laws imposed by divine fiat; it is a self-organizing organism choosing its own forms through a collaborative, evolutionary process. This makes God immanent in the world and the world immanent in God simultaneously, with neither having metaphysical priority over the other.

7.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; **Post-modern deconstruction and process philosophy are two flavors of the same critique.** Both Deleuze and Whitehead are saying that what we take to be fixed, essential, and law-governed is actually flux, difference, and process. But Deleuze’s deconstructive emphasis is critical and antagonistic, break down the ideological structures, while Whitehead’s constructive emphasis asks how things are made and finds in that making a kinship with animistic religion, ecological thinking, and spiritual practice rather than with Marxist critique.

Most people are relatively familiar with Plato, probably because at some point they were made to read _The Republic_. _The Republic_ is a dialogue between Socrates and others about the nature of justice and the good. **T:** Getting into Whitehead. You first started as a mathematician. I'm curious about how he viewed mathematics in relationship to Platonism. **N:** That's a really good question. One of the core concepts for Whitehead is the "Fallacy of Misplaced Concreteness." It means: don't mistake abstractions for actualities. The world is process, constant change, alteration, and evolution. In it, we can analyze certain forms, but don't mistake them for being concrete. For instance, we think of a human being as this essential category with a fixity to it, but really we're a process of cells, molecules, atoms, etc. It's all seething and flowing. It makes sense to think of a human being as a static thing for discussion, just like it makes sense to say a mountain is a static thing, but the mountain too is just a process. The Fallacy of Misplaced Concreteness is to impose on reality the characteristics of essential static forms that it doesn't actually have. It's pushing against Aristotelian essentialism—the idea that a thing has a finite and unchanging essence. However, in that flux, we do have certain forms that repeat themselves. Whitehead calls these "Eternal Objects," which are similar to Platonic Forms in that they are repeatable structures. The important difference is how they relate to God. Whitehead implies there is an entity called God whose job is to bestow to this constant flux the possibilities for taking a certain form. God is like a storehouse of forms. But it's not like the traditional image of God creating the universe and imposing laws from the outside. Rather, the universe itself chooses its forms. The flux of reality gathers in relationships and takes a certain form offered by God, and then that form dissipates again. The universe is creating itself from the inside out through partaking of forms.

**N:** It is a process philosophy, not a materialistic one. When we think about actualities, we need not think of them in terms of things, but events. An Actual Entity is an event.
