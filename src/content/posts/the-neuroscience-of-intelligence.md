**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Neuroscience of Intelligence_’ by_ Richard J. Haier**

I am deeply impressed by this book. Given that it is part of a distinguished series by Cambridge University Press and written by Richard J. Haier, one of the leading scientists on intelligence, I had high hopes, but it managed to surpass them. From the get-go, what I loved the most was the fact that the author clearly admitted his bias towards biological explanations of intelligence. Everyone is biased, so admitting and putting forth your bias is infinitely better than trying to hide it or being unaware of it. Nevertheless, Haier provides robust reasoning for his stance, supported by his forty years of research in the field. He argues that while social factors matter, the biological basis is often unfairly ignored, and he seeks to rectify that balance.

The book is surprisingly accessible and well written. It gives a succinct and necessary introduction to how intelligence research got started, covering its controversies and myths before moving on to the impact of genetics, neuroimaging, and intelligence manipulation. It ends with a nice reflection on the field in general. All of this is written in a manner that anyone without a neuroscience background could read, which is very impressive. I was also pleasantly surprised by how intellectually and scientifically honest Haier was throughout the text. He was always careful to remind the reader of the limitations of the evidence and how no single study is conclusive. He emphasized typical problems of neuroscience, such as low sample sizes or lack of replication, and frequently pointed to null results and contradictory evidence. This transparency is crucial yet not very common in popular science writing.

Haier systematically dismantles popular misconceptions, establishing that intelligence can indeed be defined for scientific research and that the _g_-factor is a valid concept for estimating it. He presents overwhelming evidence from modern quantitative genetic studies that supports a major role of genes in intelligence. While the environment plays a role during early childhood, the data suggests this diminishes almost entirely by the teenage years, and compensatory education has largely failed to improve IQ in the long term. He explains that intelligence is likely a by-product of many genes with small effects rather than a single "intelligence gene." He also details the Parieto-Frontal Integration Theory (PFIT), which emphasizes specific brain areas and their connections. Interestingly, higher intelligence seems to correlate with the ability to process information efficiently, often showing an inverse correlation between IQ scores and brain activity. The book also touches on the future, suggesting that chronometrics—tracking information processing in units of time—may be the measurement of the future.

Haier remains humble about the possibility of being wrong and explicitly invites the reader to prove him so. In the final chapter, he challenges the reader to find weak links and loopholes in his presentation and to design new research studies to falsify them. While I found this rigorous approach refreshing, I do wish the book explored more on the role of the environment, brain plasticity, and epigenetics. On the other hand, I sometimes felt he went into too much detail regarding neuroimaging techniques, which added little to his argument as a whole for a general reader. But of course, that is just my opinion and a hard thing to balance. Ultimately, given the strong evidence for the role of neurobiology and genetics in cognitive ability, Haier makes a compelling case that it is incorrect to blame a lack of economic or educational success exclusively on poor motivation or social factors.
