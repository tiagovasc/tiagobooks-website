Arthur Versluis has spent decades excavating a tradition that modern philosophy almost entirely lost: the lineage of apophatic mysticism running from Plato through Plotinus, Dionysius the Areopagite, Meister Eckhart, and into the Christian theosophers of the seventeenth century. In this conversation, he traces how Platonism was eclipsed, not through refutation, but through the slow triumph of materialist thinking on both the left and the right, and why recovering it matters now more than at the time of Plato himself. He explains why the forms are less important than the movement from duality to unity, how the Hudson River School's luminism is a visual embodiment of the same illumination tradition, why Schelling opens a door that Kant closes, and what the ancient mysteries, John Pordage's trance states, and the dark night of the soul all share. The conversation closes with Versluis's argument that Buddhism provides the most precise contemplative maps available today, and that the West's urgent task is to recover and recognise its own mystical inheritance before it disappears entirely.

## Who Is Arthur Versluis?

Arthur Versluis is Professor in the Department of Religious Studies at Michigan State University and one of the leading authorities on Western esotericism, Platonic mysticism, and the history of American spirituality. His books include _Platonic Mysticism_, _Perennial Philosophy_, _American Gurus_, _American Gnosis_, _Entering the Mysteries_, and _Breaking Through the Materialistic Deception_ (2024-25). He is a founder of the journal _Esoterica_ and has spent his career recovering traditions that mainstream academic philosophy has neglected or mischaracterised.

## Top 3 theses

- The eclipse of Platonism is not an intellectual accident but a structural consequence of global materialism, a worldview so pervasive it shapes both Marxist dialectical materialism and capitalist economics simultaneously, leaving no mainstream foothold for a tradition centred on transcendence.

- The apophatic tradition, running from Dionysius the Areopagite through Meister Eckhart, the Christian theosophers, and into modernity, constitutes a single continuous lineage of direct spiritual insight that is essentially parallel to what Buddhism expresses in the Heart Sutra, and Versluis's work is, so far as he knows, the only contemporary book to recover and trace this lineage in its entirety.

- New Age spirituality fails not primarily because of its guru scandals but because it is structurally materialistic, it promises awakening without a path, collapses the discipline required for contemplative ascent, and thereby mirrors, rather than subverts, the consumerist culture it claims to transcend.

## Key Insights

**1. Platonism was eclipsed by materialism, not refuted by philosophy.**

Versluis distinguishes between the widespread misreading of Plato, particularly the charge of naive dualism, and the deeper structural reason Platonism has disappeared: the dominance of a materialist paradigm shared by both the political left (dialectical materialism) and the right (capitalism). Neither has conceptual room for transcendence. The result is not an intellectual defeat of Platonism but its cultural erasure by default.

**2. The forms are less important than the movement from duality to unity.**

In Versluis's reading, the Platonic forms are not the centre of gravity, the movement from separation and duality toward transcendence and unity is. The forms function as an intermediary, analogous to what Dionysius the Areopagite renders as celestial hierarchy and angelic intermediaries. What matters is the direction of travel: from immanence toward transcendence, from darkness toward illumination. The forms are a map feature, not the destination.

**3. The Hudson River School is a visual expression of the same illumination tradition.**

The luminism of nineteenth-century American painters like Frederick Church, the school's second name, Luminist, physically enacts the interplay of light and matter, transcendence and immanence, that Versluis traces in Plotinus, Dionysius, and Meister Eckhart. The light coming through these enormous canvases is not decorative but doctrinal: it manifests the same metaphysics of illumination that runs through apophatic mysticism. Rilke's Sonnets to Orpheus and the entire Romantic tradition in Europe perform the same function in poetry.

**4. Schelling opens the door to mysticism; Kant closes it.**

Versluis locates Kant squarely in the tradition that makes mysticism impossible: the veil between the subject and the thing-in-itself is permanent, and no contemplative ascent can pierce it. Schelling, by contrast, carries within his work the mystical tradition of Jacob Böhme, and his writings on the Samothracian mysteries show a philosopher who genuinely understood the ancient initiatory tradition. Entering Schelling is entering a vast contemplative landscape, though Versluis warns that his language is so dense and his references so wide-ranging as to be genuinely difficult.

**5. The descent into darkness is a structural feature of mystical transformation, not an anomaly.**

Across the Christian theosophers, particularly John Pordage, who endured years of psychic assault with his wife before arriving at trance states lasting up to two weeks, the ancient mysteries, and John of the Cross's dark night of the soul, a common structure emerges: darkness, fear, and ordeal precede illumination. Versluis notes that Plotinus and the early Dionysian tradition are exceptions, offering a more straightforwardly contemplative ascent without this gauntlet, suggesting that the dynamic, struggle-oriented dimension of mystical transformation intensified as Christianity developed.

**6. Perennial philosophy is not the claim that all traditions are the same, it is the claim that truth is real.**

Versluis distinguishes his perennialism sharply from the popular misreading that reduces it to “all religions are secretly identical.” His argument is epistemically prior: truth is not relative, maps can be better or worse, and Indianapolis is real regardless of whether the dean thinks excellence is a relative concept. Perennial philosophy is a resistance to the relativism that has colonised contemporary academia and popular culture, a resistance inflected throughout by the Platonic insistence that reality is not constructed but discovered.

**7. Buddhism provides the most precise contemplative cartography available.**

Despite his commitment to recovering the Western tradition, Versluis is candid that if you want precision in mapping the contemplative ascent, Vajrayana Buddhism, particularly the Mahamudra tradition, with its meticulous charting of the nature of mind, surpasses anything available in the West. Plotinus is good; Buddhism is better. Versluis uses this not to abandon the West but to argue more urgently for recovering Western mystical traditions: the maps exist, they are sophisticated, and the West has nearly forgotten it has them.
