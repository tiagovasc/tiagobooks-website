Psychology has never agreed on what its subject matter actually is. For over a century, schools of thought have carved up the human mind into incompatible pieces, behaviourism, psychodynamics, cognitive therapy, evolutionary psychology, each with insights and none with a shared foundation. Gregg Henriques, clinical psychologist and Professor at James Madison University, has spent thirty years building what he calls the Unified Theory of Knowledge (UTK): a single metatheoretical framework that resolves this fragmentation. In this conversation, Tiago Faleiro draws him through each layer of the system, the Enlightenment Gap, the Tree of Knowledge, Behavioural Investment Theory, the Justification Hypothesis, and the Influence Matrix, before turning to Henriques's first-hand account of how scientific corruption inside Aaron Beck's lab convinced him that the entire edifice of evidence-based psychotherapy is organised around brand promotion rather than truth. Dense, rich, and unusually candid.

## Who Is Gregg Henriques?

Gregg Henriques is Professor of Graduate Psychology at James Madison University, where he directs the Combined-Integrated Doctoral Program in Clinical and School Psychology. He trained at the University of Vermont, completed a postdoctoral fellowship at the University of Pennsylvania under Aaron Beck, and has spent three decades developing the Unified Theory of Knowledge, a metatheoretical system integrating philosophy of mind, evolutionary biology, complexity theory, and clinical psychology. He is incoming president of the Society for the Exploration of Psychotherapy Integration.

## Top 3 Theses

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Psychology has never established a shared ontology of its subject matter, what the mind actually is, and has papered over this failure by defining itself through methodology rather than object of study. This is not a minor gap but a foundational crisis that makes the entire field incoherent.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; The jump from animal to human is not a quantitative increase in cognitive complexity but a qualitative shift into a new plane of existence, the culture-person plane, driven by the emergence of propositional language and the justification dynamic it creates. Evolutionary psychology is useful but structurally insufficient to account for this shift.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; The dominant model of evidence-based psychotherapy is organised primarily around school-of-thought brand promotion rather than the pursuit of accurate knowledge. The dodo bird finding, that bona fide therapies produce roughly equivalent outcomes, points toward common factors rather than specific mechanisms, and the empirical apparatus built to validate specific brands is structurally corrupt.

## Key Insights

**1. The problem of psychology is ontological, not methodological.**

Every other mature science is anchored to a shared understanding of its object of study. Biologists agree on what a cell is. Chemists agree on what an atom is. Psychology never established an equivalent ontological foundation for the mental. Instead, it defined itself through methodology, applying the methods of science to whatever each school happened to study, and then suppressed acknowledgement of this failure. Henriques calls this the Problem of Psychology, and argues it is the root cause of the fragmented, school-of-thought landscape that has persisted for over a century.

**2. The Tree of Knowledge maps four irreducibly different planes of existence.**

Henriques’s Tree of Knowledge system plots the evolution of complexity not as a single continuum but as four distinct dimensional jumps: the matter plane (particles, atoms, molecules), the life plane (cells and organisms), the mind plane (nervous systems and animal behaviour), and the culture-person plane (language-based human consciousness). Each plane emerges from the previous via a new information-processing and communication network, DNA, nervous system, language, and generates genuinely novel ontological properties that cannot be reduced back to the prior plane. This reframes the debate between continuity and emergence: there is both, but at specific transitions, a new dimension comes into being.

**3. Propositional language creates the problem of justification, and with it, human consciousness.**

The jump from symbolic tagging to full propositional claims, ‘There are the antelope’, is the key transition that separates humans from other primates. A propositional claim opens up negative counterfactual space: it can be questioned, denied, or reframed. This is why humans uniquely ask questions, and why children relentlessly do so from age three. The cognitive pressure to legitimise propositional claims, to answer ‘Is that true?’, ‘Why?’, ‘Should we?’, is what Henriques calls the justification dynamic. Ego, culture, social institutions, and the entire human intersubjective world are, on his account, downstream of this single structural feature of language.

**4. The Influence Matrix tracks the four dimensions along which humans monitor their social world.**

Unlike other primates, humans evolved a highly integrated system for monitoring self-other relationships across four axes simultaneously: relational value and social influence (the core black line, am I known and valued, do I have influence?), vertical power (dominance and status), horizontal affiliation (warmth versus hostility), and freedom/closeness (dependence versus independence). These map directly onto the Big Five personality traits and onto Jonathan Haidt’s moral foundations, unifying personality psychology and moral psychology within a single evolutionary framework. Clinically, the matrix explains the structure of relational trauma, the dynamics of the therapeutic relationship, and the architecture of self-consciousness.

**5. The dodo bird finding is real, and it exposes the field’s deepest institutional failure.**

Across bona fide psychotherapies delivered by competent practitioners, outcomes converge. The common-factors movement, of which Henriques is now incoming president, argues this is because what drives therapeutic change is not school-specific techniques but shared underlying relational and process variables. The implication is devastating for the evidence-based therapy industry: much of the empirical apparatus built to validate specific brands is, as Henriques argues from personal experience inside Beck’s lab, organised around brand promotion rather than truth-seeking. A study redesigned mid-trial to rescue failing results, published without disclosure of the redesign, in JAMA, under the frame of cognitive therapy efficacy, this is not an isolated failure but a structural feature of how the field operates.
