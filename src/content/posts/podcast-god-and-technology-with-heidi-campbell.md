Heidi Campbell is one of the founders of digital religion studies and has spent 30 years researching how religious communities interact with technology. In this conversation, she walks through her Religious Social Shaping of Technology framework, showing how groups from ultra-Orthodox Jews to American evangelicals don't simply accept or reject new technologies but actively negotiate them based on their core values. She explains how the pandemic forced churches worldwide to confront questions about embodiment, communion, and worship that turned out to be more sociological than theological. The conversation then moves into AI: Campbell argues that GPTs give knowledge but not wisdom, that religious chatbots inherit the biases of their construction, and that the field of digital religion suffers from a serious AI literacy gap. She closes with a career-defining recalibration: the internet has gone from a supplement to religion to, for many, its first point of contact. &nbsp;

## Who’s Heidi Campbell?

Heidi A. Campbell is Professor of Communication and Presidential Impact Fellow at Texas A&M University. She holds a PhD in Practical Theology from the University of Edinburgh and is one of the founders of digital religion studies, having researched the intersection of digital media, religion, and culture since the mid-1990s. Her books include _When Religion Meets New Media_, _Digital Religion_, and _God and Technology_.

## TOP 3 Thesis

1. Religious communities do not simply accept or reject technology; they actively negotiate it through their existing values, authority structures, and communal identity, and this process is predictable once you understand those underlying commitments.

2. AI produces knowledge but not wisdom, and the conflation of the two is one of the most consequential category errors shaping how people engage with religious AI tools.

3. The pandemic revealed that most resistance to digital worship was sociological rather than theological: communities were protecting the worship event as their primary marker of identity, not defending a worked-out position on embodiment.

## Memorable Quotes

> _"GPTs don't give us wisdom. Information is just data. Knowledge is basically the organization and sorting through that data to give us an answer to a question. But wisdom is the interpretation and application. And it's not coming from any lived experience."_

> _"Most core technology values are about efficiency, they're about progress, they're about speed. And so those are a very different set of values than human values, which are love and care and reciprocity."_

## Key Insights

**1. Religious communities negotiate technology, they don't just accept or reject it.** Campbell's Religious Social Shaping of Technology framework identifies four stages: understanding a community's history with media, identifying its core values, observing the platform those values create for response, and analyzing how the community talks about technology. The process is predictable once you know the inputs.

**2. Ultra-Orthodox Jews and evangelical Christians use technology the same way for the same reasons.** Despite vast theological differences, Lubavitch Jews and American evangelicals adopted digital tools with strikingly similar logic because both communities share an outreach-oriented mission. The research shows that mission and values, not denomination, determine tech behavior.

**3. The pandemic's real barrier to digital worship was sociological, not theological.** Campbell found that most communities framed their resistance in theological terms (embodiment, communion, the body in worship), but the deeper issue was that the worship event had become the primary marker of community identity, and changing it felt like an existential threat.

**4. AI produces knowledge but not wisdom, and confusing the two is the defining error.** GPTs organize data into answers (knowledge) but lack the lived experience and interpretive capacity that constitute wisdom. Campbell argues this distinction should be the starting point for any religious community evaluating AI tools.

**5. The field of digital religion suffers from an AI literacy gap.** Theologians and religious scholars routinely treat AI as one monolithic phenomenon. Campbell identifies the failure to distinguish between predictive, generative, and agentic AI as the single biggest blind spot in current academic and pastoral conversations.
