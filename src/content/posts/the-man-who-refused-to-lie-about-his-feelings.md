**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Stranger_’ by_ Albert Camus&nbsp;**

**Includes spoilers**

Having read _The Myth of Sisyphus_ before, I was curious to see how existentialism and the philosophy of the Absurd would manifest in a narrative format. While I wouldn’t necessarily call _The Stranger_ a masterpiece, I certainly enjoyed reading it, and it explores themes that are deeply compelling. The story is divided roughly into two parts, beginning with the main character, Meursault, attending the funeral of his mother. Later, in a sequence dominated by oppressive heat and sunlight, he ends up killing a man in a hazy moment of self-defense. The second part of the novel details his subsequent trial and imprisonment, where the focus shifts strangely from the crime itself to his character. The story is quite bizarre because the protagonist isn’t a character that creates warmth or invites sympathy. He lacks any kind of grief towards the death of his mother, and even in the end, he doesn’t have any remorse for killing a human being. He doesn’t fit into the hero archetype, yet he is not explicitly evil either. He seems to live in a state of nihilism where nothing really matters.

His psychological state is quite peculiar, however, and distinct from standard definitions of meaninglessness. For example, Nietzsche describes two types of nihilism, active and passive. The active type is the traditional existential mode of creating one's own values, while the passive type is a depressive outlook resulting from the realization of a meaningless world. Meursault is neither. His state is somewhere in between, defined by a complete and honest indifference. Although Camus is often branded an existentialist, his philosophy here is more accurately in line with the Absurd, which arises from the disconnect between the subject and the world. We are rational and meaning-making creatures living in an irrational and meaningless universe. A significant portion of the plot involves Meursault being confronted with expectations from the world—such as the expectation to cry at a funeral or lie to save himself—but never fitting into them. There is a constant mismatch between what he is and what the world wants him to be.

Throughout the novel, Meursault is constantly dragged into different situations, but he never seems to play an active role in what is going on. He simply seems to drift through the story, ending up in situations solely due to his lack of conviction. I initially thought this was an example of lacking authenticity or a negative example of the active pursuit of meaning and choice. However, I do not believe that is the case, as that would be a more traditional existential take. Instead, the novel tries to showcase the Absurd and forces us to face it because we inherently project our own values onto the character, wishing he would act differently. As the story illustrates, there is no "right" way to live because there is no objective grounding for any meaning. The Absurd must be embraced, just as Meursault does at the end while facing his death. He reflects: “For the first time, in that night alive with signs and stars, I opened myself to the gentle indifference of the world.” With this realization, he finds peace and concludes that he is happy. His happiness comes directly from his acceptance of the Absurd, much like Sisyphus pushing the boulder up the mountain.&nbsp;
