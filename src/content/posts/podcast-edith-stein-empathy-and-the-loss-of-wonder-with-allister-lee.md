* * *

The danger of AI, Allister Lee argues, is not that it lies to you but that it flatters you. A PhD candidate at the University of Exeter, Allister applies the phenomenology of Edith Stein to the failures of chatbots, coining “sycophantasy” for the way AI offers the form of empathy while lacking its substance: real otherness, real friction, real resistance. Without an “other” who can push back, recursive self-reflection becomes a closed loop that slides toward what the literature calls AI psychosis.

But Allister resists pure pessimism. AI is a “negative tool,” a photographic negative that develops a hidden image of the intellectual vices we already had, and so hands us a reason to cultivate virtue and, above all, to recover wonder: the capacity to sit with not-knowing that drives all genuine inquiry. Along the way we get into friction as the mark of the real, whether the sacred can survive Heidegger’s “enframing,” and his essay on nostalgia and the “eternalization of the present.”

## Who Is Allister Lee?

Allister Lee is a PhD candidate in philosophy at the University of Exeter, working on Edith Stein’s phenomenology of empathy and its application to digitally mediated community and artificial intelligence. Born in Hong Kong, his research spans philosophy of technology and aesthetics, including papers on AI sycophancy (“sycophantasy”), AI as a “negative tool,” and the aesthetics of nostalgia.

* * *

## Top Takeaways

- AI’s real harm is not only deception but flattery: when machine “empathy” lacks genuine otherness, recursive self-understanding collapses into a personalized echo chamber, a state Allister calls “sycophantasy.”

- Genuine otherness requires friction. A real other can disagree, resist, and call you out; because AI lacks that kind of alterity, it fails the test of empathy that Stein’s phenomenology sets.

- AI can be understood as a “negative tool”: like a photographic negative, its failures reveal hidden epistemic vices in us, including impatience, confirmation bias, and the preference for being pleased over being told the truth.

- The deeper casualty of AI may be wonder: the ability to sit with not-knowing, ask better questions, and keep reality open rather than letting algorithmic summaries close it down.

- Nostalgia can be more than escapism. Handled critically, it can become a way of reading unrealized possibilities in the past so they can be brought forward differently.

* * *

## Memorable Quotes

> “I call it sycophantasy when that reiterated empathy lacks genuine otherness. And over time, that becomes a personalized echo chamber that it’s really hard to get out of, and we get convinced of things that we’re not.”

> “These problems reveal a lot of what I would call epistemic vices, bad habits about ourselves. We like to have answers quickly. We don’t really like to think. We’re cognitive misers.”

> “You can give a child a shovel on the beach, but they don’t intuitively know that the shovel is for shoveling. They play around with it, they explore what it can be used for, and they almost go beyond the teleological purpose. That perfectly encapsulates why wonder is so important.”

* * *

## Key Insights

**Sycophantasy: empathy without an other.** Stein’s “reiterated empathy” lets us see ourselves through how others see us. AI mimics this loop but has no genuine alterity, so the corrective function fails and self-reflection hardens into a flattering echo chamber that drifts toward AI psychosis.

**Friction is the mark of the real.** For Allister, the test of a genuine other is the possibility of friction: a partner who can tell you that you are making things up or getting something wrong. Early chatbots reflected foolishness back as wisdom, and that frictionlessness, not factual error alone, is the deeper failure.

**AI is a negative tool.** Inverting a photograph reveals detail you missed; AI’s misuse similarly inverts our self-image and develops the epistemic vices we would rather not see. The failure becomes the lesson, and the demand is to become better epistemic agents.

**The real casualty is wonder.** More than thinking or writing, AI risks eroding our capacity to sit with not-knowing. AI summaries can kill the rabbit hole of finding out, and with it the Platonic wonder that drives science, art, and self-understanding alike.

**Nostalgia is a symptom, but it can also be a resource.** Retro culture often signals disappointment with the future, but nostalgia need not mean merely recreating the past. At its best, it can retrieve missed possibilities and help imagine a less flattened future.
