**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_Confrontation with the Unconscious: Jungian Depth Psychology and Psychedelic Ex_’ by_ Scott J. Hill**

Ever since reading Jung, I couldn’t help but draw parallels with the psychedelic experience. In fact, nothing seems more suitable as a framework, but somehow I never seemed to encounter much material about psychedelics and Jung. When I originally saw _Confrontation with the Unconscious_ many years ago, it was the only one of its kind, and many years later, it’s still the only one I can find. The book begins with a brief history of psychedelic research and then lays some foundation of Jung’s concepts and principles before finally diving into more direct comparisons. I liked how the author made use of the psychotomimetic model of psychedelics, which is usually told in the context of how psychedelics were first used but quickly dismissed. While the psychedelic experience is most certainly not reduceable to a psychotic episode, some similarities in phenomenology cannot be dismissed, and the author explores this in-depth in a way that I found enlightening.

I said that Jung and psychedelics seem to be a perfect match, and in many ways they are. However, they are also hard to reconcile because Jung himself discouraged psychedelic use, stating that it manifested too much of the unconscious and most people would not be able to integrate such content successfully. On one hand, Jung only lived in the very early stages of psychedelics and didn’t live to see the incredible research that was done in the 50s and 60s, and again more recently in the last couple of decades. Given how therapeutic it is for many people who are otherwise considered hopeless, it seems that Jung overstated the difficulty in integrating the experience. Nevertheless, his caution is undoubtedly wise for anyone that has used them, and given that integration is such a big part of the experience, there is more than a grain of truth in his statement. This conflict is explored throughout the book, and I found that it balances both perspectives well and remains open-minded.

The main connection that the author makes between Jungian psychology and psychedelics is the manifestation of the collective unconscious and, more importantly, the integration of the shadow, particularly in cases of trauma. Many bad experiences are the manifestation of one’s shadow and the difficulty in accepting that aspect of the personality. Furthermore, that manifestation tends to be externalized, and the author draws parallels between this and the demonic experiences that some people go through. Just like in Jungian psychology, where the shadow needs to be accepted and integrated for a successful personality transformation, it is also widely agreed in the psychedelic experience that the user needs to accept the experience, even when negative, in order to be able to transcend it.

The book goes into a lot of depth regarding specific aspects of psychotherapy, which I wasn’t really expecting. It discusses different schools of thought on how to approach psychedelic therapy, referencing therapists with vast experience in the field before they were banned. Many specific cases of psychedelic therapy were included, which I enjoyed, so the Jungian framework can be seen in actual therapy rather than being an abstract and theoretical approach. The author also discusses his own experiences and journey, which I found helpful and interesting to read. This could have easily overwhelmed the book and made it too personal, but he avoids this pitfall. His experience is just one among many used to illustrate the connection to Jung’s psychology, making the book feel very well-rounded and not too dogmatic about which aspects of Jung are explored or which interpretation is the correct one.

The only thing I disliked about the book was its heavy emphasis on psychotherapy. To be fair, this is completely understandable given that Jung was a psychotherapist, but I view Jung as much a philosopher as a psychologist (if anything, even more so). Seeing Jung’s thought reduced to therapy was a bit disappointing to me. Furthermore, a lot of the book was focused on negative experiences and their therapeutic effect. This is of course incredibly important, but it always felt like it was missing the other piece. It rarely explored the more mystical aspects of psychedelics and how they relate to Jung’s thought, which was my main interest. Nevertheless, I still found it worth reading, and the connection between Jungian psychotherapy and the psychedelic experience was explored from several different angles. Even if you’re just interested in Jung without having experience or interest in psychedelics, I think this will still provide useful insights into successful therapy and personality transformation. However, if you’re interested in psychedelics but aren’t familiar with Jung, this might be too much of a jump. While an introduction is provided, I’m not sure if that’s enough, and one might get a little lost. Being familiar with Jung’s thought and depth psychology in general is recommended to get the best out of the book.

The more you know of it, the greater and heavier becomes our moral burden, because the unconscious contents transform themselves into your individual tasks and duties as soon as they begin to become conscious. Do you want to increase loneliness and misunderstanding? Do you want to find more and more complications and increasing re­sponsibilities? You get enough of it.”
