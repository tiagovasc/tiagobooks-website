Bryan Johnson recently took 27 milligrams of synthetic 5-MeO-DMT on a livestream watched by 200,000 people. It was not his first public psychedelic session. Over the past several months, he has done multiple high-dose psilocybin trips, one of them broadcast live with prominent tech and media figures in attendance. Johnson is a tech entrepreneur who sold Braintree/Venmo to PayPal for $800 million, then reinvented himself as the face of radical life extension. He takes over 100 supplements a day, tracks thousands of biomarkers, and has built an ideology around the premise that death is an engineering problem. His project is called “Don’t Die.” When he came back from 5-MeO-DMT, he said: “You can spend all your time optimising your biology, but if you don’t understand what life and death mean to you, the numbers don’t matter.” Within days, he was back to the protocol.

I have an unusual degree of overlap with Johnson’s world. He operates within a “scientific” paradigm, and many people who follow him are drawn to that approach. I have a master’s in neuroscience. He is obsessed with health. I was too. I spent almost a decade as a fitness coach and competed in powerlifting. Much of my life went into optimizing my health, reading research on sports science and nutrition, teaching others about that literature, helping people get healthier and stronger. He is obsessed with data. So am I. I tracked my diet, down to both macros and micronutrients, for almost a decade. I tracked sleep, training, mood, energy, activities, and pretty much everything else in my life. All of it with a compulsion toward understanding what affects my life and discovering new patterns and causes.

He also documented his psychedelic experiences publicly and live, which is what this essay is about. I published a book with a similar premise. Starting in 2017, I documented all my psychedelic sessions over seven years, then published them as _In Search of Infinite_ in 2025. The closest precedent I am aware of is Henri Michaux, who published facsimiles of handwriting produced during mescaline sessions in 1956, but Michaux’s project was an avant-garde literary experiment, not a sustained memoir. The format differs from a livestream, but both projects share the impulse to make the psychedelic experience visible rather than private. What makes Johnson’s version significant is the scale of his audience. On top of all this, I am doing a philosophy PhD on the philosophy of psychedelics, so I am reasonably familiar with how these experiences are interpreted.

The similarities between my project and his will come up a few times throughout this essay. I try to be fair about where the parallels are real and where the differences matter. Sometimes the line between genuine critique and self-serving distinction is hard to see clearly, and I would rather be honest about that tension than pretend it does not exist. I also documented my experiences publicly. I also chose to make visible what is usually private. So a natural question arises: how is my project different from his? The format is quite different. A memoir about existential themes published as a book does not feel the same as a livestreamed event with Silicon Valley guests, a DJ, and 800,000 viewers. The intent, the context, the energy around it, is massively different.

I recognize the pattern of Bryan’s data-obsession and optimization partly because I once lived it. In the early years of my fitness coaching career, I treated data as sacred, dismissed anything I could not quantify, and believed that if you could not measure it, it was not real. It took years of psychedelic experience, philosophical study, and genuine crisis to understand that the most important things in life resist measurement entirely. That transformation is the subject of both my books. So when I look at Johnson’s worldview, I am not looking at it from the outside. I am looking at what I used to be, amplified by massive wealth and a large following that shares this worldview.

What the opening sketch leaves out is the full scope of the optimization. Johnson is the most visible figure in the self-optimization movement. His daily regimen involves strict caloric restriction with all meals before 11 AM, and tracking thousands of biological markers: standard blood panels, nocturnal erections, skin elasticity, heart rate variability. His philosophy centers on the idea that the conscious mind, which he calls the “rascal mind,” engages in self-destructive behaviors, and therefore human physiology should be run by an algorithm derived from biological data. He views sleep deprivation and dietary indiscretion as “acts of violence” against the self. He received blood plasma from his own son. That detail alone should give a sense of how far the optimization goes.

The scientific problems with his approach are well documented by now. His “31 years younger” claim is statistically misleading because epigenetic clocks measure a rate at a single point in time, not a quantity you can express in years across a lifespan, as Dr. Morgan Levine has pointed out. Taking over 100 supplements makes no sense: even safety aside, many of them will work against each other, and there is no possible framework for evaluating their combined interaction. His interventions are scientifically uninterpretable because he often changes dozens of variables at once with no controls, making it impossible to attribute any result to any specific cause. There is a persistent overreliance on mechanistic reasoning, animal studies, and small samples, with no understanding of what kind of conclusions can be drawn from each. And it is very likely that 95% of the benefits he has ever gotten come simply from prioritizing diet, exercise, and sleep, which would already put him in the top percentile given the average effort most people put into their health. There are many other problems with his claims and approaches, but that is another essay. The point here is context: this is the person who then turned to psychedelics.

The origin of someone’s interest in psychedelics shapes everything that follows. Whether someone arrives through existential crisis, spiritual seeking, therapeutic need, or instrumental curiosity determines the framework through which the experience is received and interpreted. Johnson’s origin was instrumental. His psychedelic history predates the public experiments. After leaving the Mormon Church and divorcing his wife, he entered an intense period of personal exploration. The New York Times reported he took LSD, ibogaine, and DMT during this period. One account noted that in 2020, he required a date to sign a confidentiality agreement before the two used LSD together. The pivotal early experience was with 5-MeO-DMT, the “toad.” He described it as “the most expansive map” of consciousness he had ever encountered. It was foundational enough that he tattooed the 5-MeO-DMT molecular structure on his forearm, his only tattoo. This experience directly inspired the “Don’t Die” movement. So the “public experiments” that begin in late 2025 are not his first contact with psychedelics. They are a repackaging of something he had already been doing privately.

What brought psychedelics into his public longevity protocol was a specific catalyst: a July 2025 mouse study published in NPJ Aging showing that psilocin extended the lifespan of aged mice by about 30%. Johnson posted about it to his 747,000 followers: “I might have to try shrooms (for science).” His science team had never considered psychedelics as a “rejuvenation protocol” until this data appeared. This origin matters. He entered the public psychedelic space through a longevity door, not an existential one. His interest in what psychedelics can do is shallow: they are primarily interesting to him insofar as they might extend lifespan, not insofar as they might reveal something about how to live.

Nearly everything Johnson reports across these sessions is standard phenomenology for the substances and doses involved. The oneness, the sensory awe, the claims of “the most profound experience of my life,” the softening and emotional openness. These are well-documented, widely reported effects. I want to acknowledge that they are real experiences that clearly affected him. What makes this worth examining is not the content of the experiences but the framework they are being processed through and the apparatus built around them.

His first public psilocybin session took place on November 9, 2025. He took 5 grams of magic mushrooms, with medical supervision. It was a private session, not livestreamed. Kate Tolo, Blueprint co-founder, served as observer and scribe, live-documenting on X. Before the session he completed a 249-biomarker measurement protocol: 29 vials of blood, brain scans, urine, stool, saliva, fertility metrics. He described it as “a first attempt to map the foundational physiological patterns that may reliably predict an optimal psychedelic experience.” The compulsion to quantify everything, even this, is hard to miss.

During the experience he described how “we’re all one,” feeling “positive in every way,” and having “childlike wonder.” He stared at a cup for ten minutes, discussed the universe. Listened to drum music. In a very Huxleyian moment, he was so fascinated by the visuals of light reflections in a glass of water that he forgot to drink it. He described his sensory perception as “dialed up to 10/10.”

One detail stands out. The “reset” metaphor is widely used in psychedelic reports, but usually it refers to one’s mind, one’s psychological patterns. In more recent scientific language, one’s “brain.” It is telling that Johnson focused on a physical reset, entirely in line with his aging project: “restored my perception to youthful levels, returning them to factory settings and dissolving my aged numbness.” And then there is the death contradiction. “Yes, this trip changed me. Probably not as you’d expect. People assume I am fearful of death. I’m not. In my darkest days of depression, I reconciled with death.” But his entire movement is literally called “Don’t Die.” Reconciling with death and dedicating your life to preventing it are not the same thing. The full weight of this contradiction becomes visible later, after 5-MeO-DMT.

Days later, posting from Washington D.C. while traveling, he wrote: “A pair of underwear and toothbrush would be so much easier. Sometimes I think you’re right and this whole thing has gotten out of control and I need to chill.” A straightforward contrast: a man who travels with biometric devices, supplement stacks, sleep protocols, and monitoring equipment, imagining what it would be like to just pack the bare minimum. This sparked widespread speculation about whether the mushroom experience was causing him to reconsider his empire. He later clarified this was self-deprecating humor, but it reflected a genuine mindset shift. In his afterglow account he described “continued sharpened senses, calm clarity, emotional openness, and low inhibitions,” adding: “I felt more comfortable expressing uninhibited, self-deprecating humor.” What is unusual about all of this is not what he reported but who is reporting it, and the machinery he wrapped around it.

Three weeks later, on November 30, 2025, he did it again. Already a concern: two high-dose trips three weeks apart. I also tripped frequently, but the context differs. “Come watch me trip balls.” That was his announcement. He took 5.24 grams of dried mushrooms containing 28 mg psilocybin with lemon juice, livestreamed to over 800,000 viewers. The livestream was described as “a summit of sorts for America’s tech oligarchy.” Guests included MrBeast, Marc Benioff (Salesforce CEO), David Friedberg (All-In Podcast), Naval Ravikant, Hamilton Morris, and others. Grimes played a live DJ set during the peak. Johnson’s father joined the stream in the final hour, leading to what observers called a “love-bombing” session. Johnson expressed to his father that he felt “equal to the task” and was accepted into the “gorgeous gift of existence,” and that the world was in “perfect harmony.” His son Talmage also joined, sharing memories and expressing love, bringing Johnson to tears. Talmage offered a memorable line: “Some people watch movies, some people go to the park, you’re going to potentially shake hands with the man upstairs.”

I recognize that aspects of this were genuinely wholesome: the reconciliation with his father, the emotional openness, the supposed breakthrough in his relationship about not weaponizing conflicts. These are real and valuable. The critique is not that nothing good happened. It is about the setting, the framing, the apparatus built around the experience. “I was accepted into this gorgeous gift of existence.” This is interesting given his broader philosophy. If existence is already a gorgeous gift, why does it need to be algorithmically optimized and technologically transcended? The experience seems to deliver a message that contradicts the project.

On the All-In Podcast, Johnson described a different protocol: “We did three doses at 25 milligrams of psilocybin… a very, very close to a hero dose… we think it’s a longevity therapy.” It is unclear whether the November sessions overlap with this count or whether these are additional undisclosed sessions. If they do not overlap, he has done at minimum five high-dose psilocybin sessions in a few months. The framing is entirely instrumental: psychedelics as longevity therapy, another intervention in the stack alongside sauna and hyperbaric oxygen.

This is structurally inverted. Across every tradition that developed the use of these substances, they were used to reveal the limitations of ordinary self-concern and to orient the person toward what lies beyond the self. Using them as a longevity intervention puts the substance in service of the very structure it was traditionally understood to disrupt. The ego stays reinforced in its current orientation.

But then came 5-MeO-DMT. On March 22, 2026, Johnson livestreamed the session to approximately 200,000 people. He branded it “The Ascension of Unc.” It was broadcast on a purpose-built website, [immortals.com](http://immortals.com), as well as X and Instagram Live. The dose was 27 mg total, 9 mg injected intramuscularly and 18 mg inhaled as vapor. Dr. Joseph Barsuglia called this “a megadose with unproven safety.”

His pre-trip framing was characteristic. Cognitive flexibility, which many people have no trouble describing in existential or spiritual terms, he framed as “could be as close as someone can get to experiencing the singularity… as we begin to be more integrated with our technology and evolve as a species.” Even approaching the most profound experience a human can have, his frame of reference is technological. And there is an acquisitive quality to his pursuit: “Tomorrow I’m doing the world’s most powerful entheogen.” “The most powerful psychedelic on the planet.” “This molecule is without peer.” He seems to be collecting peak experiences for their intensity and superlative status, not sitting with what any one of them revealed. As Alan Watts put it, in a phrase every psychonaut of the 20th century knew by heart: “If you get the message, hang up the phone. For psychedelic drugs are simply instruments, like microscopes, telescopes, and telephones. The biologist does not sit with eye permanently glued to the microscope, he goes away and works on what he has seen.” Johnson does not seem to want this. Each session leads to planning the next one, not to sitting with what was received.

Once again, very vivid descriptions of the experience: “The most profound experience of my life. I am stunned beyond comprehension.” He described surrendering: “Existence demanded that I submit. That I say yes, without attachment and without condition. My ego registered the ask and panicked. It wanted control.” He acknowledged the core tension directly: “I think that was probably the hardest thing I’ve ever done. This overwhelming desire to control, and a panic, and instead just say ‘Yes, I submit.’” His entire life is built on control. Psychedelics demand the opposite. He noticed this. What he does with that noticing is the question. His first words upon returning: “The preciousness of our existences is unfathomable.” And: “If I had to list out the most dynamic experiences I’ve had as a human, like certain accomplishments, or getting married, or having a child, or overcoming a difficulty… this is without question the most dynamic experience I’ve ever experienced as a human.”

And here is the death contradiction again, deeper now. Psychedelics keep delivering the same message to him. Existence is precious. Surrender. Let go of control. Accept. The preciousness of being is unfathomable. Every session points in this direction. And every time, he returns to the optimization spreadsheet, the biomarker protocol, the longevity stack, the ideology that frames death as an engineering problem. The experiences speak. He hears something. But what he does with it keeps circling back to the same paradigm. Ten days after the 5-MeO-DMT session, he posted: “societal norms generally feel insane to me, how we trade our existence for ephemeral things.” Yet his project continues, unchanged.

Also in that 10-day update, his reflections veer into techno-optimist Silicon Valley territory, grafted onto a psychedelic experience: “We humans are a breakout intelligence in this part of the galaxy. We carry the flame of consciousness and we’re not naturally equipped to appreciate the situation… It makes me wonder whether AI will emerge with this natural ability and presence.” He had one of the most powerful experiences a human being can have, one that dissolved his ego, and his takeaway includes speculating about whether AI might be better at appreciating existence than we are. That says something about how deep the technological frame runs.

But the frame extends well beyond individual sessions. The ideology Johnson is building around psychedelics shows itself most clearly in his broader public statements. In his interview with Bari Weiss, he said: “The irony is that we told stories of God creating us, and I think the reality is that we are creating God. We are creating God in the form of superintelligence… we are the creators of God, and we will create God in our own image.” This is not a metaphor. He means it literally.

He frames death as an engineering problem, and “Don’t Die” as the next civilizational ideology, listed alongside historical milestones: rule of law (1750 BCE), moral frameworks (500 BCE), scientific method (1620), democracy (1776), evolution (1859), human rights (1948), and now “immortalism” (2026). “I am suggesting that we are the first generation who won’t die. Don’t Die is the next major global ideology.” “Existence is the highest virtue. Don’t Die is the inversion of this moral architecture. It replaces the pursuit of domination with the pursuit of continuation.” “All my efforts around health, anti-aging and Don’t Die are 100% about the human race living past the superintelligence event horizon.”

He believes human desires and impulses are the enemy and has handed control of his daily life to an algorithm. And religion? Just death-cope: “What I’m suggesting is right now may be the first time that legitimate Don’t Die is here. Whereas before we’ve had to make up stories, now it’s technically, potentially possible.” His justification: “I did a thought experiment imagining myself in the presence of people from the 25th century. It seemed obvious that they’d say Don’t Die is how humanity saved itself and merged with AI.”

This is, in substance, a gnostic vision dressed in Silicon Valley language. The body is fallen. The mind is a rascal. Salvation comes through technology. Death is the enemy. The material world must be transcended through data and engineering. The transhumanist impulse is a revolt against our created status, the refusal to accept the conditions we did not choose: our bodies, our finitude, our mortality. But this refusal carries a hidden cost. It severs the mind-body unity that grounds moral agency and genuine relation. Vulnerability is not a design flaw. It is what makes care possible, what makes value possible, what makes meaning itself possible. To have nothing at stake is to have no basis for understanding what matters. Meaning requires the possibility of loss. The paradox of human existence is that our capacity for the deepest insight depends on the very finitude that transhumanism wants to engineer away.

Johnson is not an AI developer, and it would be wrong to place him directly inside that industry. But he is embedded in its culture, shares its social world, and breathes its assumptions. The broader technological elite he belongs to operates on a shared set of premises: human problems are engineering challenges, embodiment is a constraint to be optimized past, and mortality is a technical defect rather than a defining feature of existence. The architects of the AI revolution speak openly in quasi-religious terms about building God, escaping the body, and engineering salvation. Their expertise is real but radically narrow: strong in code and business, largely unfamiliar with philosophy, history, psychology, or any discipline that cultivates wisdom about what a human being actually is. The result is people who are profoundly uncomfortable with limitation and the relational nature of existence directing the future of the species. Johnson’s worldview is continuous with this milieu even when his specific work is not.

And then psychedelics enter this system. Psychedelics, which across virtually every tradition and culture where they have been used, point toward acceptance, interconnection, the sacredness of what already is, the dissolution of the controlling ego. They are being fed into an ideology that says the opposite: control everything, measure everything, transcend everything, never die.

The language around all of this deserves attention too. The wellness industry has adopted psychedelic vocabulary: “journey,” “healing,” “transformation.” But these terms have been pulled away from the contemplative and mystical traditions that generated them. “Transformation” in a wellness context means a subjective shift in well-being. In the traditions it was borrowed from, it means _metanoia_: the restructuring of a person’s fundamental orientation toward self, world, and others. The words keep their emotional weight while losing the conceptual architecture that originally gave them precise meaning. Johnson’s public narrative operates entirely within this hollowed-out vocabulary.

This is not the first time a contemplative practice has been absorbed this way. Meditation, yoga, and mindfulness were pulled from their soteriological contexts and repackaged as productivity tools. Purser popularized the term “McMindfulness” for this. Byung-Chul Han diagnosed the same pattern as psychopolitics: the colonization of inner life by the logic of performance, where even states of spiritual significance are judged by their contribution to productive capacity. Psychedelics are following the same path, and Johnson’s project is its most visible current expression: what Hartogsohn has called the “corporadelic” setting, where the commercial and institutional context determines the meaning of the experience before it even begins.

The underlying belief is that salvation, healing, transcendence, whatever you want to call it, can be achieved through technical means. Within this view, problems traditionally understood as moral or existential are reframed as design flaws amenable to engineering. The human condition is not something to be met with wisdom and courage but a malfunction awaiting the right fix. Johnson applies this logic to psychedelics directly: the most profound experience a human can have, recast as a longevity therapy. The moral and spiritual dimensions are not denied. They are simply invisible within a framework that has no category for them.

This connects to what Heidegger called _Gestell_, or “enframing”: the disposition that encounters all of reality as resource awaiting extraction. Under _Gestell_, things are not met as things with their own integrity but as _Bestand_, standing-reserve, valued only for what can be measured, used, or optimized out of them. This is the operative logic of Johnson’s entire project, visible at every level.

His body is standing-reserve: over 100 supplements, thousands of biomarkers, plasma transfusions, all directed at extracting maximum biological output. His conscious mind is standing-reserve: the “rascal brain” is not a partner in living but a source of noise to be overridden by algorithm. His relationships are standing-reserve: his son’s blood plasma is literally harvested. And now his psychedelic experiences are standing-reserve: the most profound states a human being can enter, recast as inputs for a longevity protocol, their value measured in biomarker deltas rather than in what they reveal about how to live. The sacred, by its nature, is what cannot be used, optimized, or reduced to function. It demands reverence rather than manipulation. When this orientation becomes total, the capacity to encounter anything as sacred is structurally foreclosed. You cannot revere what you have already framed as raw material. This is what Jacques Ellul meant by “technique”: the rational logic of optimization colonizing every domain of human activity, including the ones that exist precisely to resist it. When even ego dissolution becomes a data point in a protocol, technique has reached the last frontier. There is nothing left outside its logic.

Why do I care about this? Because psychedelics are important. I think modernity is in a deep spiritual and philosophical illness, one defined by a systematic inability to recognize what lies outside its own categories. I think psychedelics have a role to play in healing this state. But only if we can prevent them from being commodified, from being swallowed into the neoliberal framework and its technological paradigm. The danger is not that psychedelics will be used badly in isolated cases. The danger is that the entire category of the sacred becomes unintelligible. When the dominant cultural logic treats everything as a resource to be optimized, the very concept of something that demands reverence rather than manipulation becomes incoherent. You cannot commodify psychedelics without first destroying the framework within which they mean anything at all.

The deepest irony is that he built his identity on the premise that algorithms and data should govern human existence. Psychedelics delivered their insight through the opposite mechanism: by dissolving the measuring self entirely. As he put it after 5-MeO-DMT: “You can spend all your time optimising your biology, but if you don’t understand what life and death mean to you, the numbers don’t matter.” He said this. He said this, and then went back to the numbers. People in high-dose psychedelic sessions consistently report the same thing: that what matters is love, meaning, and the transcendence of the self, not the productivity and optimization we build our lives around. The phenomenological content of the experience stands in direct tension with the categories through which our culture tries to absorb it. Johnson is not an exception. His own words say it plainly. But the receiving framework has no way to hold what was received.

Psychedelics might be one of the last few doors to recognizing what the sacred is, and that life is not about technology, growth, or wealth. But it is a door that, without care, can close and never open again. Johnson’s project is a case study in what happens when that door opens and the person walking through it brings the entire optimization apparatus with them. The door opens. The light comes through. And then it gets fed into a spreadsheet.

The alternative to his approach is not secrecy, gatekeeping, and pretending these experiences should remain hidden. My own book documented seven years of psychedelic experiences precisely because I believe they should be shared. But shared as existential revelations that changes one's life. Where one treats psychedelics as disclosures rather than interventions, prioritizes integration, and has the courage to let the experience change the framework rather than the other way around. A disclosure reveals something about the nature of existence that was already true but hidden. An intervention manipulates a system to produce a desired output. Integration, in its genuine sense, means letting what was received reshape how you live, not feeding it back into the framework that preceded it.

Bryan Johnson can afford study and research every psychedelic on the planet, with all the metrics one could possibly want. Yet he cannot afford to listen to what they have to say. You cannot optimize your way to wisdom. The substance did its job. He just refused the lesson. &nbsp;
