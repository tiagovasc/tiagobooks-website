**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_Existentialism Is a Humanism_’ by_ Jean-Paul Sartre**

I always labored under the impression that this was the definitive key text for existentialism, but that turns out not to be the case. It is merely a transcript of a lecture Sartre gave in 1945, and strictly speaking, the text itself is quite short. The beginning of the book, following the preface and introduction, is arguably the strongest section because it immediately touches on the central pillars of the philosophy, most famously Sartre’s declaration that "existence precedes essence." This means human beings cannot be defined _a priori_ since we have no intrinsic nature or blueprint. Consequently, there is no objective grounding for any values besides the ones we actively choose to follow. As Sartre notes, "man first of all exists, encounters himself, surges up in the world, and defines himself afterwards." While this focus on radical freedom and responsibility is compelling, there is surprisingly little writing from Sartre himself in this volume. Out of nearly one hundred pages, only forty represent his actual thoughts during the talk, with the remainder filled by introductions, a Q&A session, and commentary.

One element I found rather disappointing is how heavily the text is embedded in the specific historical and social context of 1945. I had hoped this volume would offer a deep dive into the mechanics of existentialism, but instead, the talk is primarily concerned with refuting criticisms leveled against the movement by the public and intellectuals of that era. It feels just as political and social as it does philosophical. While I am sure this defense was of immense importance during the immediate post-war period, much of the urgency loses its significance in our current time. This issue is exacerbated by the inclusion of an incredibly long rant by Pierre Naville during the Q&A section which spans eleven pages. The preface and introduction attempt to provide background on what precipitated the lecture and caution against viewing Sartre solely through this work, yet the inclusion of the Naville portion felt overly political and frankly not very enjoyable to read.

The book concludes with a twenty-five-page commentary on Albert Camus’ _The Stranger_, which was actually quite great. It explores the theme of the absurd in the novel and connects it to his later philosophical essay, _The Myth of Sisyphus_. However, a large portion of this essay focuses on literary style and utilizes several comparisons to other works that unfortunately went over my head. I was lucky enough to have read both of Camus' major works mentioned, but for those who haven't, this portion will be rendered almost useless. Furthermore, I am not convinced it fits well with the preceding lecture. While it provides insight into Camus’ thought, his absurdist philosophy differs a fair bit from Sartre’s existentialism despite their shared historical space. Ultimately, I wouldn't say this is a bad book, but if you were as optimistic as I was, you should beware that it might differ from your expectations. Since a very small portion of the book is the actual title essay, and even that deals largely with outdated social concerns, I would personally recommend finding the text as a stand-alone piece. If you want something deeper and more focused on the philosophy itself, a general introduction to Sartre would likely be a better fit.
