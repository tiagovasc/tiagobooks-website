**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_Philosopher of the Heart: The Restless Life of Søren Kierkegaard_’ by_ Clare Carlisle**

I have read _The Sickness Unto Death_ by Kierkegaard, and while I really enjoyed it and took a lot from the text, it was incredibly hard to read. It was a huge effort, and it remained completely incomprehensible to me in many passages. _The Sickness Unto Death_ was mostly about the individual, but I wanted to dive deeper into his concept of Christian faith, which is explored in his book _Fear and Trembling_. However, I was somewhat afraid of diving into it considering how difficult the previous text had been, so I decided that I would read a biography of him first. I had seen Clare Carlisle’s book mentioned before, but I initially thought it was mostly a summary and introduction. I later discovered it was a biography, which was exactly what I was looking for. The book deals with his entire lifespan, which is not very long as he died at the age of 42. It was a life lived in melancholy and driven by a desire to answer the deepest question he could think of, asking what it means to be human. That question had others lurking around the corner which seemed to be different and yet part of the same pursuit, such as how one can live as an individual and what it means to be a Christian.

In addition to pursuing these questions, Kierkegaard was also one of the greatest attackers of "Christendom," the term he used for the Christian culture he lived in, often directed at Copenhagen but applying to Denmark and beyond. He argued that we live in a Christian age, but no one is a Christian because the doctrine had been intellectualized rather than followed. It was taken for granted and never a center of one’s life, and he felt that just like Socrates exposing the sophists in Athens, he had to expose the Christians of his age that did not follow a Christian life. What so attracts me to Kierkegaard and his Christianity is how it seems the exact antidote for the modern age. In a culture where we look at religion as making scientific and historical claims that have been proven false, Kierkegaard insisted that Christianity is not about facts, but rather the deepest of human truths beyond rationality that need to be lived. Besides his religious and existential problems, he was burdened by his relationship with Regine, to whom he was engaged before breaking it off a year later. He thought that his melancholy disposition would make both him and Regine unhappy as a married couple, and thus he decided that his life should be lived as an author alone. But he always loved Regine, and this decision, while he did not regret it, created angst in his life until the very end. She was always in his thoughts, and he dedicated his entire life of work to her. Consequently, he was deeply plagued by anxiety; while he had a fair bit of admirers in his lifetime, he was always an outcast due to both the scandal of his broken engagement and his writing. Even though he often wrote with pseudonyms, his work as a whole was known in his city, and he was constantly worried about how it would be received by the public.

His life story of struggle is incredible, and I really enjoyed reading it. While sometimes I found some events jumped around too abruptly, which was confusing, it was generally well written. I seldom felt bored, and the author’s commentary was superb, though I didn’t get an increased understanding of Kierkegaard regarding his philosophy to the degree that I expected since a lot of it is biographical in a sense that does not really touch on his philosophical work directly. Nevertheless, the biography is interesting in its own right, regardless of Kierkegaard’s ideas. Given how personal his philosophy is, maybe the biography will help me understand his ideas better in a way that I cannot even tell right now. If you are interested in Kierkegaard, I would recommend this book, and even more so if you are curious about his philosophy but know very little, as it serves as a good introduction while being enjoyable to read.
