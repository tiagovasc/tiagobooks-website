**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Voices Within_’ by_ Charles Fernyhough**

I initially became interested in the topic of voice-hearing while listening to a TED talk about a woman who started hearing voices and was subsequently diagnosed with schizophrenia, hospitalized, and drugged to infinity. Not only did the medication fail to help her, but it actually exacerbated her condition, yet she eventually found the Hearing Voices Movement (HVM) and, with its assistance, distanced herself from the standard psychiatry model to achieve recovery. Although I cannot quite pinpoint the specific talk, it unfortunately does not even matter because this story and path are so incredibly common in our current age where voice-hearing is strictly categorized as a symptom of madness or the result of a "broken brain." This perspective fits with the overall biomedical model of mental illness that is so pervasive today, where we view these experiences as simple biological misfunctions rather than meaningful psychological events.

The author’s work relies on two major pillars that serve as a critique of this current medical model of categorizing and interpreting voice-hearing, and by extension, they end up being a critique of psychiatry as a whole. Much like psychological explanations went too far in the 1960s, we are now too tied to biology and suffering from a trend of genetic and biological determinism from the 1980s that neuroimaging techniques have only made worse. We are often given the impression that we are able to track down pathologies to simple "malfunctions" of the brain when they are often just brain correlates that might not be causal. Consequently, the author argues that if we are overemphasizing biology, we are vastly underemphasizing the role of trauma, noting that child abuse alone contributes to around a third of psychosis cases. The voices are not random, as they are clearly meaningful manifestations of one’s life, yet this is rarely taken into account within modern psychiatry where the meaning of the voices is left unexplored and everything is sucked into a framework of brain pathology.

While the author certainly does not ignore the neurobiological approach and dedicates several chapters to it, the text touches on a wide range of topics including the interactions of genes and environment, epigenetics, the relationship with other conditions like PTSD, and the role of neurotransmitters. Most if not all the claims seemed quite reasonable, as there are no sketchy psychoanalytical interpretations and evidence is always taken into account, sometimes to a painstaking degree. I will admit that the book is not the most fun to read, so if you do not have a significant interest in psychiatry, I would guess this would be a hard book for most. However, its primary goal is not to be fun, and while dealing with these topics can easily get tedious at times, it is nevertheless captivating as a whole because the increased understanding one gains from it is incredibly valuable. If you truly want a deep understanding of voice-hearing and the science of how we speak to ourselves, I doubt there is a better book than this because the author's experience and knowledge are hard to match.
