I get this question a lot from friends and people who follow my work, and I never quite know where to start. It’s an incredibly difficult question, as it tends to be for most people doing one. It gets worse when the project is interdisciplinary, like mine. It also depends on how much time we have.

If you have a few seconds I’d say my PhD is about psychedelics, religion, and the crisis of meaning in the modern world.

If you have a bit more than that, I’d say that I take it seriously that psychedelics reliably produce profound and lasting transformation, and the single best predictor of that transformation is the intensity of the mystical experience itself. The ego dissolution, a sense of unity, and the encounter with something sacred. I argue that current frameworks cannot explain this. Neuroscience shows correlation, not meaning, and the philosophy of psychedelics systematically marginalizes religion despite the experiences themselves being saturated with religious content.&nbsp;

If you really want to know what it’s about, you’ll have to read the dissertation itself, of course. But what follows is a compromise between the paragraph and the full thing, for those who want a bit more. It takes roughly 20 minutes to read.

I’m still somewhat early in my PhD, so these are more a collection of notes and ideas than a fully fledged argument from A to B. You’ll have to give me some leeway in its logical progression and coherence.

**The Puzzle**

Psychedelic substances are not new. They have been integral to shamanic traditions, indigenous healing practices, and religious rituals across cultures for most of human history. Across ayahuasca, peyote, and iboga traditions, the substance belongs to a larger practice of preparation, guidance, and communal life. That matters because the unit we are trying to understand may be the whole practice, rather than the drug considered on its own.

The Eleusinian Mysteries, foundational to ancient Greek culture, likely involved a psychoactive brew and emphasized direct transformative experience as the highest form of knowledge. In the 20th century, Albert Hofmann’s discovery of LSD helped launch modern neuroscience by revealing that brain chemistry could profoundly alter consciousness. This sparked decades of promising clinical research, over a thousand papers and tens of thousands of patients treated, before political backlash shut it all down in the 1970s. The current renaissance, driven by landmark studies from researchers like Roland Griffiths and Robin Carhart-Harris, has brought psychedelics back with a force that is now impossible to ignore.

The clinical evidence is strong. Psilocybin and MDMA can produce significant, lasting relief for depression, PTSD, addiction, and end-of-life anxiety, often after just one or two sessions. The FDA has granted “Breakthrough Therapy” designations to both. But what makes psychedelics philosophically interesting, rather than just medically interesting, is this: across studies, the single best predictor of lasting positive change is the intensity of the mystical experience itself. People who undergo ego dissolution, a sense of unity, encounters with something sacred, and a conviction that the experience is more real than everyday life are the ones who get better and stay better. The sense that the psychedelic state is more real than ordinary consciousness is not a passing impression that fades when the drug wears off. It persists for months and years. That persistence does not prove the experience was true, but it remains something a philosophical account needs to explain. Participants routinely rate these sessions among the most meaningful experiences of their entire lives.

The outcomes extend well beyond symptom reduction. People report lasting personality changes, especially increased openness. They develop pro-social attitudes, deeper connections to nature, and enhanced cognitive flexibility. Their metaphysical beliefs shift away from strict materialism. There is also a consistent moral direction: across studies, there is a reliable movement away from a bounded, egoic sense of self toward a perception of universal connectedness, and away from a narrow moral circle toward one that includes distant others, animals, and the natural world.

If these experiences are just receptors being stimulated in unusual ways, why do they produce such profound and lasting transformation? If the content of these experiences, the visions, the moral insights, the encounter with something sacred, is noise generated by a chemically disrupted brain, why does that noise heal people in ways that years of conventional therapy sometimes cannot?

**Why Current Frameworks Fail**

Neuroscience has made real contributions. Psychedelics disrupt the Default Mode Network, a network of brain regions associated with our habitual sense of self and rumination. They increase global communication between regions that do not normally talk to each other. The REBUS model proposes that psychedelics relax the brain’s high-level assumptions, creating a window of cognitive flexibility. Research on brain criticality suggests that psychedelics push the brain toward a metastable state balanced between order and chaos, maximizing its capacity for insight and reconfiguration. This is often described as a “shaking of the snow globe,” where rigid neural patterns are temporarily dissolved.

These findings are useful, but my dissertation argues they leave the most important questions untouched. The problem is not that we need more data. Human beings do not only know things by knowing that something is the case. We also know how to do things, we know what it is like to see from a particular vantage point, and we know by being transformed through what we encounter. Modern science, and the philosophy of psychedelics that depends on it, operates almost entirely in the first mode: collecting and evaluating propositions. But psychedelic experience is not primarily propositional. It changes what shows up as real, what matters, and who you are in relation to it. These are shifts in perspective and in identity, not in the set of facts you believe.

Showing that a brain region is less active during ego dissolution does not explain why losing your sense of self can permanently change how you relate to other people. Knowing the pharmacology does not tell you why the content of these experiences takes the specific form it does and carries the specific meaning it carries. Treating the brain as the subject of experience commits what philosophers call the mereological fallacy: attributing to a part (the brain) what belongs to the whole (the person). And much of the celebrated neuroimaging data is built on remarkably small samples, often fifteen to twenty participants generating dozens of papers, with serious methodological concerns. The brain science is far less settled than the headlines suggest.

There is also a problem with treating the language of neuroscience as neutral. Calling an experience a hallucination already suggests something about its truth. Religious language can shape how participants report what happened, but removing that language can also exclude what they found most significant. And because people can often tell whether they received a psychedelic, expectation becomes difficult to separate from the drug’s effects. These limits make interpretation more necessary, not less.

The philosophical literature on psychedelics has its own limitations. It tends to cluster around two broad approaches. One remains broadly naturalistic, focusing on changes in brain function, cognition, and the sense of self to explain psychedelic effects and their therapeutic benefits. The other treats psychedelic experience as raising more fundamental questions about consciousness and reality, connecting it to wider debates in metaphysics and the philosophy of mind. Both approaches are valuable, but neither fully captures what seems most important about the phenomenon: its personal, moral, and transformative dimension. What happens to the person? Who are they becoming? How is their relationship to themselves, others, and the world being reoriented? Religion remains surprisingly marginal across much of the field. Evaluating its claims only as literal descriptions can miss what a symbol or practice enables a person to understand. Explaining an experience as a useful illusion also leaves open whether it might disclose something real.

My dissertation attempts to fill this gap. But to understand why religion has been sidelined in the first place, you have to understand how the modern world lost its sense of meaning.

**How We Got Here**

A large part of my dissertation traces the philosophical history of how the Western world became disenchanted. The ancient Greeks inhabited a “kosmos”: a world understood to be inherently meaningful, intelligible, and ordered. For Plato, reality was structured by Forms, with The Good as the highest principle, the ultimate source of both existence and intelligibility. For Aristotle, nature was driven by four types of causation, including formal causes (what makes a thing what it is) and final causes (the purpose toward which it moves). The universe was not a collection of stuff bumping into other stuff. It was a meaningful whole, oriented toward ends. This vision was absorbed into Christianity and provided the conceptual backbone for Western civilization for over a thousand years.

But starting in the late medieval period and accelerating through the scientific revolution, these ideas were systematically stripped away. Ockham’s nominalism severed the link between concepts and real essences. Galileo and Kepler mathematized nature. Descartes split mind from matter. The Reformation fragmented the unified sacramental cosmos. Newton gave us a clockwork universe where everything could be explained by efficient causation alone. Formal and final causes, the very concepts that made purpose and meaning intelligible, were discarded. Kant placed God beyond the reach of reason. The Romantics tried to recover a sense of the sacred through feeling and art, but it was too subjective to last. More recently, Nietzsche announced the death of God, not as a celebration but as a diagnosis, and then it culminated with the 20th century logical positivism and analytical philosophy, reducing all meaningful statements to either empirical facts or logical tautologies, and everything else to mere expressions of feeling.

The result is the world we inhabit: spectacularly powerful in its capacity to manipulate nature, deeply impoverished in its capacity to provide meaning. Charles Taylor calls this the shift from the “porous self,” open to a cosmos charged with meaning, spirits, and moral forces, to the “buffered self,” sealed off behind the walls of individual reason, invulnerable to enchantment but also unable to be addressed by anything beyond itself. The epidemic of loneliness, the crisis of mental health, the pervasive sense of purposelessness: these are symptoms of a worldview that has dismantled every conceptual tool we once had for understanding what makes life worth living. Psychedelics appear to temporarily shatter the buffer.

One of the most important arguments in my dissertation is that this loss has direct consequences for how we understand both suffering and flourishing. The classical concept of the Good and the modern understanding of mental illness are not separate categories. They are opposite ends of the same experiential axis. The Good represents the fullest realization of human potential: flourishing, meaning, connection. Mental illness represents its opposite: shame, compulsive repetition, isolation, loss of agency. Both are trajectories with momentum. Positive growth opens up further possibilities, creating virtuous cycles. Depression, addiction, and anxiety constrict experience, creating downward spirals where the range of response narrows and hardens. Addiction is the clearest case, as Marc Lewis has shown, arising not from disease but from learning mechanisms gone awry, where repetition of a desire loop collapses all possibility into a single, desperate pattern. The path toward the Good reverses this. It expands possibility and restores depth.

This is why I need a conception of the Good, rather than just a measure of how intense or pleasant an experience feels. A transformation can leave someone more convinced of themselves without making them wiser. The question is whether it makes them more capable of recognizing what is true, responding to other people, and acting on something beyond their immediate desires.

Modern psychiatry, by conceptualizing transformation only as a movement from pathology to normality, truncates this axis at the midpoint. It has no vocabulary for the upper half: flourishing, wisdom, the pursuit of excellence. Almost any premodern culture would find this strange. People embedded in living traditions were not trying to be average. They were not trying to not be miserable. They were trying to become saints, or sages, or to approach as closely as they could to the divine. The aim was the highest thing they could conceive of. The pathology model doesn’t just lower the standard; it erases the very idea of one. But the ancient philosophical tradition did have this vocabulary. For Plato, Aristotle, and the Stoics, philosophy was not an academic discipline but a way of life, a set of practices designed to transform the practitioner. Pierre Hadot’s work has recovered this vision, showing that the goal of ancient philosophy was not to produce theories but to produce wise human beings. Psychedelics, by occasionally catapulting people toward the upper end of this axis, reveal just how much of human possibility our current frameworks leave invisible. To make sense of this, we need a richer model of the mind.

**A Different Model of the Mind**

My dissertation also includes a dimension of cognitive science, specifically what is called 4E cognitive science. The “4E” stands for embodied, embedded, enacted, and extended. This framework challenges the older computational model of the mind, which treated the brain as a biological computer running software.

That computational model, which dominated cognitive science for decades, was a product of the same mechanistic worldview I just described. Born from mid-20th-century logic and early AI research, it treated cognition as abstract symbol manipulation, independent of the body, the environment, or lived experience. It failed, both philosophically (the philosopher Hubert Dreyfus showed, drawing on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty, that human intelligence cannot be captured by formal rules) and empirically (early AI based on this model hit hard limits). A second wave, connectionism, tried to model the brain rather than a computer, but still operated within a fundamentally mechanical framework.

4E cognitive science says something different. The mind is not a computer locked inside a skull. Cognition arises from the dynamic interaction between an organism and its environment. We think with our bodies, through our engagement with the world. Meaning is not computed internally; it emerges from how we are coupled with our surroundings, what the world affords us, and what we are oriented toward.

A central concept here is relevance realization. At any moment, there are more things you could notice than you could possibly process. Much of cognition consists in selecting what matters, usually before you consciously deliberate. What you fear, desire, and know how to do shapes what you see as possible. This helps explain why someone can understand their problem perfectly well and still find themselves unable to act differently. The information is there, but their sense of what matters and what they can do has not changed.

Psychedelics can disrupt that organization of relevance. Intelligence helps you solve a problem within a given frame. Rationality also involves recognizing when the frame itself is wrong. You can be very clever at pursuing something that is making your life worse. A change in perspective can therefore matter more than another argument or another piece of information. But breaking a frame does not tell you which new one to adopt. The Good gives us a standard for judging that change, and religious practices train our attention toward it.

Embodiment also shapes our abstract concepts. George Lakoff’s research on conceptual metaphor demonstrates exactly this. We do not have a set of literal, non-metaphorical concepts for understanding abstract ideas. All abstract thought is structured by spatial, physical metaphors grounded in bodily experience. “More” is “up.” Understanding is “grasping.” Time is a “journey.” They are the foundational architecture of how thinking works. Even science relies on them.

This provides a direct bridge to the symbolic structures found in religious traditions. If the deepest patterns of human thought are spatial and embodied, rooted in experiences of up and down, center and periphery, containment and journey, then the recurring symbolic structures of religion are not arbitrary cultural inventions. They are articulations of the deep grammar of human experience. Heaven above, earth below, chaos beneath. The center as sacred, the periphery as wilderness. The ascent toward the divine, the descent into suffering. These cosmological patterns show up across cultures not because of historical borrowing but because they reflect how embodied minds make the world intelligible.

**Recovering Religion**

With this grounding in place, I can now say more precisely what I mean by “religion” and why it matters.

To see why, you first need to understand stories. A story is the basic mode through which human beings make reality intelligible. Stories compress vast complexity into meaningful sequences that highlight what is significant. And stories are not isolated. They connect to larger patterns, which is why certain motifs, the hero’s journey, death and rebirth, descent into darkness and return to light, recur across cultures and across millennia. They reflect something real about how experience is structured.

Religions are, at their deepest level, meta-stories: stories that hierarchically organize all other systems of meaning, providing an ultimate orientation for human life. Their symbolic structures are not details. A symbol, unlike a sign, does not merely point to something already known. It discloses reality. It opens up a dimension of experience that might be inaccessible any other way. You can see this in how different traditions encode transformation itself. Christianity uses the metaphor of death and rebirth: “You were dead in your sins, but God made you alive.” “Unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God.” The Eastern traditions use a different metaphor for the same structure: sleep and awakening. Someone asks the Buddha, “Are you a god?” “No.” “Are you a man?” “No.” “Then what are you?” “I am awake.” Both are saying that there are qualitatively different states of being, and that the ordinary condition is the lower one.

Rituals are the embodied practices through which these symbolic structures are internalized and lived. Prayer, confession, communal worship, rites of passage are technologies of attention and transformation, grounded in the same embodied cognition that 4E cognitive science describes. They work because cognition is not abstract computation. It is shaped by what you do with your body, in a place, with other people, repeatedly, over time. This is also why the common secular move of extracting individual practices from their traditions does not work as well as people hope. The trajectory of Buddhism in the West is the clearest case. What arrived as one of the most profound contemplative traditions in human history was, within a few decades, reduced to a stress-management app. Meditation, stripped of the Eightfold Path, the sangha, the ethical framework, and any orienting conception of liberation, became another tool for self-optimization. The depth was gutted but the brand was kept. Religious traditions are complex, internally coherent systems. Their practices are not modular components you can pull out and slot into a different framework without losing most of what made them effective. A purely secular approach to psychedelic integration will run into the same problem, and for the same structural reasons.

A tradition also preserves practical knowledge that no individual could work out alone. Its practices have been transmitted, tested, and revised across generations. This gives us a reason to study them carefully without assuming that everything they preserve is wise. The question is what their accumulated experience can teach us about transformation.

At the heart of all of this is wisdom, a concept that has fallen out of fashion in academic philosophy despite being its spiritual and etymological root. Wisdom is not the accumulation of facts. It is a kind of existential competence: the capacity to see clearly, act rightly, and navigate the competing demands of a complex life. Most people already know, propositionally, that they should be less selfish, more present, more compassionate. They have known this since childhood. It does not help. Knowing that you should be a better person and actually becoming one are separated by exactly the gap that the distinction between propositional and participatory knowledge describes. You cannot think your way across it. You need practices, models, a community, and a sustained way of life that makes the knowledge real in your body and your relationships, not just in your head. Religious traditions cultivate this kind of wisdom not through abstract instruction but through embodied models. We follow people, not principles. Human beings learn how to be by imitating other human beings. This is why saints, sages, the Buddha, and Christ function not as rule-books but as living ideals that reshape the person who orients toward them. The ideal pulls a person forward, and each person who embodies it makes it more real and more accessible for others. This is also why the religious life is necessarily communal. It cannot be a private optimization project.

But understanding any of this requires what the philosopher John Cottingham calls an “epistemology of receptivity.” You cannot grasp the value of a religious tradition while standing at arm’s length, evaluating its propositional claims like a scientist testing hypotheses. Some truths require a different posture: openness, trust, a willingness to be addressed and changed. A purely analytical mode of engagement blocks the very perception it claims to be testing. This does not mean suspending judgment indefinitely. It means acquiring enough experience of a practice to know what you are judging. As with learning music, some things become available to perception only through training. The question is whether that formation improves your capacity to see and respond, including your capacity to recognize self-deception. A common objection here is that religions are essentially systems of rules and control. But at their deepest level, religious traditions are not prescribing rules. They are describing what human experience is actually like: what leads to flourishing, what leads to misery, and how to navigate the difference. The ethical demands of a tradition are not arbitrary impositions. They are maps drawn by people who walked the territory before you.

**The Sacred&nbsp;**

Reality is inherently structured by meaning. My claim is that religious traditions can disclose something real about it, and that their value extends beyond making us feel better or holding a community together. But an experience of certainty does not settle how it should be interpreted. I take the religious vision seriously while leaving open what it discloses and how its claims should be understood alongside scientific knowledge. Our relationship to reality can deepen as our way of attending and living changes.

I call this the sacred: not a place or an entity, but the ongoing process through which reality discloses itself in deeper and more meaningful ways, enabling continuous transformation. This is what psychedelics give a temporary glimpse of. But the key word is temporary.

Genuine personal change, the kind reported by psychedelic participants and sought by wisdom traditions, is not habit modification. It is closer to what religious traditions describe as metanoia, a fundamental reorientation of the mind, or kenosis, a self-emptying that makes space for something new. It is a shift in identity, not just isolated behavior. This is not a mystical abstraction. You can see the same structure in ordinary life. People who try to quit smoking almost never succeed by tapering gradually. They succeed, when they do, by making a decision that feels like crossing a threshold: one day they are a smoker, the next they are not. Addiction researchers have documented the same pattern. Alcoholics Anonymous, whatever its limitations, understood this: its entire structure was designed not to help people drink less but to become a different kind of person. The gradual, incremental model of change works for many things, but for the deepest reorientations of identity, something qualitatively different is required. A leap, not a slope.

Traditional rites of passage understood this. They followed a universal structure: first, separation from the ordinary world and its familiar structures; then, a liminal threshold state where identity is dissolved and the normal rules of life are suspended; and finally, reincorporation into the community as a transformed person. The transition from boy to man, or from outsider to member of the community, was not gradual. It was a ritualized event involving symbolic death and rebirth, and it required all three phases to work. Psychedelic experiences mirror this structure closely. People describe being pulled out of their ordinary frame of reference, entering a liminal space where the self dissolves and categories break down, and returning with a fundamentally new orientation toward themselves and the world. The difference is that traditional cultures had the entire ritual apparatus, the elders, the community, the story, the follow-through, to support each phase. Today, we have little else than a clinic room and a follow-up questionnaire.

Three philosophers help me articulate what is at stake here. Kierkegaard understood that the most important truths cannot merely be known; they must be lived. His analysis of faith as a leap, a transition from possibility to actuality, captures something essential about the discontinuous nature of transformation. Heidegger showed that truth is not a correspondence between statements and facts but an unconcealment, reality actively disclosing itself. His critique of the technological worldview, which reduces everything to raw material for manipulation, describes exactly the threat that the modern framing of psychedelics poses. And Iris Murdoch argued that genuine moral progress comes not from willpower but from a quality of loving attention that gets the ego out of the way, what she called “unselfing.” Murdoch also provides the corrective that Heidegger lacked: without orientation toward the Good as a transcendent standard, authenticity becomes ethically empty and vulnerable to ideology.

But psychedelic experiences fade. This is the central practical problem of the entire field and it remains unsolved. There is no agreed-upon model for what “integration” even means. To the degree that a pattern exists, it is medical: more sessions with a therapist, more talking, more cognitive processing. But psychedelic insight is not primarily propositional. It is a shift in how you inhabit the world, and talk therapy, however valuable for other purposes, is not designed to sustain that kind of shift. The practical question is how a change in perspective becomes a stable change in conduct.

**Why This Matters Now**

Psychedelic substances are being integrated into mainstream medicine, and millions of people will have access to these experiences in the coming decades. The question is what framework we will use to make sense of them.

Commodification is well underway: psychedelics that were symbols of radical freedom in the 1960s are now being repackaged as productivity tools. Microdosing explicitly avoids the transformative dimension. But the more insidious version is subtler: even full-blown mystical experiences can be absorbed into the logic of optimization if the surrounding framework remains one of productivity and self-improvement. Without a framework that transcends utilitarian thinking, even the most profound experience gets metabolized by the very system it momentarily disrupted.

Psychedelics are also not without real dangers. Without interpretive frameworks, these experiences can fuel delusional belief systems, magical thinking, and grandiosity. But these problems do not discredit the experiences. They demonstrate the need for exactly the kind of tested, grounded, communal wisdom traditions that historically guided people through such states. The temptation is to invent new rituals from scratch, secular frameworks that capture what religion does without any symbolic, narrative and metaphysical considerations. But genuine traditions develop slowly, organically, over centuries. They cannot be engineered on a whiteboard, and the track record of modern attempts to manufacture “spiritual but not religious” frameworks is not encouraging. The existing traditions, with all their difficulty and strangeness, deserve serious engagement rather than dismissal.

I did not arrive at this conclusion easily. I spent years dismissing tradition as the residue of pre-scientific thinking, confident that I knew better than people who had lived centuries or millennia before me. I kept being proven wrong, enough times that I had to revise my position. The real work of sustained transformation, the movement from suffering toward flourishing that both psychedelics and wisdom traditions point toward, requires not a molecule but a way of life: practices of attention, self-emptying, communal belonging, ritual, and ethical orientation sustained over years and decades.

That is what my PhD is about.
