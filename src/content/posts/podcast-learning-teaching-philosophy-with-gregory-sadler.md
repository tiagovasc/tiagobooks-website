Gregory Sadler spent years as a self-described burnout before stumbling into philosophy at random, and he has spent three decades since trying to get out of the way and let the thinkers speak. In this conversation he traces that journey from a Catholic high school substitute teacher who taught Plato in a sacraments class, through the Army and a series of wrong turns, to becoming one of the most prolific philosophy educators on the internet. The discussion moves through the craft of teaching, why caring about the student matters more than caring about the subject, why philosophy never actually died no matter how many times Hawking or Russell said it did, and why the smartest move in a room full of business executives is often to keep Aristotle anonymous until after the ideas have landed. Sadler and Tiago then work through the honest trade-offs between primary texts and secondary literature, the dangers of autodidactic bias, and what it means to believe in God without having a particularly good relationship with him.

## Who is Dr. Gregory Sadler?

Gregory B. Sadler is a philosopher, educator, and public intellectual who taught for nine years as an assistant professor at Ball State University and Fayetteville State University before relocating to New York. He runs the ReasonIO YouTube channel, one of the most comprehensive free philosophy resources on the internet, and works as a consultant bringing philosophical frameworks, drawn primarily from Aristotle, Stoicism, and Cicero, into professional and business contexts. He is also a co-founder of the Half Hour Hegel project and teaches online courses through the Institute of Competitive Intelligence and other platforms.

## Top 3 Theses

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; The teacher's investment in the student is at least as important as the teacher's investment in the subject. A great philosophy class is not about the material, it is about whether the person in front of you can feel that you want them to actually engage with it.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Philosophy is not dead and has never been dead. Announcements of its demise go back centuries and are always made by people who lack the philosophical depth to evaluate the discipline they are dismissing.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; The most effective way to introduce philosophy to skeptical audiences is often to withhold the brand name entirely. Present the idea from Aristotle or Aquinas as a practical insight, let it land, and only reveal its source when people are already persuaded by it.

## Key Insights

**1. Great teaching is Socratic midwifery, not content delivery.** Sadler distinguishes between teachers who care about the subject and teachers who care about the student developing. The second type, the substitute who made him stay for discussion, the professor who said read whatever the hell you want, are the ones who changed his life. The insight is Socratic: the teacher's job is to bring to birth ideas in other minds, not in their own head, and that requires connecting not just to the subject but to the person.

**2. Philosophy never died; it just keeps getting eulogized by people who haven't read enough of it.** Sadler pushes back directly on the Hawking-Dawkins strain of dismissal, pointing out that positivistic declarations of philosophy's death go back to Herbert Spencer and beyond. His diagnosis is consistent: people like Hawking lack the philosophical chops to say anything meaningful about the discipline. The announcements reveal the limits of the announcer, not the limits of the field.

**3. When you're teaching outside the discipline, don't lead with the label.** Sadler's method with business clients is to present Aristotelian or Stoic ideas as practical frameworks and only disclose their origin once the ideas have already landed. Leading with Thomas Aquinas triggers the Ryan Holiday reflex. The authority Sadler trades on is not philosophical prestige but his own standing as a consultant, and once the ideas have worked, the attribution becomes welcome rather than a barrier.

**4. Primary texts and secondary literature involve a real trade-off with no perfect answer.** Sadler takes the question of reading primary versus secondary sources seriously rather than defaulting to the standard academic position. He grants that secondary literature can contain real misinterpretations, the thesis-antithesis-synthesis cliché about Hegel, the 19th-century devaluation of Stoic philosophy, but he also acknowledges that reading Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations probably benefits more from a summary than from wandering through it alone. The practical advice: you cannot read everything even professionally, the good news is that professionals read comparatively little more than serious amateurs do.

**5. Autodidactic bias is real but manageable once you name it.** The conversation surfaces a genuine danger in self-directed reading: you follow your enthusiasms, amplify your existing sympathies, and never seriously engage with thinkers you are already disposed to dismiss. Sadler's response is measured, not the broccoli-only diet of reading only what is good for you, but something closer to the friend who eats broccoli before steak. The discipline of occasionally reading what you don't want to read is part of intellectual maturity, and the awareness of the bias is itself most of the remedy.

I see people doing this with Stoicism, saying "I'm only going to read Stoic literature," when the Stoics themselves read other books. It's like someone saying, "I only eat potato chips." You have a brain that lets you choose other things. There can be a danger, but once we are aware of it, we can work past it. Sometimes people just go through phases—like an obsession with Nietzsche, or getting into CrossFit or veganism—where they talk to everyone about it, and later they might look back and wonder why they were that person.

**S:** I would start by saying there's no such thing as "religion as such" or "philosophy as such." They are historical and complicated. **T:** When you mentioned that you believe in God, would you say your belief is derived from a philosophical analysis? Or would you say it is a truth that goes beyond and encompasses philosophy?

**S:** I would say it encompasses philosophy, and philosophy informs it. God gave us brains because He wants us to use them.
