John Cottingham has spent fifty years working at the intersection of Descartes, moral philosophy, and the philosophy of religion, and this conversation covers the full arc of those concerns. He begins by arguing that philosophy should never be a narrow academic specialty but a synoptic discipline engaging the deepest questions of the human condition, then traces how he tries to make those questions accessible without diluting them. The conversation moves through the limits of scientism and the need for an "epistemology of receptivity," through the relationship between the Platonic Good and the Christian God, and into why abstract affirmation of the Good is not enough without practice, liturgy, and embodied enactment. It closes with a reflection on career, integrity, and what Aristotle meant when he said that living without a telos is a sign of great folly.

## Who Is John Cottingham?

John Cottingham is Emeritus Professor of Philosophy at the University of Reading and an Honorary Fellow of St John's College, Oxford. He is one of the foremost scholars of early modern philosophy, particularly Descartes, and in the last two decades has become a leading voice in the philosophy of religion. His recent books include In Search of the Soul (Princeton University Press) and The Humane Perspective (Oxford University Press). His work consistently argues for a broader, non-scientistic conception of philosophical inquiry that draws on literature, art, emotion, and spiritual experience alongside analytic argument.

## TOP 3 Thesis

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Philosophy should be synoptic, not specialized: the big questions about meaning, purpose, and rightness are not academic curiosities but endemic to the human condition, and the philosopher's job is to articulate them as clearly and accessibly as possible for everyone, not just specialists.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Scientism is the distortion, not science: science is one of humanity's greatest achievements, but the error lies in treating it as the sole touchstone of truth. An "epistemology of receptivity", a mode of knowing that requires openness and porosity rather than detached analysis, is necessary to perceive the dimensions of reality that science cannot capture.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; The good must be enacted, not merely affirmed: the gap between Platonic philosophy and living Christianity is not primarily doctrinal but practical. Worship, liturgy, and embodied ritual are not decorations around a philosophical truth, they are the mechanism through which human beings actually incorporate allegiance to the good into their lives.

## Key Insights

**1. Philosophy’s central questions are not specialists’ questions, they are everyone’s.**

Cottingham pushes back against the academicisation of philosophy, which has retreated into technicalities and smaller and smaller research areas of interest only to other specialists. His alternative is a synoptic discipline that addresses why we are here, what the purpose of our life is, and whether there are objective moral standards. Most people turn to these questions at least occasionally, he argues, so the philosopher’s task is not to create a separate discipline but to articulate what all thoughtful people already wonder about.

**2. An epistemology of receptivity is needed alongside the scientific method.**

The distortion Cottingham identifies is not science itself but scientism, the view that unless something can be expressed scientifically, it is not a valid truth. He proposes an alternative model of knowledge he calls an epistemology of receptivity: certain realities can only be discerned through openness rather than detached scrutiny. Drawing on Martha Nussbaum’s image of being “porous” to great literature, and Wittgenstein’s remark that life, including suffering, can educate one to belief in God, Cottingham argues that left-brain analysis alone closes off entire dimensions of reality rather than illuminating them.

**3. The conflict between science and religion is real but routinely framed wrongly.**

Cottingham refuses the simple “no conflict” line. His more nuanced position is that science’s intelligibility of the cosmos is itself compatible with, even suggestive of, a theistic logos at the heart of existence. On the incarnation and resurrection, he follows a Wittgensteinian strategy: these claims cannot be extracted from their whole form of life and evaluated in isolation. Their meaning is embedded in the life and teachings of Jesus of Nazareth and the subsequent faith of the church. Forcing them into a verificationist mold simply misunderstands what kind of claims they are.

**4. The Platonic Good and the Christian God are closer than Iris Murdoch thought, but the gap that remains is decisive.**

Murdoch’s phrase “the good is the reality of which God is the dream” privileges the Platonic form over the personal God of Christianity. Cottingham argues that when Murdoch herself describes the Good as having active power to transform lives, as commanding allegiance, as drawing human beings as a source of meaning, the gap between Platonism and theism narrows considerably. What remains, however, is critical: in the Abrahamic traditions, the sovereignty of the good is grounded in a cosmological claim about the source of all existence, not just a moral claim about what we should pursue.

**5. Practice, liturgy, and embodied enactment are not optional supplements to philosophical religion, they are its engine.**

The conversation’s sharpest insight is Cottingham’s answer to why abstract affirmation of the Good is insufficient. In worship, people do not merely assert their allegiance to goodness, they enact it: in movement, in song, in solidarity, in liturgy that plugs them into a tradition. Cottingham invokes Iris Murdoch’s novels to show the pitfall of thinking philosophy alone can solve life’s deepest problems; her characters are often clever philosophers who are strikingly unself-aware about the feelings that actually drive them. Self-knowledge, and orientation toward the good, requires practice as much as theory.

**Cottingham:** Yes, I suppose it does, in a way. There are complications with psychedelics. When I was growing up and in my early adulthood (60s, 70s), they were often associated with a very fluid sense of life — just going with the flow. That felt liberating compared to the rather gray, constrained post-war 50s. But it also led to considerable trouble, with people trying to live without structures often coming to grief.

What do I think about it now? This connects to spirituality: what is spiritual practice about? It seems crucial to authentic spirituality, in my view, that spiritual experience is a _response_ to something beyond us, not purely endogenous. When we respond to nature’s beauty, great art’s wonder, or the moral imperative (the normative dimension you mentioned), we feel a response called forth by something other than ourselves.

Many who extol psychedelics, including contemporary atheist thinkers like Sam Harris, seem to view spiritual experience as endogenous — generated by internal feelings. He might suggest you can get it via fasting or drugs; the generation method is somewhat irrelevant. It’s seen as an internal state making you say “Wow!” (to use a 60s term). If these are just strange, quirky experiences triggered by brain chemistry, they lack real significance. They might feel good temporarily, up and down, but have no ultimate meaning — just psychological or brain states, on a strict atheist materialist view.

Yet, these kinds of experiences have traditionally been characterized (in literature, music, religion) as having profound significance for understanding ourselves and our place in the world. So, there seems to be a divide here between religious or quasi-religious forms of spirituality and the cultivation of internal states within a purely materialist framework. Perhaps I haven’t expressed that perfectly, but there’s a broad contrast. If ‘psychedelic’ refers only to the latter — endogenous states triggered by LSD, etc. — then I don’t see how they can have the transcendent significance I would ascribe to genuine spiritual experiences.

**Tiago:** Yes, it’s tricky. First, I’m not sure that’s exactly Sam Harris’s position.
