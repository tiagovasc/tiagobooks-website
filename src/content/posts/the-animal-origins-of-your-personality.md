**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Emotional Foundations of Personality_’ by_ Jaak Panksepp and Kenneth L. Davis &nbsp;**

I have always been interested in what drives human behavior, and it has become increasingly evident to me that personality is a major contributor to how human beings and animals perceive and act in the world. This realization is largely what initially drew me into psychology. While it seems clear that personality must have an evolutionary foundation, that link is surprisingly rarely made in academic circles. Instead, the study of personality has mostly focused on psychometrics and correlating traits with various aspects of behavior. While this statistical approach has value, I always felt that it was not going deep enough into the biological framework we have inherited. Reading _The Emotional Foundations of Personality_ was, in some sense, a bit bizarre because it answered a lot of questions that I thought had no answer yet, or even answered questions I did not know I had. I felt as if I had managed to peek into the future to see a new paradigm of personality science. The main author, Jaak Panksepp, was the father of affective neuroscience and a figure whose work is paramount in contemporary psychology. He authored over ten books and six hundred research papers, garnering over 43,000 citations, and is perhaps most famous for discovering that non-human animals laugh. His magnum opus, _Affective Neuroscience_, will surely be remembered as a historical pivot in human understanding. It captured the emotional reality of beings—a subject usually abstract and hard to pin down—and rooted it in biology. This was a feat attempted by Darwin himself, but Panksepp achieved it not only in terms of adaptive value and phylogenetic emergence but also regarding neurochemical structure. This approach not only elucidates our position in nature and reminds us of our animal underpinnings, but it also elevates and humanizes animals, especially mammals, given the undeniable overlap between our emotional realities.

The book serves as a summary of Panksepp’s legacy but focuses more heavily on personality, tracing its roots all the way back to Darwin’s emotional theory in _The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals_. It links our personality to basic emotional circuits present since early mammalian brain anatomy, with some systems going as far back as fish and worms. Sadly, Panksepp passed away in 2017, so this book was co-authored by Kenneth Davis, a former student and later colleague. Together, they outline how basic primary emotions evolved to tackle particular environmental challenges or patterns of behavior. These are present in all mammalian brains and are localized subcortically. By studying them, one can finally develop a sound personality theory with an epistemology based on evolutionary understanding. Panksepp identifies seven primary emotions: SEEKING, RAGE, FEAR, LUST, CARE, GRIEF, and PLAY. A spirituality dimension is also mentioned at one point, although he unfortunately never explores it. In the text, all systems are referenced in capitals to ensure it is known that the authors are referencing specific neural circuits and not just the colloquial usage of the words. The affective neuroscientific validity of these basic emotions is based on three rigorous criteria. First, behaviors resulting from affective feelings can be aroused by electrical stimulation of their corresponding brain locations. Second, the reverse is also true, meaning the brain areas associated with each affect are in use whenever the affect is present naturally. Third, these affects rarely originate from higher prefrontal neocortical regions. While the cortex can regulate them, it is not their origin.

To understand the architecture of personality, one must look at how these specific systems function. SEEKING is involved with the reward system and can be thought of as a drive, similar to what Freud called "libido" and Spinoza called "conatus." It is especially associated with novelty and generates everything from basic curiosity to intellectual pursuits like philosophical discussion. It is closely associated with Openness to Experience in the Five Factor Model (FFM) and heavily influences LUST, CARE, and PLAY. RAGE is what is classically thought of as the fight-flight response and is most often associated with the restriction of freedom, such as cornering a cat. It generates aggressive behavior to eliminate threats or arouse the FEAR system in the opponent. It is inversely associated with Emotional Stability and Agreeableness in the FFM. FEAR, conversely, protects animals from physical damage and death, leading to freezing or fleeing. In humans, it presents as anxiety and is inversely associated with Emotional Stability. LUST has an obvious purpose and is heavily influenced by estrogen and testosterone, creating gender-specific tendencies that influence trust, confidence, and jealousy. CARE ensures paternal investment and is heavily associated with maternal care and Agreeableness. GRIEF, previously called PANIC/SADNESS, is the system for receiving nurturance; it deals with social attachment and bonding, causing crying in infants and general distress at social rejection. Finally, PLAY represents rough-and-tumble activity. While it often looks aggressive, it results in positive affect and is essential for mammals to learn social rules. Lack of play is associated with ADHD and degrades plasticity in the prefrontal cortex, yet it is often unfortunately demonized in modern society.

It is honestly mind-blowing how deep these systems run and how blatantly obvious they are, even though we often fail to recognize them. For example, both humans and other animals bare their teeth when angry, and our hair stands up when we are fearful. These are examples of ancient, innate responses that proved adaptive to our ancestors. However, the book explains that personality is not just these primary drives. There are also secondary-process emotions rooted in learning via the basal ganglia—classical and operant conditioning—and a third level of affect which is neocortical. This "tertiary" process includes executive functions, thoughts, planning, and emotional rumination. In simpler terms, primary-process emotions are basic instinctual frameworks, secondary processes are based on learning, and tertiary processes are related to cognition. The primary emotions are what lay the foundations of personality—how we perceive and act in the world. Personality theory initially belonged to medicine and the theory of humors, which, funny enough, is actually more in line with Panksepp’s neurobiological approach than our current models. He links the sanguine temperament with PLAY, choleric with RAGE, melancholic with GRIEF, and phlegmatic with FEAR.

Personality was later claimed by psychology, although at the time it was more a ramification of philosophy with little empirical work. In the mid-twentieth century, statistics were seen as the solution to escape the philosophical grey waters of personality theory. By looking for patterns among the adjectives we use to describe other people, researchers hoped to provide an objective account of personality. Panksepp claims this was useful in the era of the "black box" of psychology, but it is now of limited relevance. These models may only show the conceptual and descriptive categories of language used to describe behavior rather than the causes of the behavior itself. As Darwin recognized, emotions arise well before language. Affect is the language of the genes, the oldest language of them all. The FFM seems especially defective at dealing with negative emotions, which are all clumped into "emotional stability" even though there are clearly several distinct systems at play that produce specific psychopathologies. Top-down statistical models try to describe personality, while bottom-up approaches explain it. The book is incredibly well written and, while it contains some technical aspects, no in-depth background is needed to appreciate it. It lays out a new understanding of personality grounded in evolutionary biology that is not only fascinating but also extremely useful, particularly for psychiatry, where future treatments may be based on adjusting out-of-balance emotional systems.
