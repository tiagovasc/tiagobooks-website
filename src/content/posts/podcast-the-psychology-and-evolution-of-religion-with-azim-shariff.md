Why does religion keep surviving? Azim Shariff, a social and moral psychologist at the University of British Columbia, offers one of the most rigorous and compelling answers available in contemporary science. In this wide-ranging conversation, he traces the origins of moralistic monotheism to the problem of large-scale cooperation with strangers, explains why cultural evolution selects for certain religious ideas over others, and explores whether religion still offers adaptive value in a secular age. The conversation also covers what makes religious belief difficult to study scientifically, how cultural environments shape which aspects of a religion take hold, and the story behind a retracted paper on religiosity, crime, and IQ that generated significant controversy. Equal parts rigorous and honest, this is a masterclass in applied moral psychology.

## Who is Azim Shariff?

Azim Shariff is Associate Professor of Psychology at the University of British Columbia, where he studies social and moral psychology with a particular focus on the psychology of religion. His research examines how religious belief intersects with morality, prosocial behaviour, and cooperation. More recently, he has expanded into applied moral psychology, including the ethics and public perception of self-driving cars. He completed his graduate training at UBC under Ara Norenzayan.

## Top 3 Theses

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Religion and morality evolved together to solve the same problem: enabling large groups of unrelated strangers to cooperate. Moralistic gods who watch and judge behaviour emerged precisely when societies outgrew the trust networks of small bands and needed a supernatural enforcer to hold cooperation together.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Cultural evolution does not guarantee that everything which persists is adaptive. Religious ideas survive through a combination of cognitive fit with the human mind, demographic reproduction, and prestige copying, and some linger well past their usefulness, functioning as what Dawkins would call mind viruses.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Religion is far more than a set of beliefs. Participatory practices, painful rituals, and synchronized physiological states between members produce a kind of group fusion that cannot be fully captured by questionnaires, and that science is only beginning to measure.

## Key Insights

**1. Morality and religion solved the same evolutionary problem.**

From an evolutionary standpoint, morality exists to regulate social relationships. Shariff argues that religion culturally emerged for the same purpose, the “socially effervescent” function described by Durkheim. The religions that survived were those whose moral frameworks most effectively enabled large-scale cooperation. The moralistic, all-seeing god familiar from the Abrahamic traditions emerged specifically when societies expanded beyond the 150–200 person bands where reputation and trust could do that work alone.

**2. Cultural evolution selects for religious ideas, but the process is messy.**

Ideas compete culturally the way genes compete biologically, but the evidence is harder to weigh. Shariff identifies repetition across cultures as the strongest signal of adaptation. Joe Henrich’s work on cousin-marriage bans offers a case study: small theological decisions with enormous downstream effects on group cohesion and economic development. Still, historical accidents, an assassination, a drought, a chance migration, can elevate ideas regardless of their adaptive worth.

**3. Religion may still be adaptive, and the next century will test that.**

Projections show that the most religious populations are also the most fertile, meaning the world will become proportionally more religious over time through demographics alone. Shariff resists the secularisation hypothesis, the idea that modernity inevitably erodes religion, arguing that harder environmental conditions (climate disruption, war, scarcity) reliably increase religious belief. Whether secular institutions can fully replace religion’s social functions is an open empirical question, not a settled one.

**4. Religion differs across cultures because it fills different niches.**

Buddhism’s emphasis on individual transcendence and Christianity’s emphasis on community reflect the different social environments in which each evolved. Where Confucianism already handled social cohesion in East Asian contexts, religion didn’t need to. Where no such framework existed in the early West, Christianity had to evolve to fill that role. This insight extends to contemporary Christianity: newer sects, emerging within societies that already have robust secular legal institutions, are freed to fill entirely different psychological and community niches.

**5. Theological incorrectness explains why sacred texts and lived religion diverge.**

What religious texts say and what believers actually do are often different things, because some doctrines simply don’t fit human cognitive architecture. Most Buddhists deify the Buddha despite explicit instructions not to. Canadian Muslims may avoid pork but drink alcohol, not because they endorse inconsistency, but because other cultural forces push back against different prohibitions with different force. Religions are cultural packages that evolve in contact with the ideas that surround them, which is why globalization is already liberalising many traditionally conservative religious communities.

**6. The retracted IQ paper: bad data, not a bad question.**

Shariff speaks openly about a retracted paper examining whether religious decline predicts increases in violent crime, and whether this effect was moderated by national-level cognitive ability. The retraction was not about the underlying hypothesis, which Shariff still considers genuinely interesting and worth pursuing. The problems were twofold: imputed homicide data for lower-income countries was confounded with the religiosity measure itself, and the cross-national IQ data used (traced entirely to one researcher, Richard Lynn) was based on samples so unrepresentative as to be meaningless. He draws a careful distinction between the politically charged topic of racial IQ differences and the more tractable empirical claim that nutrition, health, and iodine deficiency, unevenly distributed across countries, causally affect cognitive development.
