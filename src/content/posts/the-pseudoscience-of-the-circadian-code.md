**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Circadian Code: Lose Weight, Supercharge Your Energy and Sleep Well Every N_’ by_ Satchin Panda**

I have struggled with sleep problems for as long as I can remember. I historically went to bed late, rarely felt sleepy until well past midnight, and often required more than an hour to actually fall asleep. When I eventually got into fitness in my late teens, I began the arduous process of fixing my sleep schedule. Little by little, I managed to normalize it, but doing so required great effort and took many years of trial and error. To aid this process, I read extensively on sleep hygiene and, by extension, the circadian rhythm that heavily influences it. Recently, someone recommended _The Circadian Code_ to me, noting that Dr. Satchin Panda is a supposed expert on the subject. Although I already knew a great deal about the topic and my sleep was decently managed, I figured I might find some novel information to further improve my quality of life. That assumption was a mistake. The book is bad, and I regret picking it up. I noticed the issues immediately and wanted to stop reading, but I decided to finish it for the sake of this review, hoping to better inform others before they waste their time.

While the basic information regarding the circadian rhythm is acceptable, the author makes wild extrapolations where his claims far exceed the presented evidence. Most of the cited studies are on rats, and the few human studies presented are often weak or preliminary. He includes countless anecdotes, but I find it hard to believe he does not recognize the heavy selection bias that entails. The worst aspect is that this book attempts to go beyond explaining how the circadian rhythm works and tries to function as a typical all-encompassing diet and self-help book. Because of this overreach, the author commits numerous scientific errors. For example, he repeatedly claims that fasting enhances fat burning based on an oversimplified model of insulin. In reality, while you burn fat from reduced insulin while fasting, if your feeding window is smaller, you will simply have a larger fat formation response when you do eat. Over a 24-hour period, the net effect is the same. He makes similar erroneous claims that fasted cardio burns more fat and that high-protein intakes are dangerous for kidneys, both of which have been debunked by research (Phillips, 2016; Schoenfeld, 2014).

The errors continue into his discussion of gut health and hydration. He suggests diet soda is universally bad for the microbiome, a claim that lacks nuance regarding different sweeteners and realistic dosages, and he perpetuates the myth that coffee dehydrates you. While caffeine is a diuretic, the water in the coffee compensates for this, and the diuretic effect tends to fade in habitual drinkers. Beyond general nutrition, the author even stumbles on advice directly related to his field. He recommends melatonin dosages between 1mg and 5mg, which is far too high for most people and can cause side effects like grogginess. A more appropriate dose is closer to 0.3mg, meaning he is off by a factor of eight. Furthermore, the complete absence of light therapy in the book is unacceptable. Light therapy is one of the most effective tools for aligning the circadian rhythm, especially in high-latitude regions with limited winter sun. The author acknowledges the problem of insufficient light but offers no solution, ignoring a standard treatment for circadian disorders.

Ultimately, the book is a structural mess. While the final section covers promising research on how circadian rhythms influence cancer prevention and immunity, the evidence is consistently exaggerated and does not compensate for the misinformation found elsewhere. The author even contradicts himself, at one point claiming TRF is a guaranteed weight loss protocol and later admitting you might not lose weight at all. His language is often dangerously absolute, claiming that time-restricted eating is the "only way" to prevent obesity, which ignores the thousands of people who sustain weight loss using the exact opposite approach. Part of me hates this book because it is intellectually dishonest. Another part of me hates it on a personal level because, despite following a TRF protocol myself, I do not experience the miraculous benefits the author promises. I wish there were a magic bullet for health, but there simply isn’t.

Nevertheless, if your circadian rhythm is disrupted—which is likely in our modern lifestyle—it is worth fixing to improve your energy and mood. Since this book is a poor resource, I will offer the 90% solution here: wake up and go to bed at the same time every day, maximize blue light in the morning through sun exposure or light therapy, and minimize blue light two hours before bed. Establish a relaxing, consistent bedtime ritual, which is sleep hygiene 101 yet completely missed in the book. If you wish to optimize further, ensure your room is pitch-dark and cool, use white noise if necessary, and time your meals correctly. Eat close to your wake-up time, avoid large meals and alcohol before bed, and cut off caffeine 6 to 12 hours before sleep depending on your metabolism. That is all you need to know, without the exaggerated claims.
