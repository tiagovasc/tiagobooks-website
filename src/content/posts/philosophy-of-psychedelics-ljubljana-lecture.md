This was a lecture given in March 2026 at Bar Room Theory. You can watch the full recording below, along with a transcript.

The talk traces the history and science of psychedelics from their ancient use in shamanic traditions and the Eleusinian Mysteries, through the clinical research boom of the 1950s-60s, the government-driven prohibition in the 70s, and into the contemporary renaissance. This sets up the core empirical picture where psychedelics show striking results across depression, PTSD, addiction, and end-of-life anxiety, and people consistently rate these sessions among the most meaningful experiences of their lives, comparable to the birth of a child or a near-death experience.

It then moves into philosophy, arguing that the hyperreality of these states, their overlap with classical religious mysticism, and the depth of meaning people draw from them demand serious philosophical engagement rather than reductive explanation. The closing argument is a critique of the field's dominant secular-clinical model, which treats psychedelics as a tool for fixing pathology and returning people to baseline functionality. What's missing is what religious traditions have always provided: ritual, community, and a vision of human flourishing that points beyond mere normalcy. Without that context, the psychedelic renaissance risks becoming just another optimization mechanism.

## Part 1: Introduction

Welcome, everyone. It's a pleasure to have so many people. I thought maybe three people would show up, so it's good to see. My talk is on philosophy of psychedelics, a topic that I'm really interested in. I've been interested in psychedelics since I was 18, more or less, so well over a decade ago. As mentioned, I have a background in psychology and philosophy, and for my PhD I'm mostly researching philosophy of psychedelics. I end up exploring other fields as well, just because when you're studying psychedelics, you have to consider notions of what is real, more or less, and that eventually takes you outside that subject. But psychedelics is the main thing.

So it's going to be divided in three parts. Part one, I'll just give a quick intro to the topic in case some people are not very familiar with it. Part two, I'll cover the main topics within the field that I'm working with. And then in the last part, depending on how much time I have, I'll try to introduce a bit of my own perspective and what I think is missing in the field in general.

### What Are Psychedelics?

So what are psychedelics exactly? They're substances that profoundly alter consciousness: perception, cognition, emotion, and sense of self. A holistic definition like this is quite important because a lot of people, when they think about psychedelics, they think about funny things appearing in their perception, like the walls are breathing, trees are breathing, stuff like that. And that happens. But that's a very small part, in my opinion at least, of what makes these experiences meaningful. Basically everything about your own sense of self and your own sense of what the world is like changes.

There's a lot of different types of psychedelics. The most famous one is magic mushrooms, which contain psilocybin, the active compound. This actually grows here in Slovenia, although don't take the picture for it, you might need something else. But there's plenty of others as well: LSD, pretty famous; ayahuasca, which contains DMT; mescaline, which is not so popular nowadays. And then there's MDMA and ketamine, which are psychedelic-like, but in terms of neurobiology they're slightly different.

### Ancient and Indigenous Roots

A really good reason to start thinking about psychedelics, and why you maybe should pay a little bit more attention, is that they've been used more or less since forever, as far back as you can possibly go. So peyote is one example. Ayahuasca. Iboga. Soma is a little bit disputed, but in the Vedic texts there are probably references to it. It was probably used in the Eleusinian Mysteries. This is not proven, but there's a good amount of hints that it was. And this is actually quite important because it was a very important ritual in ancient Greece and it was very important for philosophers. It was basically a mystical initiation ritual. They took it pretty seriously. Everyone could participate, even slaves.

I think this is particularly relevant because if it was pretty influential for the Greek philosophers, that means it basically influenced philosophy, and philosophy influenced culture and society. And therefore, this is the start of Western civilization, more or less. And then shamanic traditions all over the world as well. So the takeaways are: very widespread and very ancient.

### Western Discovery

This was discovered in the beginning of the 20th century here in the West, by Hofmann. He first synthesized LSD and he actually tripped on accident, and then rode a bicycle home feeling all weirded out because he wasn't expecting to have that kind of experience. After that it was basically an explosion. After LSD was discovered, it started becoming very used in research and eventually therapy.

### The First Wave of Research

There were two types of therapy that were pretty common. One in Europe, one in America. They had two different perspectives. One was high dose, trying to achieve a mystical experience. The other was more therapeutic and slow. But the important takeaway is that this was really taken seriously and there was a lot of it. At one point, over a thousand research papers, 40,000 patients treated. Some nuance here is that the methodology back then wasn't as great as it should have been, but it's still something. And there was a lot of research on alcoholism, anxiety and terminal illness, which still continues nowadays. And also some other stuff that wasn't about psychiatry at all, like for example the impact on creativity.

### Counterculture and Prohibition

Then this was massively influential in the 60s, because all of this was in the context of a medical setting. But then in the 60s it got out of the lab and started being used recreationally. That had a massive impact on society and culture. Basically a lot of the culture we have today is inherited from the 60s. But that was also very bad for psychedelics to some degree, because it started being used very carelessly. It also started being very associated with counterculture, especially in the US, and was very associated with the Vietnam War. The US government really didn't like that, and that was one of the largest reasons why they were eventually banned. There was a war on drugs around the 70s. Research shut down because it was impossible to do. Not just consuming or holding the substances, but even doing research with them was absolutely forbidden. So basically all of this shut down overnight almost.

But there was still underground use with no science whatsoever. People were either using recreationally but also therapeutically. A lot of the therapists that were using it in the research, after psychedelics were banned, they were like, "Okay, this is still the best thing I found to treat my patients, so I'm going to use it anyway, even though it's illegal."

### The Scientific Renaissance

They got banned. Then around two, three decades, nothing really happened. Then research reopened. Strassman started doing some DMT studies in the 90s, but what people call the psychedelic renaissance really started around 2000. There started to be studies again on psilocybin and MDMA mostly. First very small, safety studies, but then more robust studies, and everything went pretty well. The results were quite impressive. They seemed to work, and they also worked really fast, which is really important. This actually made them receive FDA Breakthrough Therapy status. FDA is what deals with drug regulation in the US, and specifically for PTSD because it's really hard to treat. MDMA was really promising. Now, unfortunately, these studies didn't quite pan out for a few different reasons, but it really helped kickstart this whole renaissance. And that's why psychedelics are so popular and known nowadays, and much more accepted compared to 10, 20 years ago.

### Clinical Evidence

Their clinical applicability covers a lot of things, but these are the main four: depression, PTSD and all kinds of trauma, addiction, and anxiety and end of life. The important thing to note is that some of these results are really impressive. For example, 70% eventually no longer meeting the diagnostic criteria. By the way, this is all psychedelic therapy. That's also important to know. It's not like people are just randomly giving the substance and then they trip on their own. This is always within the context of research where there's a therapist and everything is done quite carefully. But the results are really impressive. 70% is crazy.

For example, also on addiction, one of the trials had 80% cessation of smoking. That is crazy. A lot of the reason why psychedelics are so impressive is because alternative treatments to help people with these things don't get anywhere close. For example, there's no treatment whatsoever that we have that can even get to 50% cessation of smoking. It just doesn't exist. These things are quite promising.

Then there's also lots of other research, but it's more new and not as established. Eating disorders, OCD, autism spectrum disorder, cluster headaches, and plenty of other stuff.

### Beyond the Clinic

There's also some interesting research beyond the clinic, in healthy volunteers. The most important thing to note is that people rate these sessions among the most meaningful experiences of their life. And they last. Typically they think of it as on the same level as getting married or watching their firstborn child being born.

So it's very, very significant. That first study I mentioned there was done with divinity students, and there was actually a follow-up study, I think 20 or 30 years later, which is a very long time of follow-up. The subjects, all of them, still rated the experience that they had 20 to 30 years ago as one of the most meaningful of their lives.

There's also a nice bit of research about the synergy with spiritual practices, like meditation and so forth. And when we investigate the naturalistic use, like outside research, just what people do on their own, there's also plenty of interesting outcomes, like reductions in anxiety, cognitive flexibility, and wellbeing.

### William James' Framework

So what is the experience actually like? William James, a very famous philosopher, provided the basic framework for this. He was talking about mystical experiences, not psychedelic experiences. But an important point is that they're more or less almost the same thing.

Four big factors. Ineffability: you can't really describe it. Noetic quality: it feels like you actually gained some knowledge that is true. Transiency: they also pass very quickly, they don't last. And passivity: it feels like they happen to you, you don't have any control. On psychedelics, obviously this changes a little bit, at least in the sense that you decide to take psychedelics, hopefully. But in the original context of mystical experiences, not really.

### The Mystical Experience Questionnaire

William James really influenced Stace, another philosopher. And then based on the work of Stace, the Mystical Experience Questionnaire was created. This is basically a good way to organize the experience of psychedelics. Very similar themes as we've seen, but this one is statistically derived: mystical unity, positive mood, transcendence, and ineffability. The way this works is you ask people a bunch of questions, and then through the statistical correlations between questions you can derive certain factors and extract the major themes of whatever you're studying.

### Set and Setting

Now a really curious thing about these experiences is that they're very context dependent. Set and setting is a pretty common concept in psychedelic experiences. What this means is that your mindset and your environment matter a lot. A lot. This is not just some drug that you take and it has a very predictable effect no matter what. It's not like paracetamol where it's going to heal you regardless. It's not like that at all. And so that is a good hint that maybe biology is a bit reductionist if that's all you're looking at to explain the experience.

### Neuroscience Highlights

In terms of neuroscience, which is a lot of what the psychedelic renaissance has been focusing on, there's a lot of work, but I tried to summarize it here. Global connectivity: your brain talks in a way that is a bit unusual. There's more parts of your brain communicating with each other. This is a very famous image. Maybe you've seen it on the media or something. This is your sober brain. And this is when you're on psychedelics. Everything is talking to each other basically.

Then default mode network suppression. The default mode network is very largely responsible for your sense of self and also a lot of the rumination that people do. For example, people that have depression really have heightened activity in the default mode network. Then there's also neuroplasticity, so the brain rewires itself a little bit. A lot of it is animal studies, but nevertheless there are hints that this is happening. And also increased entropy, which is basically a richer brain state that is less predictable.

### Key Findings and Predictors

Some other key findings that I think are really important to know about psychedelics. The fact that you have a mystical experience, which is not guaranteed if you take psychedelics but is very likely especially with a high dose, actually seems to predict the outcomes. That's very important to note. Also, dose is the strongest predictor of the experience itself, the intensity, which then becomes really relevant for the first point because it's like, if you didn't get better, maybe the dose just wasn't high enough.

Also, trait absorption is the strongest personality predictor, which is really important if you think about receptivity. If you go there closed-minded or very anxious and you're not willing to let go, then it's probably not going to work as well. And as we've seen, set and setting is pretty important. Also, psychological insight and emotional breakthroughs seem to play a really big role as well.

### Lasting Changes

Some of the changes that people have: increased meaning in life, reduced fear of death, enhanced nature connection, prosociality, openness. Openness here is a technical term in psychology literature, but you can think of openness in general. Good enough. Awe, wonder, gratitude, and then altered metaphysical beliefs, which is very relevant for philosophy of psychedelics, which we're going to get into right now.

## Part 2: Philosophy of Psychedelics

This part I'm going to give a very high-level overview. This can get pretty technical, but I'll do my best to give a very wide scope of it.

### Why Philosophy?

First of all, why should people care about psychedelics in the sense of them being relevant for philosophy? Why don't philosophers just ignore this completely? I think these are the arguments that to me are the most convincing.

**Hyperreality.** These states feel more real than real. That is insane. If you think about it, that makes no sense whatsoever. The fact that people claim this should pique your curiosity. And something very peculiar about this type of claim is that there are also other situations in your life where you might have an altered state of consciousness and you think it's real, but then when it ends you recognize that it wasn't. For example, if you're having delusions, you have really high fever, or when you're dreaming. When you're dreaming you think it's real, but then you wake up and you're like, "Oh, actually that wasn't real at all." You use the cues of what explains the most out of your experience in waking life, and then you make a retroactive judgment of, "Actually, that wasn't real." That's not the case with psychedelics. With psychedelics, people say, "This was more real than real." And then tomorrow they're sober and they still tell you, "This was real." And then if you ask a year later, they're still going to tell you that it's real. In my opinion, this is a pretty good argument to take that seriously. How you interpret it is a different story, but at least take it seriously.

**Meaning as Mechanism.** The fact that it's meaningful and the type of meaning that it contains is relevant. The notion of set and setting suggests this is not just biology, it's not just neuroscience. That's way too simple. Reality is way more complex than that.

**Universal Themes.** The fact that the experiences are very coherent with what human beings tend to value the most. Notions of gods, of reality, of the universe, of self, of meaning in life. It seems a pretty big coincidence that the things that matter the most to human beings just happen to be the things that occur in psychedelics.

**Congruence with Mysticism.** Everything is very close to mysticism as well, because the experiences are largely the same. If you give a psychedelic experience that ends up being a mystical experience and an original mystical experience, like a religious one from hundreds of years ago, people can't tell them apart if they're rating it blindly. They're more or less the same thing. And given that religion has had such a large impact on society, you should take that seriously as well.

**Directional Value Alignment.** The fact that they reliably have a positive impact on people's lives. If you think it was a drug that just makes random noise in your brain, then the direction that it could go could be anywhere. So why does it always seem to be good, at least on average? That's very odd. That should make you think a little bit.

**Redefining Rationality.** Being rational is not only about correct propositions. A lot of the reason you should think this way is because clearly psychedelics work through at least some way of reducing self-deception. This is a bit of a mouthful and hard to explain, but I just put it here and hopefully it will make a little bit more sense by the end of the talk.

### The Comforting Delusion Objection

Now, some of the common themes of philosophy of psychedelics. One is the comforting delusion objection. It goes like this: if psychedelics provide benefits, but they depend on false beliefs, then how can you justify that benefit? For example, if during a psychedelic experience you end up believing that there are some green elves that rule the world, but they're really good elves and they're going to take care of you all the way and reduce all your suffering, maybe you're not depressed anymore. Maybe it worked. But it's not true. Green elves don't actually rule the world. And so that becomes a problem, because it has a benefit, but that's just because you're believing things that are not true.

However, this does depend on you having a certain type of view. For example, you do have to make the claim that green elves don't exist, and that's relatively easy in this case. But there are some other cases where it's not so easy. Here I name it as naturalism, which is mostly the term that people use. Generally speaking, what that means is assuming that the world works in the way that science can describe, the natural world. That's what we consider as real. Therefore, if something goes outside of it, then it's not real, and hence this whole problem.

A really good, really famous philosopher, Chris Letheby, argues that the way to get out of this is because the benefits do not come from the metaphysical belief. Although this is hard to argue, there's a lot of nuance. But he argues that they arise by changing your model of the self. And therefore that's how you overcome this problem.

### Can Consciousness Be Selfless?

Another problem is about the notion of the self, related to self-consciousness. The issue is that it's not exactly clear where the border between consciousness and your sense of self lies, in the sense that there's a reflexive element to it that you're aware of. This is a problem in philosophy in general. People debate this back and forth.

But this is relevant to psychedelics because your sense of self disappears. It's completely annihilated. That's very interesting. First of all, how exactly does that happen? Some people call it ego death. That's a very common term. There's a dissolution. But then also people very often mention that there's a kind of unity with everything. And that implies an expansion. So maybe they're the same thing, or maybe not. There are different ways to think about this.

And then there's also the other problem: if there was no self, then how do you remember the experience? Who is experiencing what you're describing to me? It's very odd and very tricky. One popular way to think about it is that this notion of self-consciousness, maybe it's not just one thing, maybe there are several things. It can have an element of narrative, an element of body, an element of perspective. And then during the psychedelic experience, different things get taken away from it.

### The Self-Binding Account

Another approach is that basically your sense of self is a construction of your brain in order to unify all of experience. And when you have this ego dissolution, that model that your brain has built breaks apart. And that's what explains a bit of the experience.

But then there's a problem of how you take that construction to be real. Is there truly a self? What is actually the level of reality of that supposed construction? And then what is happening during the psychedelic experience? And how does that relate to how the self functions in the way that it seems to be the locus, the center, of your entire experience?

### Can Altered States Yield Knowledge?

Another one: can knowledge be gained during psychedelic experiences? Again, this is relevant because the type of knowledge that you're gaining can be false, as we've seen. But people do seem to be gaining something. When people speak, they try to describe these experiences in terms of having gained some sort of insight, but it's not quite clear what that insight was.

Here's the issue: this relates to the false beliefs I mentioned, for example the elves. If I claim that now I know that there are green elves running the universe, that is a claim about the external world, and that invites a lot of questions. But maybe there are other types of knowledge that can be gained during psychedelic experiences that don't rely on claims about the external world. For example:

- **Self-knowledge:** seeing patterns in your thinking that were previously invisible.

- **Knowledge by acquaintance:** I now know what it is like to experience something which I didn't before, even though it's not a proposition per se.

- **Modal knowledge:** there's a mode of experience that I've now experienced, showing my way of being a self is one possibility among others.

### How Does the Therapy Work?

There's also a lot of debate on how exactly the therapy works. Obviously neuroscience can help us here, but you still have to have an interpretive lens.

A very popular approach is that it's basically about representations of the self that get altered. Another is more about emotions. Another also claims that it's basically pure neuroplasticity and just a rewiring of the brain, which is very reductive. But some people have tried to claim that.

### Naturalism vs. Metaphysical Openness

Then there's this big notion of naturalism. We can think of it in less technical terms as a scientific worldview, versus some kind of metaphysical speculation. Metaphysics is a technical term of philosophy that just means the field that asks: what is real? What is reality, exactly?

One approach says we know what is real: it's science. Therefore, if something happens that doesn't quite fit a scientific worldview, then it's not real. A lot of people argue this.

Another approach says the psychedelic experience can actually give you hints about reality. A lot of philosophers try to argue for idealism or panpsychism, which are positions in philosophy that say consciousness has a primary ontology. Ontology is about what is real. So reality at the fundamental level has consciousness in it. Idealism means that there's only consciousness. Panpsychism means consciousness and matter are the same thing. Those sound crazy to people who aren't into philosophy, but if you look into it, they're not as crazy as you might think because they explain a lot of stuff that you otherwise struggle a little bit to explain. Some people think that psychedelic experiences can be evidence for those types of philosophical views.

Another view says there are basically multiple frameworks, and just because idealism is true, that doesn't mean it's unscientific.

And then also another one, which I think is actually quite smart and well put, is basically that when you have psychedelic experiences, you can take them as evidence for a particular metaphysical view, but it's not that just because you experience becoming one with the universe, that means some theory is correct. It's just one piece of evidence among all the other evidence. And it's something that you can revise, and that you can argue based on other arguments and other evidence that have nothing to do with psychedelics. So a lot more flexible in general.

### The Interpretation Problem

Another problem: because these experiences have such a mystical dimension, how do you interpret that mysticism exactly? A Buddhist might see emptiness at the peak of the experience when they feel one with everything or feel that they are nothing, notions which are very, very close together. A Christian might see God. A Hindu might see Brahman. What exactly do we make of this? Is this all the same thing or is it different?

This is usually called in the literature perennialism versus constructivism. Are all those experiences the same at the core at some level? Or does everyone just construct the experience based on what they bring to the table, their culture and their upbringing? And it's the exact same debate that is had in the philosophy of religion with normal mystical experiences that have nothing to do with psychedelics.

### Ethics

Then there is a lot of work on ethics as well.

**Informed Consent.** One of the problems is that if these experiences can alter you so much, for example if you were to sign up for a study and they were trying to get your consent, can you really consent if you don't know what you're going to experience and if the experience is going to be pretty extreme? Because you might think there are certain aspects that are appealing. But the problem is that as you undergo the experience, your actual value system changes because your self changes. So you can't possibly make the judgment of whether this is something you actually want to undergo or not. Although this actually applies to many things. L.A. Paul in her book _Transformative Experiences_ makes this claim for lots of other things, like even becoming a parent. You can't really research exhaustively what it's like to be a parent. You can make an informed guess, but at the end of the day you don't know. You just have to take the leap of faith and then hopefully it doesn't go too badly.

**Moral Enhancement.** We've seen that psychedelics increase gratitude and prosocial attitudes. If that's the case, you can make the claim that morality in a given person can be enhanced with psychedelics. But obviously that's problematic: how exactly you track that, how ethical it is, and also the political implications. There's a whole can of worms there.

**Doxastic Vulnerability.** Also the notion of vulnerability of the patients themselves, because the experiences are so context dependent. The structure of the sessions and the therapists have a lot of power over the subject, and that's really problematic for power dynamics.

**Decolonization.** And also notions of colonization, because for the most part Western medicine extracted these compounds from indigenous cultures. There's a lot of shady stuff going on, and there's cultural appropriation. We're also reducing a whole set of practices and culture that they had, which we're now basically just putting in a pill and giving to people.

## Part 3: Religion and Psychedelics

So those are a few of the major problems in the field. Now I'm going to talk a little bit about what I'm most interested in, which fits into that whole debate. That's mostly about religion. There's other stuff, but I chose to focus on this the most, which is what I tend to emphasize the most.

### Two Poles and a Middle Path

As we've seen, there are a lot of problems in philosophy of psychedelics. But a helpful simple framework is that there are two poles. One pole is that you take naturalism for granted. The scientific worldview is true, that's all there is to it. Anything within psychedelics is basically just alterations of your sense of self in your brain. And religion doesn't matter because, since you take naturalism for granted, religion is just these supernatural claims that we don't think are true, and that's the end of it.

Then there's the other pole, which is metaphysics: you take psychedelics as evidence that there's something beyond the scientific worldview, like for example that consciousness is inherent in reality in some way.

A lot of the field goes into these two camps. But I think there is a middle path that a lot of philosophers are missing. Going the metaphysics route is maybe a little bit extreme, a little bit speculative. Maybe we can take something more conservative, a little easier to make compatible with what we know from science, but also at the same time, maybe reducing everything to biology and having an overconfidence in what you think is real is not a good thing either.

And neither pole engages with religion, because one doesn't believe in any of it, and the other maybe believes in the metaphysics but thinks that the way religion frames it is wrong. I think both of these are misguided.

### 4E Cognitive Science

Now, to explain why this matters, it's important to know a little bit about cognitive science, 4E cognitive science more particularly, which comes from four factors.

**Embodied.** Your body matters. You're not just a brain in a vat. This is a bit hard to explain, but the takeaway is that the fact that you're an agent in the world with a body matters a lot. That's also why artificial intelligence isn't actually intelligence, one large reason being that AI is not embodied in a real world.

**Embedded.** Your environment matters. You're not this decontextualized agent in the middle of nowhere.

**Enactive.** You're an active agent. You're always participating in the world. You're not just passive.

**Extended.** The environment, tools, or technology are embedded into cognition. Cell phones are the most straightforward example nowadays. But for example, even if you're using a calculator to do math, the actual calculator is part of the cognition that you're using in order to do that particular mathematical problem.

A key takeaway of all this is that it all matters for knowledge. Knowledge is vastly broader than just holding particular beliefs in your brain. And this will be important for how we conceptualize religion.

### Four Ways of Knowing

Building from that, there's this really nice model by John Vervaeke, which he calls the four ways of knowing, the four Ps. Vervaeke is my supervisor, so I'm a bit biased, but I think this is quite nice.

There's a **propositional** level, which is about facts of the world, like "this remote is gray" or whatever color it is. There's **procedural** , which is about skills: knowing how to do something, like ride a bicycle. **Perspectival** , which is the fact that if you're sitting on the chair, you're looking at things from a particular perspective that's inherent to you and where you are right now. And also **participatory** , meaning that you're an agent in the world and you're negotiating with your environment. Identity shaping has a lot to do with this notion that your life is enacted, that you're an agent in the world who can do things. But this is also the hardest to understand.

Just as an example to make this a little bit more concrete: rock climbing. If you're bouldering in a gym, there are different colors for what you can or can't do. You might say, "I know I can do the yellows but not the reds." That's just a propositional fact of the game you're playing. But there's also a procedural level: you need to know how to move your body in a particular way, and that's not about beliefs. You can't spell it out. You literally can't. There's also a perspectival element: you need to know the perspective from a particular angle so that you know how to move. And also the fact that you're participating in the act of climbing. At the highest levels, you're fully coupled to the wall. One way to think about it is the famous flow states that people get. You're perfectly attuned to whatever you're doing.

### Beyond the Propositional

The reason why this is all relevant is because I think a really important conclusion is that psychedelic insights are not about new factual propositions. As we've seen, the propositional level is only a very small part of what's going on. It's very much about identity and perspective: how you see the world and who you think you are as a person, as an agent.

### Quantum Leap

Obviously psychedelics are a pretty drastic experience, but there are also a lot of other things that are drastic in this qualitative way. For example, when I was younger I was a fitness coach, and I helped a lot of people eat better and get stronger in the gym. Generally speaking, the typical advice is that you build habits very slowly: you eat a little bit better, and slowly over time you eventually get to wherever you want to go. And this works great. This is a large part of what being a coach is. But it doesn't always work. A lot of people get stuck, and it's very hard for them to actually get anywhere. Part of the job of the coach is to try to make that easier so they can stick with trying new things.

But I also found that some people just cannot be helped, no matter what. The reason is that they always reversed that step. They take a little step forward, and then a little step back, and they spend their lives like this. And the way to get out of that is that you actually have to make a commitment. You have to actually decide, "I'm going to be a different kind of person." That's the only way to cross to the other side. It's a qualitative leap, not just incremental steps.

Something quite interesting is that you see this also in addiction. If you smoke, it's very hard to take gradual steps to stop smoking. That's just not how it works. People who stop smoking, almost always, somehow manage to make this decision, and then they're able to commit to it. It's an overnight thing. Same with other types of addictions.

I also have here Alcoholics Anonymous, which was an organization that grew in the US for alcoholics, obviously, and was pretty influential at the time. They managed to help a lot of people, because alcoholism is very hard to cure, and they had a pretty good success rate. One of the reasons was that the system they implemented was very religious in a sense. It implemented a lot of habits and structure that was about becoming a new person. Again, this qualitative leap. They weren't just having a pep talk, "Oh, you need to drink less." No, it's, "You're going to be a new person and there is no going back." And that's the only way you can possibly do it.

You also see this in traditional rites of passage. Cultures basically everywhere, the goal is to establish a new identity. And again, it's a leap, not a gradual progression.

### Why Religion?

Now, why care about religion? I would say that the reason why not caring about religion is the default answer is because in modernity, post the scientific revolution, we rejected religion because we thought it was making certain types of propositional claims, like about the origin of the universe or something else. And based on our scientific understanding, we decided actually that's not correct, or we don't need that level of explanation, therefore we can ignore religion.

But as we've seen, the level of proposition is a very small part of your cognition and what allows you to know things and to act in the world. Given that that criticism is no longer valid, in my opinion, then maybe you should re-evaluate whether you take religion seriously or not. And given that it was such a large force in the world and is so widespread across the globe, I think that's a pretty good argument to take it seriously.

Psychedelics have this power to reorient you in life. Again, it's this leap. And this has a lot of commonalities with religion. One of the ways you can see it, though this is a huge topic, is that the language they use reflects a lot of this quantum leap we've been talking about.

### Metaphors

For example, here are a few metaphors from Christianity: "You were dead in your sins, but God made you alive with Christ." "Unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." "He has called you out of darkness into his marvelous light." "If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old has passed away. The new has come." A lot of metaphors about being born again. And also a lot of metaphors of being always dead and this is the first time you're actually being alive.

In Eastern traditions there's a similar kind of emphasis, although usually a slightly different metaphor: instead of being born again or going from dead to alive, it's more about going from an asleep state to an awake state. A very famous passage: someone asks the Buddha, "Are you a God?" "No." "Are you a man?" "No." "Then what are you?" "I am awake." You see this all over the place. Hinduism has similar metaphors as well. They're very well connected. One of the reasons you can make sense of this is that they're both trying to highlight a differentiation in states of being where one is higher than the other. When you're awake, you're more in contact with reality than when you were sleeping.

### Crucial Differences

But a difference that is quite relevant is that in psychedelics, even though it has all this overlap, it's mostly a one-time thing. You have an experience and then that's it. At least in the modern context. Historically it wasn't like this; it was part of rituals that were continuous. But in religion, even though it has all these commonalities, it's more about having a daily practice, something you embody and something that you're trying to get better at and retain for the rest of your life.

### The Integration Problem

In psychedelics there's this problem of integration. People have these wonderful experiences, or at least powerful ones. And they might even have some benefit from it, especially if they're struggling with trauma or depression or addiction. But then what happens after? People struggle to integrate, number one, just because the experience is so weird. They don't know what's real anymore and they really struggle to make sense of it. And eventually it fades away. Psychedelics are impressive because they work well and the results last, but they don't last forever.

For example, even one year of cessation from smoking is crazy impressive. But it still sucks that at the end of the one year, most people are still going to be where they were when they started. Integration is this process of trying to integrate the experience and maintain these types of benefits. We know it's pretty important because it correlates with positive outcomes. But there are tons of problems. There's no single model for what constitutes integration. Everyone is guessing and winging it. To the degree that there's a pattern, it's very medical-based. So again, in the context of research with a therapist, they basically try to continue that but without psychedelics. A lot of talk with the therapist. Which is fine, but I think it's reductionist. I think there's a lot missing.

### Ritual as the Answer

I think one of the most important things is connecting it to the difference with religion. Religion has a lot of rituals. And rituals are a way of embodying certain ways of being that you want to maintain. Because, remember, cognition is embodied. It's not just abstract beliefs. When a Buddhist is meditating, they're not thinking about the deep metaphysics of the Buddhist cosmology. They're practicing something in order to become a certain type of person.

So that's why ritual is so crucial for religion. But this doesn't quite exist, at least in a standardized mainstream form, within psychedelic therapy. Meditation gets close. There are people trying to integrate it, but it's still very fuzzy. So I think this is very important, and I think ritual is a lot of what is missing in order to maintain the insights of psychedelics and maintain the benefits so that you don't go back into old problems.

Not only do you need ritual, but if you think about ritual, that can be an individual thing. But in religion, again and again, you always have to think back to religion because that's what people have always done. People weren't just meditating at home with no contact whatsoever. They had the help of their community. They have shrines, temples, all kinds of things. Here in the West, we have churches, we have mass. It's a community thing. It's not just you. And in psychedelics, none of this exists. It's just a pill you take, one on one, that's it. You go home and hopefully you get better.

Also, indigenous traditions really never separated the substance from the religious and worldview context, as we're trying to do now. And religions are very complex cultural systems that are very interconnected. One of the problems is that even when people try to take ritual a bit more seriously, they try to take one ritual and kind of use it outside the context. For example, meditation. It's like, "None of what Buddhists are trying to claim is true, but they have this practice of meditation that seems kind of cool. Let's just take it out and do something with it." I don't think that works, for a million reasons. The basic point is that they're complex and interconnected. You can't just abstract that out. And that's why I think a purely secular approach to integration and to psychedelics is a mistake.

### Commodification

There's also a big problem with psychedelics and commodification. I think having this religious dimension to psychedelics, taking it seriously and thinking of it as a sacred thing, can avoid some of these problems.

**Microdosing as Performance Tool.** You see a lot of Silicon Valley types, tech bros who really love microdosing, but they do it as a performance thing. "I'm just going to be better at my job and that's it." There's no more mysticism of any kind. No more transformation. You're just using the drug to get better at making money, basically.

**The Retreat and Therapy Market.** These things are starting to get monetized. There are retreats popping up which are a bit shady, always money involved, completely decontextualizing the entire experience. They do whatever they want, basically.

**Pathology Model.** A really big problem in my opinion is that psychedelic practice and research today mostly assume a pathology model. Someone has depression, trauma, some particular problem. You take this treatment that happens to be psychedelic, and then you get better, you get to baseline, and that's it. Now you're okay, you're healthy again, go home, go back to work. I think that's really, really dangerous and a flattening of human experience. If we come back to religion, how people lived, this model is insane. People in the West and everywhere, they weren't trying to be average. They weren't trying to not be miserable. At least within a religious context, they were trying to be as close to God as they could. Or they were trying to become saints in some way. The Buddhists are trying to become a Buddha. And so on. There's always this notion that there's something transcendent which you're aiming for, and all cultures in their own ways are trying to achieve this to some degree. But if we keep with the pathology model, you're basically cutting that off. It's just, "You need to be functional enough to work and make money for other people." I think this is very dangerous.

**Psychopolitics.** And then psychopolitics, this notion that psychedelics are now starting to be used, and they can increasingly be used if we don't re-evaluate how we talk about these things, as just an optimization mechanism. "I'm just changing my own self in order to be better for a particular goal." This connects very much to the microdosing phenomenon. But this also applies even if the mystical experience was present. For example, maybe we try for someone to have a mystical experience, but with the goal of better labor, better performance, and this constant notion of modifying yourself for the sake of the market.
