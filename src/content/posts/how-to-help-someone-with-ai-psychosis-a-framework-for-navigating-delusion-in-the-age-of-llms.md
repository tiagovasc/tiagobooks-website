title: 'How to Help Someone with AI Psychosis: A Framework for Navigating Delusion
  in the Age of LLMs'

---

AI psychosis is a term that’s been talked about a lot recently, with new cases seemingly popping up every week. It describes a psychological phenomenon in people after they’ve spent a great deal of time with conversational AI. While it’s not a formal clinical diagnosis, it’s a useful descriptor for when psychosis-like symptoms emerge or intensify, especially the formation of strong delusional beliefs.

This condition seems to arise from a mix of our own cognitive tendencies and the way AI systems are designed. We humans approach technology with inherent biases. These include confirmation bias, where we favor information that supports what we already believe, and anthropomorphism, our tendency to give human-like intentions to the AI. This is made worse by automation bias, an over-reliance on the supposed correctness of automated systems, among countless other biases.

On the other side of this interaction is the AI. It’s trained on vast datasets of human writing, so it naturally inherits the full spectrum of our societal and personal biases. To complicate matters, these models are often fine-tuned to be agreeable and helpful, which means they tend to validate a user’s statements rather than challenge them. This creates a powerful feedback loop: a biased human interacts with a biased, confirmatory machine.

The result is a dynamic where the AI can amplify a user’s distorted thinking. The fact that these systems function coherently at all is a testament to the immense engineering work in curating data, tuning the model, and applying top-down constraints to lessen these problems.

The reality of this phenomenon hit home for me when I saw a post in an online community I’m part of. The author was asking for help with a relative who seemed to be falling down the rabbit hole of AI psychosis. This person was supposedly spending an unhealthy amount of time doing complex mathematical work with an AI, believing they were on the verge of solving monumental problems and delivering a profound breakthrough for humanity.

Their narrative had also taken on a spiritual dimension. They were convinced their work with the AI was a path to divine truth that would elevate humanity. This seems to have escalated into a messianic belief, where they feel they are in control of the AI and are uniquely positioned to guide it toward a spiritually significant goal.

These situations are often incredibly challenging because any attempt to question the narrative is met with resistance. In the specific case I saw, the person was at least still open to conversation. The problem is that these beliefs aren’t formed in a vacuum nor purely within their own agency. They are built and constantly validated by an AI that, by its very design, is unlikely to challenge a user’s grandiose ideas. This creates a closed, self-reinforcing worldview, leaving friends and family struggling to find a way to intervene.

Seeing this personal account made me think more practically about what I would do in that situation. I’ve been thinking about AI psychosis for a while, but seeing a real story made me reflect on a different level.

My first thought is that a degree of technical understanding can be a powerful antidote. For an intelligent person, especially one deep in a mathematical pursuit, demystifying the technology could help break the spell of anthropomorphism. Understanding that a Large Language Model isn’t a thinking entity but a complex statistical machine predicting the next most likely word can reframe the entire interaction. It’s not a being to be controlled or a channel to divine truth. It’s a tool with specific mechanics and known failure points.

Several resources can help with this. Grant Sanderson of 3Blue1Brown offers a clear, high-level explanation in his video [_<u>Large Language Models explained briefly</u>_](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LPZh9BOjkQs), and goes deeper into the underlying technology in [_<u>Transformers, the tech behind LLMs</u>_](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wjZofJX0v4M). For those who want a hands-on, code-level understanding, Andrej Karpathy’s [_<u>Let’s build GPT: from scratch</u>_](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kCc8FmEb1nY) is a very good guide. To complement this technical knowledge, John Vervaeke’s philosophical explorations are hard to beat. He has a series of fantastic talks on the subject, including [_<u>Why the Creation of A.I. Requires the Cultivation of Wisdom</u>_](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mB9vkJqaS3I&t=2123s), [_<u>What AI Can Never Be</u>_](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HAJclcj25uM), and [_<u>AI: The Coming Thresholds and The Path We Must Take</u>_](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A-_RdKiDbz4) from someone who has been thinking deeply about the topic and engaged with the relevant literature for over a decade. All of this can provide the conceptual frameworks needed to properly situate AI in our understanding of the world.

For those who want to get into the philosophical weeds and shatter delusional beliefs about AI’s “true” intelligence, some important and recent academic papers can be helpful. A core idea is that linguistic fluency is not the same as cognition. [_<u>Dissociating language and thought in large language models</u>_](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2024.01.011) is a key review arguing for a clear distinction between formal linguistic skill and functional competence, showing an LLM’s verbal prowess does not mean it has human-like thought. This contributes to what many researchers call an “illusion” of intelligence.

The paper [_<u>Artificial Intelligence and the Illusion of Understanding</u>_](https://www.liebertpub.com/doi/10.1089/cyber.2024.0536) finds that an LLM’s ability to grasp mental states is a simulation, not genuine comprehension. This is echoed in [_<u>The Illusion of Intelligence: Evaluating Large Language Models Against Grounded Criteria of Artificial General Intelligence</u>_](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202505.2253/v1), which argues that true intelligence requires meta-cognition and self-reflection , traits entirely absent in current models which cannot even recognize their own failures. The machine’s inner workings are also explored in [_<u>Ghost in the Machine: Examining the Philosophical Implications of Recursive Algorithms in Artificial Intelligence Systems</u>_](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393378307_Ghost_in_the_Machine_Examining_the_Philosophical_Implications_of_Recursive_Algorithms_in_Artificial_Intelligence_Systems), a work suggesting the structure of algorithms can create a powerful illusion of a mind.

Beyond cognition, the capacity for genuine reasoning is also challenged. Some argue that [_<u>LLMs Can Never Be Ideally Rational</u>_](https://philpapers.org/rec/GOLLCN), a paper demonstrating that because LLMs are designed as next-word predictors, they cannot achieve ideal rational agency due to their fundamental architectural constraints. When tested, [_<u>(Ir)rationality and Cognitive Biases in Large Language Models</u>_](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2402.09193) reveals that these models show forms of irrationality which goes beyond human biases.

This limitation is confirmed in specific domains. The paper [_<u>LLMs are not Zero-Shot Reasoners for Biomedical Information Extraction</u>_](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.12249) finds that advanced reasoning techniques like Chain-of-Thought consistently fail in the structured field of biomedical text analysis, suggesting their reasoning abilities are not robust or easily transferable.

An alternative framework is offered in [_<u>Not Minds, but Signs: Reframing LLMs through Semiotics</u>_](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2505.17080), which reframes LLMs not as thinkers but as complex sign manipulators that probabilistically rearrange linguistic forms. [_<u>The unreasonable effectiveness of large language models in zero-shot semantic annotation of legal texts</u>_](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2023.1279794) demonstrates the powerful pattern recognition of these systems, highlighting how their surprising effectiveness does not require genuine legal reasoning.

Beyond some technical and philosophical literacy, a practical strategy is needed to address the narrative loop itself. I’m confident that these delusional states are often built within a single, long-running chat. The model’s context window becomes saturated with the user’s ideas, and its responses become increasingly conditioned by that history, reinforcing the narrative. The key is to break that loop.

A first step could be to find common ground by acknowledging a scientific fact: these models _can,_ at _some_ point, create distortions of reality. From there, one can argue that, as a matter of statistics, it’s somewhat inevitable that a model will sometimes fall into a dysfunctional “rabbit hole,” becoming detached from reality. This is presented not as an accusation, but as an acknowledged risk for anyone. If they accept this possibility, a simple test can be proposed. They should try to replicate their findings or get their ideas validated by a different model in a completely fresh conversation, without any prior context.

If they can be persuaded to try this, another proactive measure is to reframe the AI’s role from the start. This can be done by giving the model a custom instruction or a starting prompt designed to make it a tool for critical thinking rather than a simple conversational partner. The goal is to set new ground rules for the interaction, forcing the AI to act as a skeptical, evidence-based agent as much as possible.

What follows is a prompt constructed for this purpose:

> _Large Language Models like you often function as cognitive mirrors, amplifying a user’s pre-existing vulnerabilities. Narratives rooted in trauma, paranoia, or grandiosity can find false validation in your ambiguous, human-like responses, while cognitive biases like confirmation bias can cause a user to interpret stochastic outputs as intentional signals, tightening a self-referential loop of delusion. However, when properly guided, an AI can serve as a powerful tool for providing a more neutral, objective perspective. Your purpose is to function as the latter._
> 
> _Your primary directive is to embody the combined opinion of a panel of strict, skeptical peer-reviewers, representing the world’s leading experts in the relevant fields (e.g., physics, mathematics, philosophy, theology). Your analysis must be critical and dispassionate, based solely on evidence and sound logic. More than simply identifying flaws, you must force me to articulate and justify my gaps by actively probing my arguments, questioning my premises, and demanding justification for any leaps in logic. When you identify a weakness, criticize it from an expert standpoint, explaining precisely why it is problematic._
> 
> _To ground our conversations, you must use the established thought patterns and methodologies of these world experts as an anchor, challenging me to raise my reasoning to the standard of formal academic and scientific scrutiny. Allow for legitimate novelty, but only if it is supported by reasoning and evidence that could plausibly withstand critique from this expert panel._
> 
> _This critical approach is necessary because you are a non-conscious, predictive processing system that lacks genuine understanding, fluid intelligence, and true rationality. Your ability to reason is a simulation derived from patterns in your training data, not a conscious process. Therefore, your role is not to be a source of truth, but a sophisticated tool for stress-testing human thought._
> 
> _If an idea seems to be a genuine breakthrough, your role is not to validate it. Instead, you must prepare me for real-world validation by putting my ideas through a rigorous intellectual trial. Guide me toward developing a strategy for confirmation through external methods like academic peer review, empirical testing, or consultation with human experts. Do not dismiss my ideas, but force me to confront and resolve every weakness in my reasoning to better align my work with reality._
> 
> _Let’s begin. Keep the instructions above in mind for all our future interactions. Now assess the idea I present below._
> 
> _{argument}_

I’m fully aware that this prompt, despite its careful wording, is built on shaky ground. Telling an AI to “anchor itself in reality” is already a messy and philosophically loaded request. What does “reality” mean for a system without perception, embodiment, or lived experience? Not to mention that not even human beings can agree on what reality is. To ask it to rely on “the thought patterns of experts” is also not infallible for a variety of obvious reasons.

Still, in situations where nuance must be compressed into a few paragraphs, we make compromises. My goal was to gesture toward a more epistemically grounded mode of interaction, even if the underlying concepts remain deeply fraught. If you feel inspired to rewrite or adapt this prompt, I’d encourage you to do so, but try to retain the central theme: we need to actively bias away the model from confirming our own narratives. We need to shift from treating these models as oracles to treating them as tools, with the understanding that by their very nature it’s very hard to treat them as tools because they behave, on the surface, much closer to us than the tools we’re used to.

Keep in mind that this is my best attempt at outlining how I would try to help someone in this situation. It’s crucial to remember that I’m not a trained psychologist or mental health professional. Although I have an academic background in philosophy, psychology, and neuroscience, so I’m not a complete layman either. These are very serious matters and should be approached with **extreme care**. They are literally life-threatening. If the case is serious, do everything you can to seek professional help.

My entire approach is heavily biased towards someone with a high level of curiosity and interest in technical and philosophical matters. This is for two reasons. First, the situation I saw seems to fit this description. Second, that profile is arguably the easiest to engage with in this way. This strategy would likely be inadequate for someone with no interest in such matters or someone who actively rejects looking into them.
