**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Crisis of the Modern World_’ by_ René Guénon**

I was surprised by this book, and it was a pleasure to read. It was published in French in 1927 by René Guénon, in which he argues for the decay of the modern world. It is, in some sense, the long prophetic vision of the apocalypse, but rather than the “literal” destruction of the material world, it is instead the death of culture. He encapsulates this trajectory in the cycle known as “Manvantara” from Hindu doctrine, which is divided into four periods. He claims we are in the fourth age, the “Kali-Yuga” or dark age, which is a state of dissolution before the whole cycle renews. The whole book is a deep critique of modernity and the idea of “progress,” serving as a call for a return to a religious and symbolic experience of the world.

Early in the book, he presents the spiritual distinction between the West and East. While the author has a whole book dedicated to this topic, here he gives a concise introduction to lay the groundwork. The crisis of the modern world, as Guénon argues, is a Western phenomenon. The East, due to its more traditional culture and a lack of hyperfocus on science, managed to retain some of its traditional and symbolic frameworks, and he argues that if the West is to survive at all, it has to learn from the East. What Guénon argues for in terms of tradition is a lot deeper than modern conservatism because it is deeply tied to religious metaphysics. These are truths that have been hidden and inaccessible to the West for hundreds of years. We cannot simply revert to tradition because we do not know what it means, having lost the meaning of its symbols. He tracks the crisis to the 14th century as philosophy became ever more entrenched in rationalism. It became profane, losing its metaphysical and religious roots. The Renaissance and Reformation were significant points in this progression, culminating with Descartes, who is the foundation of modern rationalism. The more we lost our connection to what the divine means, the more we attached scientific knowledge as a spiritual band-aid. But Guénon argues that this is knowledge of the lowest order. It has never-ending multiplicity and is never directed by a higher metaphysical principle that guides the culture. Nature is reduced to a mere machine upon which we study dead components for manipulation. Even with Aristotle, the father of science itself, physics was subordinate to metaphysics, but as science progressed, this structure was lost. This created a materialistic culture, and its connection to rationalism is a direct link. In turn, both created individualism, in which nothing can be higher than the individual. It is the culmination of science’s obsession with multiplicity, which ignores higher levels of abstraction that are just as real and just as important but scientifically invisible.

There were some parts of the book of which I was not the biggest fan. I did not agree with everything, and I certainly do not embrace his emphasis on societal hierarchy, especially applied to religious institutions and even more so to political systems. In addition, his thought is unfortunately often vague, although that is the very essence of the topic and in large part why it has been rejected in modernity, where everything has to be explicit and articulate. If it is not “in the light” of rational thought, it does not exist. But overall, I found the book engaging and with useful insights. I read this shortly after _The Master and His Emissary_ by Iain McGilchrist, which lined up perfectly, and I have reviewed it on Medium previously. Although in an older language, many of the same problems are covered. If you are under the impression that tradition is useless, or under the spell of how the Enlightenment was the division between good, scientific modernity and the bad, old, superstitious dark ages, then this book will make no sense whatsoever. But I certainly do not think that is the case, and Guénon explores some difficult topics regarding what was lost in the process. If you want to explore these topics, I would read McGilchrist’s work first, which presents it in a much more digestible and modern format aided by neuroscience. Then you will be able to better appreciate Guénon’s insights into the modern world and its imminent collapse. At close to one hundred years old, this book could not be more relevant, and many of the problems he expressed are more obvious now than when they were written.
