#### Rescuing the Ultimate Good

 ![](/uploads/image_122963.png)

I read The Sovereignty of Good many years ago, and I was deeply&nbsp; impressed by Murdoch’s writing and her worldview, particularly in&nbsp; bringing back Plato’s ultimate Good, which at the time deeply helped me&nbsp; better understand religion and what the concept of God meant. I always&nbsp; knew of her other work of Metaphysics as a Guide to Morals, but given&nbsp; how long it was, I was intimidated by it for several years until I&nbsp; finally gained the courage to read it. My intimidation wasn’t misguided.&nbsp; It’s a very difficult work, and at times very tedious. It’s also very&nbsp; long, around 600 pages (with a very small font, mind you). Also, the&nbsp; book doesn’t always feel the most coherent and logical. It feels a bit&nbsp; scattered, almost like my own writing, funny enough. I felt like I was&nbsp; tasting my own medicine.&nbsp;

There are huge discussions on Plato,&nbsp; Anselm, Kant, Schopenhauer, and Wittgenstein, and to a smaller degree&nbsp; also Heidegger, Derrida, Simone Weil, Ayer, and much more. It tends to&nbsp; be interpreted as an extension of The Sovereignty of Good, but it’s much&nbsp; more wide ranging in its scope, and in some cases it looks like it&nbsp; comes from a different perspective altogether.

Despite being very&nbsp; difficult, this is probably one of my most annotated books ever. What I&nbsp; find so appealing is her approach to religion. She recognizes its&nbsp; problematic nature within a scientific worldview, yet at the same time&nbsp; maintains its absolute grounding for all human life. What she thinks,&nbsp; and throughout her work tries to do, is provide Platonism as a bridge.&nbsp; While doing so, she provides an interpretation of Plato that she feels&nbsp; is more accurate while also avoiding many of the pitfalls that make&nbsp; modern people and philosophers dismiss it.

The title itself&nbsp; signals that metaphysics, our understanding of reality’s fundamental&nbsp; nature, should guide ethics rather than the two being kept separate. The&nbsp; organizing question is how large philosophical conceptions relate to&nbsp; mundane daily life, how metaphysics can be a guide to morals. Murdoch&nbsp; defends a substantive metaphysics that includes objective value and the&nbsp; reality of the Good, drawing heavily on Plato. This metaphysical&nbsp; framework provides orientation and grounding for moral life, showing&nbsp; that morality isn’t arbitrary or merely conventional but connected to&nbsp; the nature of reality itself.

She positions herself within a&nbsp; religious tradition of philosophers like Plato, Kant, Wittgenstein,&nbsp; Schopenhauer, and Hegel who were committed to protecting absolute value.&nbsp; These philosophers faced a problem: how to preserve the absolute,&nbsp; necessary nature of moral values in a world full of accidents, changes,&nbsp; and randomness. Their solution was to separate value from the&nbsp; contingent, messy world of everyday life, placing moral truths in an&nbsp; eternal realm untouched by change. For Murdoch, morality is a continuing&nbsp; part of all human activity rather than a separate small segment of&nbsp; philosophy studied in ethics classes. Happiness is part of morality, but&nbsp; morals go beyond it. Morality aims at something higher than personal&nbsp; satisfaction or pleasure. It’s an orientation, not just following rules.&nbsp; The moral life is about directing oneself toward the Good rather than&nbsp; mere rule-following.

Several key ideas follow from this&nbsp; orientation. The moral life involves interpreting ideals into practical&nbsp; action, not imitating examples. The Platonic painter gazes at the Good&nbsp; and interprets it into life’s particulars, rather than copying it&nbsp; literally. The Good is a map. It provides direction and orientation for&nbsp; navigating moral life. The Good as reality principle is how we find our&nbsp; way about the world, not just a distant ideal. Each one of us is defined&nbsp; by our relation to the good. We are either moving towards it or away&nbsp; from it, making moral progress or regression. There is no neutral place.&nbsp; We cannot step outside the moral dimension of existence.

Against&nbsp; modernity, Murdoch wants to defend or re-examine the old idea of the&nbsp; self, the truth-seeking individual person, as a moral and spiritual&nbsp; center. She's protecting and continuing the tradition of absolute values&nbsp; that have an authority, a demand, what she calls the purity of value or&nbsp; the absolute reality of a pure good. Yet this project faces a&nbsp; fundamental tension. Our minds naturally want everything to fit together&nbsp; neatly, to create unified, coherent pictures of reality. Murdoch calls&nbsp; this "one-making." But this desire for neat unity can falsify reality by&nbsp; making us ignore uncomfortable truths. The real world is messy. Things&nbsp; happen by accident. People die. Life is full of trivial, meaningless&nbsp; details. This messiness, what philosophers call contingency, threatens&nbsp; to make moral values seem like just another arbitrary feature of the&nbsp; world rather than something absolute and necessary. To protect the&nbsp; absolute nature of value, traditional philosophers separated it entirely&nbsp; from this messy, contingent world, placing values in an eternal realm&nbsp; beyond change and accident.

Murdoch’s key move reverses the&nbsp; traditional approach. Instead of separating value from contingency and&nbsp; redistributing it back into the world, she locates value in contingency&nbsp; from the start. Contingency is our only medium of transcendence. She&nbsp; introduces the idea of the “broken circle.” Any good image or whole must&nbsp; acknowledge its incompleteness and point away toward what we don’t yet&nbsp; understand. Any idea of the good is incomplete. Our conceptions are&nbsp; always partial and must remain open to revision.

This view of&nbsp; value as located within contingency, always incomplete yet pointing&nbsp; beyond itself, shapes Murdoch’s account of moral life. Truth-seeking and&nbsp; virtue are inseparable. The pursuit of truth is itself a moral&nbsp; activity. Moral perception requires cognitive transformation. Seeing&nbsp; morally requires disciplining and training our vision. Moral vision is&nbsp; seeing the invisible through the visible, perceiving the real through&nbsp; the apparent. Goodness is linked to reality and knowledge. Moral&nbsp; progress means seeing things more clearly as they truly are.

Plato&nbsp; becomes crucial to this vision. Plato's Forms address both how we know,&nbsp; epistemology, and how we live, ethics, unified by the concept of truth.&nbsp; The connection of virtue and knowledge in Plato is fundamental. For&nbsp; Plato, moral virtue is inseparable from knowing what is real. Among all&nbsp; the Forms, Beauty holds a special role in Murdoch's reading. Beauty is&nbsp; the most readily accessible Form because we can perceive it directly&nbsp; through our senses. When we see something beautiful, we're experiencing a&nbsp; Form shining through the physical world, giving us immediate access to&nbsp; the transcendent. This makes Beauty uniquely important for moral&nbsp; education. The Form of the Good operates similarly. It is transcendent&nbsp; yet present, both beyond us and available to orient us here and now in&nbsp; our daily moral choices.

In re-animating Plato, she gives force to&nbsp; the reality of the Good, and to a sense of the moral life as a&nbsp; pilgrimage from illusion to reality. Murdoch insists that Plato is&nbsp; talking in metaphysical metaphors, myths, images. There is no Platonic&nbsp; “elsewhere,” no separate realm like the Christian heaven. What is higher&nbsp; is, as Eckhart observed, inside the soul. Plato presents his myths and&nbsp; metaphors with playful irony, marking them explicitly as metaphors,&nbsp; “something like this might be true,” not literal doctrines.

Murdoch’s&nbsp; Platonism leads her to reject key assumptions of modern philosophy.&nbsp; Murdoch rejects the sharp separation between facts and values that&nbsp; dominated 20th-century philosophy, particularly the view that facts are&nbsp; objective while values are merely subjective preferences. She argues&nbsp; that moral perception is itself a form of truthful seeing rather than&nbsp; emotional projection onto neutral facts. When we perceive someone as&nbsp; courageous or cruel, we’re not adding subjective feelings to value-free&nbsp; data but accurately recognizing real features of situations and people.&nbsp; This challenges the idea that morality is fundamentally non-cognitive or&nbsp; separate from how things actually are.

For Murdoch, morality is&nbsp; cognitive because it centrally involves knowledge, accurate perception,&nbsp; and understanding rather than simply emotion or willpower. Consciousness&nbsp; is essential because moral work happens primarily through attention,&nbsp; learning to see situations and people clearly, free from the distortions&nbsp; of ego, selfishness, and fantasy. Moral improvement means disciplining&nbsp; our consciousness to perceive reality more truthfully, which requires&nbsp; sustained effort and the cultivation of virtues. This makes morality&nbsp; fundamentally about vision and awareness, not just about making choices&nbsp; or following rules.

She contrasts Plato and Kant on passion. Plato&nbsp; sees Eros transformed into spiritual force through disciplined&nbsp; purification, while Kant treats feelings as dangerous to morality and&nbsp; separates reason from emotion entirely. For Murdoch, this distinction&nbsp; matters because it shapes how we understand religion and moral life.&nbsp; Religion, on her view, is a matter of continuous consciousness.&nbsp; Everything always matters, every second, forever. Religion is a mode of&nbsp; belief in the unique sovereign place of goodness or virtue in human&nbsp; life, something that must go all the way, to the base, to the top. It&nbsp; must be everywhere. Religion adheres essentially to the conception of&nbsp; being human, and cannot be detached. This totality means religion is not&nbsp; accidental, does not exist contingently, but rather stands above being.

She&nbsp; takes the idea of the world as full of images of God and hierarchies&nbsp; pointing to God as fundamental in religion and, if we put Good for God,&nbsp; in morality. She thinks this is what the world is full of. There is a&nbsp; way of seeing, what theological tradition calls the affirmative way,&nbsp; that recognizes traces of the divine everywhere, even in our ordinary&nbsp; desires and thoughts. This approach connects spirituality to the whole&nbsp; of our being rather than confining it to special moments or practices.&nbsp; Good is fundamental reality, not derived from God but what God&nbsp; symbolized. God-language points to the reality of the Good. The idea of&nbsp; perfection haunts all our activity, and we are well aware of how we try&nbsp; to blot it out. This “Good” is not the old God in disguise, but rather&nbsp; what the old God actually symbolized.

This connection between&nbsp; perfection and reality leads Murdoch to engage seriously with the&nbsp; ontological argument. While she doesn’t accept it as a successful proof&nbsp; of God, she finds it helpful for her defense of moral realism. The&nbsp; ontological argument attempts to show that the concept of a perfect&nbsp; being logically entails that being’s existence. For Murdoch, this offers&nbsp; a suggestive parallel for her conviction that the Good is something&nbsp; real and transcendent rather than a human construction. The argument&nbsp; suggests that perfection isn’t merely an idea we’ve invented, but&nbsp; something that must exist because of what perfection means. Similarly,&nbsp; Murdoch believes that when we think seriously about goodness, we’re not&nbsp; just expressing preferences or cultural conventions but recognizing&nbsp; something that transcends our individual perspectives.&nbsp;

The&nbsp; argument’s movement from concept to necessary existence resonates with&nbsp; her Platonist view that recognizing true goodness means acknowledging&nbsp; something beyond us that orients our moral life. In other words, the&nbsp; ontological argument shows that goodness is not contingent but necessary&nbsp; to our very conception of what is, built into the structure of reality&nbsp; itself rather than optional or subjective. And drawing from Tillich, she&nbsp; notes that we can recognize the limits of the ontological argument, yet&nbsp; what matters more is our very capacity to pose the question, to wonder&nbsp; about God’s possibility. From Wittgenstein, “running up against the&nbsp; limits of language is ethics.” Ethics involves straining against what&nbsp; cannot be said, yet the attempt matters.

These abstract questions&nbsp; about necessity, transcendence, and the limits of language lead Murdoch&nbsp; to confront a concrete problem: what to make of Christianity itself.&nbsp; She’s struggling with the necessity of de-mythologizing it, yet the very&nbsp; danger of doing so. Can we drop Christ out of Christianity? Very&nbsp; unlikely. Can we drop the resurrection out of Christ? It’s a very&nbsp; difficult question. The difficulty lies in determining where to draw the&nbsp; line in this process of demythologization. Can we remove the entire&nbsp; notion of God, as Buddhism and Hinduism, which she repeatedly alludes&nbsp; to, have done to some degree? But as she says, wouldn’t that be&nbsp; “changing religion into philosophy before our eyes?”

Her tentative&nbsp; answer is that Christianity could perhaps continue without a personal&nbsp; God or a risen Christ, without beliefs in supernatural places and&nbsp; happenings, such as heaven and life after death, instead retaining the&nbsp; mystical figure of Christ occupying a place analogous to that of Buddha,&nbsp; a Christ who can console and save, but who is to be found as a living&nbsp; force within each human soul and not in some supernatural elsewhere.&nbsp; This radical reimagining would preserve and renew the Christian&nbsp; tradition as it has always, in one way or another, been preserved and&nbsp; renewed. Christianity has always changed itself into something that can&nbsp; be generally believed. Yet Murdoch doubts whether this transformation&nbsp; can be brought about soon enough, that is before Christian belief and&nbsp; practice virtually disappear. She notes that accomplishing such a leap&nbsp; might require philosophers to become theologians and theologians to&nbsp; become philosophers, which seems unlikely.

This connects to the&nbsp; broader problem of demythologization. Murdoch points out that words&nbsp; vanish or alter their meanings naturally through language use and social&nbsp; change, but thinkers can also deliberately shape these shifts. Theology&nbsp; needs speculative imagination. The word “God” is used less often now.&nbsp; This raises urgent questions: Should its traditional meaning be left&nbsp; unquestioned? Should we let it dwindle and disappear, along with the&nbsp; person it once designated? Or should we preserve and renew its ancient&nbsp; power while allowing its sense to change, no longer designating a person&nbsp; but pointing to something real nonetheless? Murdoch argues we need to&nbsp; surround our “great words” (concepts) with narrower, more specialized&nbsp; ones with smaller, clearer meanings. We must protect the precision of&nbsp; these secondary moral words, keeping them in active use. The artistry of&nbsp; good prose writing, moral reflections in fiction and non-fiction, can&nbsp; serve this purpose. Living is making distinctions and indicating order&nbsp; and pattern.

It’s not a book for everyone. In fact, despite how&nbsp; much I gained from it, I’d hesitate to recommend it to almost anyone.&nbsp; The density, length, and occasionally scattered structure make it&nbsp; genuinely difficult, and most readers would be better served by The&nbsp; Sovereignty of Good. The exception: if you’ve already read that book,&nbsp; found it compelling, and want to dive much deeper into Murdoch’s&nbsp; thought, accepting the massive time investment required. But it was&nbsp; still deeply fulfilling, and many of the ideas that I describe above are&nbsp; the most important in all philosophy, and they’re articulated&nbsp; brilliantly here, sometimes better than anywhere else. There is nothing&nbsp; more important than values. That’s what the very concept of importance&nbsp; is underpinned by. And likewise, there is nothing more important than&nbsp; rescuing a worldview where we can make sense of values as real and see&nbsp; their deep interconnection with religion.
