**_A book review of Aion by Carl Jung_**

Aion is one of Jung’s central books and focuses on the concept of the Self, though some other Jungian themes like the ego, shadow, and anima/animus are also mentioned. Since I’ve touched a bit on Jung himself and his philosophy in my review of _The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious_, I will spare you the repetition here. After some general introduction to archetypes, the book starts by discussing the notion of evil, particularly in Christianity. The religion holds a tradition stating that evil is simply the lack of good rather than a thing in itself. Jung argues against this from a logical standpoint, but more importantly, he illustrates how this type of thinking has harmed society’s notion of evil and thus the shadow of each individual. This is crucial because it plays a key role in the individuation process, which is the act of becoming the self.

He presents the self archetype through the lens of Christ, characterizing him as the living myth of our culture. Christ represents the totality of personality and the embodiment of the divine. To achieve this totality, however, one must also embrace our unconscious dark side, what Jung calls the shadow. This is often represented with animalistic symbolism, commonly illustrated by a snake or a reptile, or in myths and fairy tales as a dragon. This dark side is not purely negative but is also a source of insight, power, and creativity. While this was recognized in Gnosticism, Christianity rejected this inferior aspect of the personality and externalized it into the Antichrist. Hence Jung’s insistence at the beginning on the reality of evil and the mistake of casting it aside only in relation to goodness.

There is a heavy influence of alchemy throughout the text, where the _lapis philosophorum_ and its countless variations all point to the Anthropos. The _lapis philosophorum_ is the philosopher’s stone which allows for the turning of base metals into gold, and it is associated with immortality and enlightenment. The Anthropos is the first human being in the Gnostic system, and for Jung, this is the perfect and whole man in which the conscious and unconscious processes are united. This self is the antithesis of the subjective ego-psyche. Jung’s writings on alchemy are quite difficult, but from what I’ve gathered, it concerns the symbolism between matter and psyche. It is obsessed with transformation, yet it is quite different from chemistry (even beyond proto-chemistry) because it takes into account the ego doing the transformations. The transformation of matter depends on the transformation of the spirit, as they viewed it as the same thing. The alchemical opus, or the process of making the philosopher’s stone, is the individuation process. The transformation of the spirit wasn’t inside you (the ego) per se, but rather it was outside of you (the unconscious) and cast upon matter. A large chapter is dedicated to the fish symbolism of the self in which a fish may be caught in the deep sea and incorporated into the human body. The sea represents the unconscious and the unknown, and the fishing is the symbol for the self, just as the fish was for a long time a symbol for Christ. It also represents a tradition of integrating Christ into one’s inner experience, or the Christ within.

There are many other things touched on in this book, although they generally cluster around the topic of the self and its symbolism. I’ve only described a tiny portion of it, perhaps about five to ten percent. It is quite possibly the most difficult book I have read. There are countless times where I have no idea what he is talking about for pages on end. Sometimes it clicks after he touches it from another perspective, and sometimes it doesn’t. His writings on astrology, in particular, I found close to impenetrable. Most of the time, I recognize he is trying to reverse engineer what led to astrology in the first place and its symbolism. Yet the way he does so often seems so disconnected from our current worldview that it is very hard to follow. Besides, I felt at times that it was mixed with metaphysical beliefs which only makes it an infinitely more confusing topic. If you want to take a deep dive into the concept of the Self, you will definitely find a lot of material here. However, if you just want a general conception of it or to dip your feet into Jungian theory in general, this is likely a bad choice. It is complex, time-consuming, and hard to follow. Even so, like in any of Jung’s books, there are countless golden insights into the psyche which for some may justify the read. It certainly did for me.
