Peter Sjöstedt-H is a philosopher of mind and associate lecturer at the University of Exeter whose work sits at the intersection of panpsychism, altered states of consciousness, and meta-ethics. In this conversation he traces his path from Eastern philosophy through Schopenhauer to panpsychism, and explains why the hard problem of consciousness is not a puzzle awaiting a solution but a symptom of a fundamentally broken physicalist paradigm. He defends a thoroughgoing moral anti-realism, arguing that cruelty and kindness are real, but that calling them objectively good or evil requires a transcendent standard most people quietly abandon the moment they examine it. He then turns to psychedelics: why salvia and DMT crack open states beyond mind and matter entirely, why the ancient ritualized approach to these substances was correct and our recreational dismissal of them is not, and how Whitehead's process philosophy, with its actual occasions, prehensions, and eternal objects, may be the metaphysical framework best equipped to make sense of what these experiences reveal. The conversation closes on emergence, epiphenomenalism, and why mental causation remains the central unsolved crisis of the materialist worldview.

## Who Is Peter Sjöstedt-H?

Peter Sjöstedt-H is an Anglo-Scandinavian philosopher of mind and associate lecturer and research fellow at the University of Exeter. His PhD focused on panpsychism, the view that mind is ubiquitous throughout nature, and he has written extensively on altered states of consciousness, Nietzschean ethics, and the philosophy of Spinoza and Whitehead. He is the author of Noumenautics (2015) and is based in Cornwall, England.

## Top 3 Thesis

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; The hard problem of consciousness is not a gap that physicalism will eventually close but a falsification of the entire physicalist paradigm: you cannot explain mind through what we currently understand matter to be, and this has been the cul-de-sac of the philosophy of mind since Leibniz's mill.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Moral anti-realism is not nihilism but an honest recognition that there is no absolute standard of good and evil independent of the subject doing the valuing, Spinoza and Nietzsche were right that what we call good is what advances oneself or one's group, and the argument for objective morality quietly presupposes the Platonic Form of the Good that almost no one will openly defend.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Psychedelics, approached with the reverence of ancient ritual rather than recreational indifference, may give access to attributes of nature beyond mind and matter entirely, a possibility Spinoza himself hinted at, and Whitehead's metaphysics of prehension and eternal objects is currently the framework best positioned to account for what those experiences reveal.

## Key Insights

**The hard problem of consciousness is a symptom, not a puzzle.**

Sjöstedt-H argues that physicalism keeps running into the same cul-de-sac Leibniz identified with his mill: no matter how deeply you zoom into neural activity, you never find the subjective experience itself. What most philosophers treat as a difficult question awaiting more neuroscience, he treats as evidence that the entire cosmology is wrong. The parallel he draws is medieval medicine: the fact of recurring pandemics was not explained by better humoral theory but by the discovery of an entirely unknown category, microorganisms. Something analogous, he suspects, is missing from our account of mind and matter.

**Moral anti-realism does not deny kindness, it denies that kindness has a cosmic scorekeeper.**

The conversation surfaces a distinction Sjöstedt-H considers underappreciated: the difference between the reality of cruelty and kindness as modes of behavior, and the metaphysical claim that they are objectively good or evil. He accepts the former entirely. What he rejects is the latter, which he argues requires positing something very close to Plato's Form of the Good, a transcendent standard most people quietly abandon the moment it is named. Cultures have valued revenge as a virtue and kindness as weakness; this fluctuation is not evidence of moral ignorance but of the absence of an objective arbiter. His position on aesthetic value is notably different: he suspects objective beauty may be real, which makes the asymmetry philosophically interesting rather than merely convenient.

**Psychedelics may reveal attributes of nature that are neither mind nor matter.**

Drawing on Spinoza's metaphysics, Sjöstedt-H notes that Spinoza posited an infinity of attributes to nature, of which humans have access to only two, thought (mind) and extension (matter). The philosopher hinted that altered states might open access to others. Sjöstedt-H takes this seriously: the most radical psychedelic experiences, the salvia trip that deposited him on a pink harbor street with no memory of who he was, the sense of gaining qualia with no referent in ordinary experience, may not be better described by panpsychism alone but by something more radical: access to modes of reality that fall outside the mind/matter binary entirely.

**Whitehead's process philosophy is the framework most compatible with psychedelic phenomenology.**

The conversation moves from metaphysics to the specific architecture of Whitehead's system: actual occasions as the fundamental units of experience, prehension as the primal form of perception through which those units absorb and constitute one another, and eternal objects as the Platonic-adjacent forms that account for higher orders of sentience. Sjöstedt-H argues that under psychedelics, the normally dominant mode of perception, presentational immediacy, the sharp subject/object picture, is suppressed, and causal efficacy, the primal prehensive connection to the world, is amplified. This is why one can feel one is becoming the leaf. He stops well short of claiming proof; the point is that Whitehead's framework makes sense of these experiences in a way that physicalism simply cannot.

**Mental causation is the central unsolved crisis of the materialist worldview, and epiphenomenalism makes it worse.**

The discussion of emergence leads to Jaegwon Kim's argument: if mind supervenes on matter, how does the supervening thing exert downward causation on the body? Emergentism cannot easily answer this. Epiphenomenalism, the view that mental states are after-effects with no causal power, like steam from a locomotive, avoids the problem only by making it worse: it implies that seeing the wolf has no causal role in running from the wolf. This seems straightforwardly false, and Sjöstedt-H argues that evolutionary considerations reinforce the intuition: something that plays no causal role would not persist across so many independently evolved lineages. His preferred resolution is not to separate mental from physical causation but to reframe the physical itself, in line with Whitehead, so that what we call matter, properly understood, already includes the mental.

**P:** There are a number of distinctions. So the smallest unit for him is an Actual Entity or Actual Occasion, which is like a drop of experience from William James. It is essentially sentience.
