**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_Phenomenology: An Introduction_’ by_ Anthony Chemero and Stephan Kaufer**

I’ve become increasingly fascinated with phenomenology whenever I encounter it, and I felt it was worth dedicating some time to the subject. Phenomenology is generally considered to start with Husserl in the 1890s, and it is still practiced today. The book starts with Kant’s philosophy because it is the background into which phenomenology is born, and it represents what many of the earlier thinkers were critiquing or trying to improve. Then it moves on to the “first” phenomenologist, Edmund Husserl. This sets the entire tone for what the book will be, as most of the field relies on insights Husserl made over 100 years ago. From there, the book moves to Heidegger and his existential phenomenology. It deals with the intelligibility of the everyday world, the misguided cognitivism of Descartes, Being-in-the-world, the existential conception of the self, and finally themes like death, guilt, and authenticity. I somewhat liked that the book wasn’t afraid to occasionally deviate from pure phenomenology to the general philosophy of its thinkers, for while this is perhaps not a foundation to the topic, it is nevertheless connected and dependent upon it.

After Heidegger, the text shifts to Gestalt psychology. This was a movement that attempts to emphasize the notion of emergence and how the whole is more than the sum of its parts, meaning we perceive patterns and not just individual sensations. This was a movement against atomistic psychology, and it greatly influenced Maurice Merleau-Ponty, who along with Husserl is likely the most influential phenomenologist. The discussion starts with his work on perception, which is what he focused on the most. He shows how perception isn’t just passive reception of information but already comes “pre-packaged” with meaning. It is anti-representationalist (going against Kant) and shows that we perceive the world according to its utility. Afterward, the book touches on Jean-Paul Sartre with his phenomenological existentialism and James Gibson’s ecological psychology.

The book finishes with Hubert Dreyfus’ work and phenomenological cognitive science. This was my favorite part, and it ended the book nicely. Dreyfus argued against the cognitive revolution within cognitive science which neglected the insights of phenomenology. This was especially relevant as it was the start of artificial intelligence, and Dreyfus successfully argued that the massive failure of the field was caused by wrongful thinking of the “mind” as basic rule-based computation. Regarding cognitive science, it touches on the frame problem that plagues AI, and then some phenomenologically inspired subfields of cognitive science, like radical embodied cognitive science and dynamical system theories. This was delightful to read, especially because it puts phenomenology into such a practical use, and it is a clear example of philosophy directly guiding scientific understanding. It is remarkable that there is even “Heideggerian” cognitive science, and that it can help us understand how to develop intelligent and human-like agents by abandoning the cognitivist approach and instead emphasizing the world of the body and how the world manifests itself by its meaning and utility. Overall, I found the book very well written. Although at times difficult, the text is generally accessible, and no heavy background is needed. I felt each chapter had an appropriate length, and most of the time I dived into the appropriate amount of depth. I only missed a summary chapter at the end, as it just ended abruptly. Concluding the book and connecting all the dots that were explored throughout the history of phenomenology would be tremendously helpful, and it should be introduced in the next edition.
