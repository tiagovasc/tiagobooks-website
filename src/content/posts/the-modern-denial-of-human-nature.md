**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Blank Slate: The Modern Denial of Human Nature_’ by_ Steven Pinker**

Both the title and subtitle of this book could not be more perfect, as Steven Pinker provides a comprehensive overview of the modern denial of human nature and explains why the view of the blank slate is simply wrong. He is not arguing for biological determinism or claiming that nurture has no effect. Rather, he argues against the extreme view that we have no inherent nature, a perspective that is quite persuasive in our culture and even more so in intellectual circles, particularly within the social sciences. Pinker begins by identifying the philosophical roots that sustain this belief, outlining three main pillars. The first is the "Blank Slate," which is heavily tied to John Locke and empiricism, although similar claims go as far back as Aquinas and Aristotle. The second is the "Noble Savage," which states that human beings are good by default but are corrupted by society. This concept stems mostly from romanticism, and Pinker highlights Jean-Jacques Rousseau as a major influence here. The third and final pillar is the "Ghost in the Machine," originating from Cartesian dualism. I thought the case against the Blank Slate was argued quite well and nails exactly what causes the current climate against innate behavior. This seems to be the foundational error in society that drives much of the antagonism against evolutionary psychology. The Noble Savage is, from my experience, more politically charged and remains a strong pillar of social science. I was not as much of a fan regarding his treatment of the Ghost in the Machine. At times he tried to pull the discussion toward a free-will debate, which I do not think it necessarily is, but as a tangent, it certainly influences the popular belief that we exist "outside of biology."

A very large portion of the book is dedicated to arguing that the fear of human nature is unwarranted and that the assumed social or political consequences of recognizing our nature are misguided. As I was going through the book, I was initially rather disappointed by this approach. I expected the text to be very science-heavy, outlining the evidence behind human nature from all possible angles. While Pinker does provide evidence for his claims, it was often presented in summary. Initially, I thought this was a mistake and that he would have been better off laying out the science as in-depth as possible. However, after finishing the book, I realized that Pinker likely followed the best route. The resistance to these ideas is not caused by a lack of science but rather by our philosophical and political assumptions. Throwing counter-evidence at such deep-seated beliefs is likely ineffective for most readers and may even cause a backfire effect where people hold their original position even more strongly. By focusing on the philosophy, Pinker makes the argument more convincing while still teaching the basic foundations of evolutionary psychology and behavioral genetics. Furthermore, the fact that the book managed to receive such a positive reception without the excessive controversy usually associated with these topics was likely derived from Pinker’s caution and his heavy emphasis on the social and political implications of the science.

A denial of our nature is attractive largely because it promises to eliminate racism, sexism, class prejudice, and almost every immoral act one can think of. To accept human nature is often thought to mean accepting our darkest side with no hope of progress. Pinker deals with many of these anxieties, and the book shines brightest when dealing with the specific fears of inequality, imperfectibility, determinism, and nihilism. I disliked the latter part of the book, which was heavily focused on the arts and postmodernism. While there is certainly some truth to the links he outlines, I think it is a much trickier connection with a lot of nuance that was likely lost in his critique. Regarding the debate of nature versus nurture, the goal for those dedicated to denying a pure nurture position is often laid out as a necessity of not denying the truth simply because it may be uncomfortable. Pinker does an incredible job of showing that the denial of human nature has serious consequences, which is a crucial point that is not discussed enough. It is not just about having a more pleasant worldview, for ignoring our biology causes avoidable suffering and death. In the very last portion of the book, he illustrates many of the points made throughout _The Blank Slate_ using literature, drawing from Mark Twain, Kurt Vonnegut, Emily Dickinson, and others. It was highly unexpected and enjoyable as it encapsulated the need for science and the humanities to work together to gain insight into the human condition.

The book was perhaps a little longer than necessary, and I thought some of his points could have been argued better, but overall I think Pinker did a fantastic job. Anyone not ideologically blinded should recognize that the denial of human nature is both wrong and dangerous. I do think that he likely overstated what some call the Standard Social Science Model (SSSM), and thus he is sometimes defeating an exaggerated fringe position. However, I believe this is unavoidable when trying to attack any entrenched position, and Pinker is biased in much the same way that every human being is. Additionally, the problem with ignoring fringe positions is that unless the root cause is addressed—specifically the Lockean-Rousseauian-Cartesian assumptions—they have a strong tendency to permeate everyday culture, meaning what is considered extreme today may be less extreme in five or ten years. Of course, this type of discussion is always hard and politics very easily creep in. Nevertheless, that should not invalidate scientific research. If there is an actual criticism against the research, then counter-evidence should be provided. Generally, that is either not done at all, or the evidence provided is quite weak, and Pinker gives an extensive amount of examples to prove this. Science aside, the discussion should be about what to do about our nature and how to interpret it, not that the science is invalid because of the feared political and social consequences. Pinker did his best to address this issue, and I hope this book contributed in a small way to the increased acceptance of evolutionary psychology and similar fields.
