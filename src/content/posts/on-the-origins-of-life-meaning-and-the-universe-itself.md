**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Big Picture: On the Origins of Life, Meaning, and the Universe Itself_’ by_ Sean M. Carroll**

I have seen Sean Carroll’s name pop up here and there, but I never read or heard anything substantial until I stumbled upon a clip of his on Joe Rogan explaining Laplacian Determinism, and I really enjoyed it. I liked his personality and I found his way of explaining engaging. I listened to the whole episode afterwards, and at some point, he mentioned he had a book on the meaning of life where he tried to bridge many disciplines together. That was music to my ears given that I find that is always where the most value comes from if you do it properly. He also seemed to take philosophy seriously, which gave me some confidence that he might have something worth saying. The whole book is based on what he calls "poetic naturalism," a framework which tries to be the bridge between a sort of reductivist, nihilistic materialism and the richness of everyday life. In this view, what is ultimately real is the quantum world, and our everyday world is purely a human construct consisting of things like chairs, love, and nations. Yet, he argues these are useful constructs that we are right in regarding as real within their own domains.

A decent portion of the book serves as a pop-science and pop-philosophy introduction, though I don’t mean that necessarily in a negative way. It makes the content pretty accessible and tries to provide a foundation for the "Core Theory" of physics he argues for later. A lot of it is structured to justify a naturalistic worldview, with a supernatural or religious worldview as the antagonist, which is something I usually find done very poorly. However, I found his approach a lot more humble and grounded than many famous "New Atheists" like Dawkins or Sam Harris. I attribute this to his above-average philosophical literacy compared to most in that community, although Daniel Dennett is quite an accomplished philosopher, so maybe a good dosing of humility is also required. His Bayesian approach to epistemology was particularly well-argued. Although he avoids dense technical terms, that style of probabilistic thinking was always what drew me towards a naturalistic worldview when I was younger. It shows, to the extent possible, why that worldview is "rational" or at least why it possesses sound reasoning. I think it provided the best foundation for naturalism out of anything that I have seen. It is mostly based on David Hume but with a fancier repackaging aided by modern statistics and scientific advancements, and to his credit, he explicitly cites Hume and presents his ideas well.

Nevertheless, despite many positives, I disliked certain aspects of the book. For one, while I understand and value his approach to naturalism, I do think it ends up being a bit reductionist at times. While I found him much humbler than most in his position, there were a few cases where I thought he overstepped and could have handled the topic better. His understanding of religion is not the greatest and often misinterprets what it is trying to achieve and its role in human understanding. He often tries to impose a materialistic worldview onto a religious one, much like many on the opposite side try to impose a religious worldview onto a materialistic one, and neither approach works. This was particularly odd to me because he constantly emphasizes the point that any model is useful for a given context, ranging from Newtonian mechanics to Einstein’s relativity, quantum fields, and even our everyday life with things like relationships. Everything works in its own domain, but if you try to bring one language into another field, it breaks down. Yet he repeatedly mixed a religious and materialistic worldview. In addition, I disliked almost everything he wrote on consciousness. I understand where he comes from, and I think he argues his side well enough, but to be brutally honest I just think he is wrong. He tries to suggest that the "Hard Problem" of consciousness isn't really a problem at all, which I didn’t quite buy. Unfortunately, consciousness permeates a fair bit of the book since it is rather important to retain a coherent naturalistic philosophy.

Overall, I quite enjoyed the book and I think he did a brilliant job of combining several fields together, which is exactly what I hoped for. His writing is very good, and I found the structure of the book well organized as it moves from the cosmos to microscopic particles and finally to human morality. It presents science at its best and highlights the appeal and reasoning of a naturalistic worldview against a supernatural one. I disagreed with some topics, but that is mostly disagreeing with his position more than anything else, and I must admit that I think he argued well for his side. Especially for those new to these types of topics like philosophy and religion who want to dip their toes in, I think this is a brilliant book.
