**_A book review of&nbsp;_ Descartes’ Error** &nbsp; **_- Psychosis and Human Nature’ by&nbsp;_Antonio Damasio**

I saw this book referenced frequently, along with Damasio himself, and always with a positive connotation, so I eventually decided to give it a shot. I was expecting a common pop-psychology or neuroscience book pointing out how the mind isn’t immaterial and depends on the brain. While it does do that, it certainly does a lot more, and it is a lot more technical and in-depth than I expected. Damasio’s main thesis is that feelings cannot be divorced from reason because reason requires emotion at a very primordial level. The duality is a lot blurrier than commonly assumed, and the brain and body have an inseparable connection that constructs human consciousness. It is not only that the mind depends on the brain, but it also depends on the body, mainly because of the somatic aspect of emotion that later contributes to cognition.

The common assumption that your mind controls a robot-like body with no interaction is wrong. Descartes’ “res extensa”, as he defined it, doesn’t exist. To be clear, Damasio isn’t trying to solve the hard problem of consciousness but rather to point out the unbreakable tie between “normal” human consciousness and the body, including but not exclusively the brain. Even more modern ideas and analogies, like software and hardware, become problematic when looking at the neurophysiology of how the brain operates. The “reason” we typically think of is not an abstract process but an embodied one. This is not exactly a new revelation, but it sums up nicely the past decades of research shedding light on the topic. It is also full of theorizing on Damasio’s part, thankfully always with a disclaimer that such is the case, regarding how many aspects of consciousness, reasoning, and experience occur.

It is honestly a difficult book. I am not new to most of the topics covered, yet I found many parts very dense. It is not impossible to get through it, even without any prior knowledge, but it is most definitely not a casual read and takes a decent amount of work as it takes you through a pretty extensive look at the many aspects of the neurobiology of cognition and emotion. This is not bad by itself, and often required when topics are complex, but I frequently felt it wasn’t necessary. To get his main point across, many technical aspects could have been spared. The densest parts came from his own theorizing, which often only provides possible mechanisms of action. Unless you are a neuroscientist yourself, this means very little. Perhaps Damasio’s goal wasn’t to make it readable to the general population but rather as extensive as he could make it without restriction to share his ideas. That would be fine, but the marketing should reflect that this is the case, and I felt it did not. The book is indeed excellent, but if you just want to get the main gist of the connection between mind and body or cognition and emotion, there are probably easier routes out there.
