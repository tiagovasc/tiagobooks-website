Shaun Gallagher is one of the leading figures at the intersection of phenomenology and cognitive science, and this conversation covers nearly every dimension of that project. He begins by tracing his intellectual formation from existentialism through Merleau-Ponty into the cognitive sciences, and explains why the interdisciplinary structure of cognitive science, unlike mainstream psychology, was uniquely hospitable to phenomenological inquiry. The discussion moves through the 4E framework (embodied, embedded, extended, enactive), unpacking what each "E" actually claims and where the disagreements between camps lie. Gallagher then addresses how embodied cognition opens up entirely new therapeutic affordances in psychopathology, from movement therapy to virtual reality environments for schizophrenia, phobias, and anorexia. He describes his neurophenomenological study of awe and wonder during simulated spaceflight, the connection between the overview effect and mystical experience, and why funding remains the binding constraint on that line of research. The conversation closes with his book Action and Interaction, where he defends interaction theory against simulation and theory-of-mind accounts of social cognition, and offers a sharp critique of Heidegger for missing primary intersubjectivity in favour of a purely pragmatic account of other minds.

## Who Is Shaun Gallagher?

Shaun Gallagher is the Lillian and Morrie Moss Professor of Excellence in Philosophy at the University of Memphis and a Senior Researcher at the University of Tromsø. He is one of the editors of the Oxford Handbook of 4E Cognition and has published widely on phenomenology, embodied and enactive cognition, psychopathology, agency, and intersubjectivity. His major works include The Phenomenological Mind (with Dan Zahavi), Enactivist Interventions, and Action and Interaction. He is a leading proponent of interaction theory as an alternative to theory-of-mind and simulation accounts of social cognition, and directed the neurophenomenological spaceflight study published in The Neurophenomenology of Awe and Wonder (2015).

## Top 3 theses

- Phenomenology is not simply a method for describing subjective experience. It is a rigorous philosophical tradition with its own internal debates, and when brought into dialogue with empirical cognitive science it opens up the body, temporality, intersubjectivity, and the environment as legitimate objects of scientific inquiry, not just introspective curiosities.

- Embodied and enactive approaches to cognition are not a minor amendment to standard cognitive science. They are a fundamental reconception: the cognitive system includes brain, body, and environment as constitutively coupled, and the specific details of biological embodiment, not just functional organisation, determine what kind of cognition is possible.

- Understanding the other person is not primarily a matter of reading their mind from the outside. It is achieved through bodily, pragmatic, and emotionally engaged interaction, and this face-to-face primary intersubjectivity is the foundation that both Heidegger and standard theory-of-mind accounts systematically miss.

## Key Insights

**1. Phenomenology is not introspection, and its scope is much wider than qualia.**

The caricature of phenomenology, that it is simply looking inward and describing conscious experience, misses nearly everything that matters. The tradition, from Husserl through Heidegger to Merleau-Ponty, addresses intentionality (the directed structure of consciousness), temporality (the way experience itself has a time-structure), embodiment (how bodily processes contribute to cognition), and intersubjectivity (how relationships with others shape cognitive processes). These are not peripheral concerns; they are the core topics that make phenomenology relevant to contemporary science.

**2. The 4E framework covers a genuine spectrum of positions, not a single unified theory.**

Embodied, embedded, extended, and enactive cognition are related but distinct claims. Embedded cognition holds that the environment causally influences thought; extended cognition makes the stronger claim that cognition is constituted by couplings between brain, body, and environment, as in the famous Otto’s notebook example from Clark and Chalmers. Enactive cognition goes further still: it rejects functionalism and insists that the specific biological details of embodiment matter, that cognition is fundamentally grounded in action and shaped by the kind of body that evolved. The disagreements between these camps are substantive.

**3. Embodied cognition expands the therapeutic toolkit for psychopathology.**

If the cognitive system includes brain, body, and environment, then something can go wrong at any of those levels, and intervention can target any of them. Rather than defaulting exclusively to pharmacological treatment aimed at neural processes, an embodied approach opens up what Gallagher calls “therapeutic affordances”: movement therapy, dance therapy, environmental restructuring, and virtual reality environments. VR work at the University of London has been applied to schizophrenia (allowing paranoid patients to rehearse frightening scenarios safely), phobias, and anorexia nervosa, where body image distortions can be directly addressed through manipulation of the virtual body.

**4. Awe and wonder during spaceflight can be partially replicated in the laboratory.**

Gallagher’s neurophenomenological spaceflight study combined first-person phenomenological interviews, EEG and heart monitoring, and structured questionnaires across 104 subjects in a VR simulation of spaceflight. The study identified 34 experiential categories that mapped onto the journal entries and interviews of actual astronauts. Scale effects, the sense of personal smallness against the vastness of the universe, were more common in the VR subjects than in astronauts themselves. The feeling of unity was rarer and harder to replicate. The study also surfaced a category that the original 34 did not anticipate: ineffability, the failure to find adequate words, visible in the transcripts as dense ellipses where subjects struggled and then settled for inadequate language.

**5. Dreyfus and Varela represent two distinct phases in bringing phenomenology to bear on cognitive science.**

Dreyfus’s early critique of artificial intelligence, drawing on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty to argue that embodiment and pragmatic environmental coupling are beyond the reach of classical symbol-manipulating AI, was an adversarial opening move. Varela’s neurophenomenology project was a constructive one: rather than saying what AI could not do, Varela asked what phenomenology could positively contribute to empirical science, and developed the Mind and Life Institute with the Dalai Lama to explore methodological bridges between mindfulness practice and neuroscience.

**6. Heidegger’s analysis of being-with collapses into secondary intersubjectivity, missing the face-to-face layer.**

Heidegger rightly identifies Mitsein, being-with, as a fundamental structure of human existence. But when he analyses how this actually works, the other person consistently appears through the mediation of shared pragmatic projects and instrumental contexts: we encounter others via the tools and tasks we jointly engage with. This is secondary intersubjectivity. What the developmental psychology literature shows, and what Gallagher argues Heidegger systematically elides, is that primary intersubjectivity comes first: the direct, face-to-face, emotionally laden, embodied encounter with the other as another person, prior to any shared task.

**S:** Finally, the **Enactive** approach, which comes out of the work by Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and people like that. They also think that the environment is important and that the body is important. The contrast between the extended mind and the inactive mind is along the lines of how they treat the importance of the body. For the extended mind, they think of it in functionalist terms. The actual physical instantiation of the process is not that important. Whereas on the enactive side, the enactivists would say that really cognition is fundamentally biological in nature. They reject that kind of functionalism; they think that the details of human embodiment will allow us to say something about human cognition, and human cognition is going to be very different from frog cognition or whatever. A different environment—what some people call a behavioral environment—opens up for a human body in contrast to the environment for the frog. They like to talk about the _umwelt_—von Uexküll's idea—that the way we experience the world on our cognitive system is all relative to the kind of body that we have. Specifically for the enactivists, the focus is on action. It's a very pragmatic analysis. This is why it builds upon the phenomenologists, for example, Heidegger's notion of the ready-to-hand or _Zuhandenheit_ and Merleau-Ponty's notion of operative intentionality. It's a very embodied and action-oriented type of analysis of cognition. The way the cognitive system has evolved is highly dependent upon both the evolution of the body and the pragmatic concerns that different animals would have for survival.
