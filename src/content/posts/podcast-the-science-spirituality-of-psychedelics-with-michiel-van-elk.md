Psychedelic science is riding a wave of hype, and neuroscientist Michiel van Elk thinks that is precisely the problem. In this conversation, van Elk challenges two of the field's most entrenched assumptions: that psychedelics are proven treatments for depression and anxiety, and that they work by "resetting" the brain through new neural connections. Neither claim, he argues, is adequately backed by the current evidence. The conversation then moves into deeper territory, the limits of the biomedical model, the poverty of cognitive science of religion, and what the concept of "more real than real" actually means in psychedelic experience. Van Elk draws on his Pentecostal upbringing, Tanya Luhrmann's anthropology of real-making, and his own prism lab research to argue that psychedelics open a door, but that the integration, ritual context, and sustained spiritual practice required to walk through it are almost entirely absent from contemporary research paradigms.

## Who Is Michiel van Elk?

Michiel van Elk is Associate Professor of Cognitive Psychology at Leiden University in the Netherlands, where he teaches courses on consciousness and empirical research methodology. He leads the PRISM Lab, Psychedelic, Religious, Spiritual, and Mystical experiences, a research group using multimethod approaches including neuroimaging, psychophysiology, and qualitative interviews to study altered states of consciousness. Raised in a Pentecostal church in the Netherlands, van Elk studied philosophy before turning to neuroscience, and his work sits at the intersection of empirical psychology, the science of religion, and the philosophy of mind.

## Top 3 theses

- The public vastly overestimates the evidence for psychedelic therapy, and scientists vastly overestimate the entropic brain hypothesis, both misconceptions share the same flaw: compelling theory running well ahead of empirical support.

- The "more real than real" quality of psychedelic experience is not primarily perceptual, it is a reconfiguration of the person's entire framework for meaning-making, one that demands the same kind of post-experience integration that religious traditions have long provided through ritual and community.

- The biomedical model of psychedelic treatment is both practically necessary for regulatory approval and philosophically impoverished, it strips away the very contextual, relational, and existential scaffolding that makes these experiences transformative in the first place.

## Key Insights

**1. Psychedelic science is caught between hype and evidence, and the gap is wider than most people think.**

Van Elk points out that general audiences expect regulatory approval of psilocybin for depression to be imminent, when the clinical data, including a Compass Pathways trial showing only a 2.6-point difference on a 60-point depression scale compared to placebo, tells a far more modest story. The entropic brain hypothesis, which proposes that psychedelics work by increasing neural entropy and resetting connectivity, is similarly influential but similarly undersupported. Van Elk's position is not that these substances are ineffective, but that premature certainty on either question is itself a scientific problem.

**2. The "more real than real" phenomenon goes far beyond perception, it is a shift in the entire framework of meaning.**

Van Elk discusses the neural explanation, psychedelics stimulate visual areas from the inside out, producing finer-grained experience than normal perception, and the brain uses fine-graininess as a cue for realness. But the host pushes back: the most significant claims people make after psychedelic experience are not perceptual at all. They involve abstract entities like God, selfhood, and meaning, things you cannot see in more detail. Van Elk agrees that there may be multiple pathways to felt realness, and that the epistemic weight people place on these experiences after they have left the psychedelic state, remaining convinced despite sober reflection, demands to be taken seriously.

**3. Psychology of religion has largely failed to study what religion actually is, and psychedelic science is repeating the mistake.**

Van Elk critiques cognitive science of religion for focusing either on vast evolutionary timescales or on five-minute mystical experiences, neither of which captures what religion means for ordinary people in their daily lives: a lived orientation toward salvation, a set of inherited coping mechanisms, a community, a story. He argues that psychedelic science has inherited this same blindspot, extracting "spiritual" language from its original context, treating the psychedelic experience as a discrete medical event rather than a doorway into an ongoing practice of integration, ritual, and meaning-making.

**4. The placebo effect in psychedelic therapy is not a confound to be controlled, it may be the mechanism.**

Drawing on Anne Harrington's concept of the "meaning response," van Elk proposes that psychedelics function as a "super placebo" by amplifying the subject's sensitivity to set and setting, to expectation, ritual context, and relational support. Rather than treating participant selection bias (prior positive psychedelic experience predicting better outcomes) as a methodological flaw to be corrected, van Elk suggests it may be pointing toward the actual population for whom psychedelic therapy is genuinely appropriate: people who already have a minimal openness to the experience and some positive expectation that altered states might help them.

**5. Psychedelic enthusiasm at scientific conferences now resembles the evangelical Christianity van Elk grew up in, and that should concern the field.**

Van Elk draws a frank parallel between the conversion-and-transformation narratives that circulate in the psychedelic research community and the testimonial culture of Pentecostal Christianity. He does not deny the validity of these personal transformations, he experienced something similar himself, but he argues that allowing evangelical enthusiasm to drive the science leads to the same epistemic failures that plague faith healing research: motivated reasoning, selective reporting, and underweighting disconfirming evidence. His hope is that the field matures into something resembling normal science: sober, rigorous, and less dependent on its practitioners' personal stake in the outcome.
