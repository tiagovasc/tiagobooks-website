A book review of ‘The Blind Spot:&nbsp;Why Science Cannot Ignore Human Experience’ by Adam Frank, Evan Thompson, and Marcelo Gleiser

* * *

If you spend enough time grappling with the questions of consciousness, reality, and meaning, you eventually run into a profound and frustrating gap. It’s a break between two worlds we all inhabit. On one side, we have the immense power and success of the scientific worldview, which has given us an unprecedented understanding of the universe by describing it as a system of impersonal laws and material objects.

On the other side, there is the undeniable, immediate, and primary reality of our own subjective experience, a world of color, sound, value, and feeling, which that same scientific worldview seems utterly incapable of explaining or even acknowledging.

It’s a gap that feels&nbsp;_fundamental_, and it’s at the very heart of this book. They attempt to diagnose exactly how this gap came to be and, more importantly, how we might begin to close it.

Their central argument is that modern science has a foundational&nbsp;“blind spot”. It systematically excludes the very thing that makes science possible in the first place, which is conscious experience. The scientific method, in its revolutionary quest for a purely objective “view from nowhere,” intentionally abstracts away from the messy, situated, first-person perspective.

This move was essential for its success. But over time, a profound amnesia set in. We committed what the philosopher Alfred North Whitehead called the&nbsp;_fallacy of misplaced concreteness_. We forgot that our scientific models are abstractions. These models are powerful maps, but we began to treat them as the territory itself.

This fundamental error led directly to what Whitehead termed the&nbsp;bifurcation of nature, the clean split between a “real” world of primary qualities (mathematical, quantitative properties like mass, shape, and motion) and a merely “apparent” world of secondary qualities (subjective experiences like color, scent, sound, and warmth).

Nature, in this view, became, in Whitehead’s memorable words, “a dull affair, soundless, scentless, colourless; merely the hurrying of material, endlessly, meaninglessly.” All the richness of the lived world was relegated to the status of a mental illusion, a ghost in the machine, an epiphenomenon of the “real” machinery of atoms. They trace this historical and philosophical trajectory brilliantly, showing how this blind spot became the unquestioned, often invisible, paradigm of physicalism and reductionism.

They make an incredibly strong case that we live with the consequences of this abstraction every day, even in something as basic as time. The authors revisit the famous debate between Henri Bergson and Albert Einstein. For Einstein, time was what a clock measures, a dimension to be spatialized in diagrams. For Bergson, this was a&nbsp;profound mistake. He argued that clock time is a useful abstraction derived from the primary reality of lived time, which he called&nbsp;_duration_: the continuous, flowing, qualitative experience of becoming.

A clock has no memory; it just displays a series of discrete “nows.” It is we, in our durational consciousness, who hold the past and present together to make sense of a clock’s reading.

To mistake the abstract grid of clock time for the reality of time itself is a perfect example of the fallacy of misplaced concreteness. The same goes for concepts like temperature, which in physics is an abstraction (the average kinetic energy of molecules) from the concrete reality of felt warmth and cold. It sounds a little crazy, but it’s not if you go through the argumentation carefully.

A good example of the blind spot is AI. The first wave AI, for instance, repeated the fallacy of misplaced concreteness by assuming the world consisted of discrete, logical objects and properties that could be represented computationally. It tried to build intelligence by giving a machine a pre-made map of the world, forgetting that learning how to create the map is what&nbsp;genuine intelligence&nbsp;is. That’s why it failed so badly.

Today’s advanced machine learning appears to solve this by no longer needing explicit programming. Instead, these systems effectively “farm” their maps by processing massive data of human language, art, and behavior. Yet this process only creates a more sophisticated illusion of intelligence. The system still understands neither the territory nor the maps themselves.

It excels at what the philosopher Brian Cantwell Smith calls&nbsp;_reckoning_, or pure calculation, but it has no capacity for&nbsp;judgment. AI systems are masters of reckoning, but they possess no judgment. This logical failure is laid bare by so called adversarial examples, where changing just a few pixels in an image can cause a system to misclassify a bus as an ostrich.

This shows that the system does not understand what a bus is.&nbsp;It only learns to recognize statistical correlations in data, not the concepts themselves. It cannot abstract the idea of a “bridge” from pictures of bridges to the concept of “bridging a gap.” And because these systems lack true judgment and can only mimic patterns, they inevitably absorb and reproduce our historically sedimented biases, which are baked into the human data they are trained on.

The book is surprisingly science-heavy, particularly regarding the historical background that led to modern physics, but it is all situated within a grounded philosophical context. I have read a fair bit on the topic, and yet there were tons of facts and perspectives that were completely new to me, and sometimes even difficult to understand, although still very worthwhile reading.

The intellectual history is traced not just as a sequence of events, but as the unfolding of an idea. You see the progression from ancient Greece, through the increasing abstraction of classical mechanics with figures like Lagrange, to the critical breaking points of relativity and quantum mechanics. This historical grounding is&nbsp;essential&nbsp;to their argument, as it shows how the blind spot is not a recent mistake from digitalization but a deeply seated inheritance of almost the entire history of science.

They demonstrate, for instance, how the&nbsp;_observer problem_&nbsp;in quantum physics is the spectacular, unavoidable return of the very observer that the classical worldview tried so hard to forget. It’s the point where the forgotten territory of experience comes back to challenge the map.

Interpretations like the Many Worlds Interpretation, which postulates a near infinite branching of universes, are then presented not as serious physics but as a key symptom of the blind spot, a desperate and ontologically extravagant attempt to save the objective reality of the mathematical formalism at any cost, rather than question the philosophical assumptions that led to the paradox.

Similarly, the book explores how recent findings in biology challenge simple reductionism from the ground up. Life, they argue, cannot be fully explained by its constituent parts alone.&nbsp;Drawing on concepts like&nbsp;autopoiesis, they show how a living system like a cell is not a machine assembled by an external designer, but an autonomous whole that actively constitutes itself, continuously creating and maintaining its own boundary and thus its own identity.

This autonomy is also visible in evolution. They explain how life seems to escape a purely mechanistic framework through concepts like Stuart Kauffman’s&nbsp;_prestateable phase space_. Unlike a predictable clockwork system where all future possibilities are known from the start, the future of the biosphere cannot be pre-stated. Evolution constantly creates new functions and new niches in ways that cannot be predicted by physical laws alone; for example, feathers that originally evolved for temperature regulation are later co-opted for flight, opening up an entirely new way of being. This suggests that life is not just based on physics but is a process of constant, creative becoming that transcends it, without being supernatural.

This leads to their proposed solution, heavily influenced by Thompson’s work on enactivism. The&nbsp;enactive view&nbsp;rejects the idea that the mind is a computer inside the skull processing data from an external world. Instead, consciousness is an activity of the whole, embodied organism as it interacts with its environment. As they put it, we need brains to perceive, but perceiving isn’t inside our brains, just as a bird needs wings to fly, but flying isn’t inside the wings. Flying is a relationship between the animal and its environment. Likewise, perceiving is our relationship with the world.

They use the&nbsp;_horizon metaphor_&nbsp;to explain this. Consciousness is the horizon within which our world appears. We can never step outside of it to get a view from nowhere, because wherever we go, we carry our horizon with us.&nbsp;Conscious experience is therefore the necessary point of departure and the ultimate source of validation for all knowledge.

We experience so many paradoxes in science and philosophy precisely because we have taken our own abstractions for reality. If we untie this knot, if we see the map for what it is, we can solve the paradox. There is no gap between the human and the scientific because the scientific was never truly real in the first place, in the deepest sense. It was always a story told from within the horizon of experience.

This is where I find myself both deeply appreciative and slightly hesitant. Their critique of materialism, reductionism, and the historical amnesia of science is one of the most&nbsp;compelling&nbsp;I have ever read. They argue that the hard problem of consciousness is a false problem, an artifact of the very bifurcation they deconstruct. If you don’t separate the world into objective physics and subjective mind in the first place, then there is no gap to bridge.

However, I’m not quite sure if their proposed solution fully resolves the core mystery of consciousness, which they use this framework to supposedly explain. I understand the intent, and it is intellectually well presented and argued for. Yet I still feel there is something about consciousness which is undeniably different than non-consciousness, which requires, to some degree, a special ontology.

Enactivism rightly emphasizes the deep link between mind, life, and environment. It does a brilliant job of explaining the structure and conditions for experience. But in its effort to create a coherent framework with no dualisms, I can’t help but feel the&nbsp;uniqueness&nbsp;of consciousness is lost in the process.

It feels like a brilliant explanation for everything around the central mystery without touching the mystery itself, the raw&nbsp;_what-it-is-like-ness_&nbsp;of experience, and how that comes about in a world that can be described without experience.

Of course, their argument is precisely that this feeling, this insistence on a lingering mystery, comes from the deeply seated dualistic assumptions that are part of the blind spot. Maybe that’s the case, and it gets quite technical and difficult to fully flesh out a counterargument without falling into the very traps they’re trying to dismantle.

I remain aware that my own intuition here may be a failure of imagination, a sign that my thinking has not yet fully escaped the paradigm they are attacking. But given that I mostly agree with their criticism of the supposed hidden assumptions (ie reductionism), I still rather put consciousness forward as something that inherently requires explanation on its own terms, and equating it with life and agency is not enough.

But this is a minor reservation in the face of a&nbsp;monumental&nbsp;work. Overall, it’s a fantastic book. And even if you disagree with their take on consciousness, there is so much more that is incredibly valuable about the book.&nbsp;It is a masterclass in interdisciplinary thinking and a work of profound synthesis. In many ways, this is the peak of contemporary philosophy on how to think about the role between science, life, and reality. It’s truly a great work that summarizes and explains an unbelievably complex and long problem in a way that’s largely accessible.

It systematically unpacks how science’s foundational blind spot arose from the fallacy of misplaced concreteness, the error of mistaking abstract models for reality itself. In showing how this single mistake created a bifurcated nature and led to profound paradoxes across science, the authors provide the tools to dissolve them, proposing an enactive, embodied worldview in their place.

It ultimately points the way toward a more integrated and human future for science by calling for a&nbsp;New Enlightenment, one that moves beyond the original Enlightenment’s purely objective and mechanistic worldview to one that reintegrates meaning, value, and first-person experience back into our understanding of the cosmos.
