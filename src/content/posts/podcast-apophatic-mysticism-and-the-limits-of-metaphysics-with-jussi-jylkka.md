Finnish philosopher and psychologist Jussi Jylkkä describes a mystical experience he had years before entering psychedelic research and explains the metaphysical agnosticism he developed from it. All metaphysical-isms, he argues, are language games. Worldviews are like eyeglasses that become visible only when we learn to see them as models. Classical mystics overreach when they convert their experiences into claims about the ultimate nature of reality.

After we finished recording, Jussi contacted me because he felt that he had not been completely honest. Rather than replace the original conversation, we recorded a second part. Jussi discusses his Christian faith, his decision to join the Lutheran Church, and an experience in which he saw physical reality as the body of Christ. We then consider whether his philosophical agnosticism can coexist with personal faith, and whether Christianity offers an account of morality and spiritual direction that his earlier position lacked.

## Who Is Jussi Jylkkä?

Jussi Jylkkä is a Finnish philosopher and psychologist whose research spans the philosophy of mind, the philosophy of reference, executive functions, metacognition, consciousness, and psychedelics. He is also a clinical psychologist. His current project examines metacognition and our relationship with AI. He published _Consciousness and the Scientific Worldview_ in Finnish, and his recent papers include “Naturalism and the Hard Problem of Mysticism in Psychedelic Science” and “Comforting Delusions? How to Evaluate the Plausibility of Mystical-Type Insights in Psychedelic Experiences.”

* * *

## Top Takeaways

- Metaphysical theories such as physicalism, idealism, panpsychism, and theism cannot be decisively verified or falsified. Jussi argues that metaphysical agnosticism is therefore more defensible than commitment to any single ism.

- Scientific models represent reality but are not reality itself. Meditation, philosophy, and psychedelics can make our normally transparent worldviews visible as models.

- Mystical experiences do not justify claims of contact with the ultimate nature of reality. For Jussi, genuine mysticism is an awareness of the limits of knowledge.

- Metaphysical agnosticism can become a hiding place. Jussi requested a second recording because the original conversation did not disclose the Christian faith that shapes his life.

- Jussi interprets physical reality as the body of Christ. He presents this as a personal faith that remains compatible with science, not as a metaphysical conclusion established by evidence.

## Memorable Quotes

> “All the models in science and everything that we know about the material and the physical are just models. They are representations, abstractions in our minds. But I myself am part of the reality that the abstractions are about.”

> “The problem that mystics usually make is that they infer that they have encountered the fundamental nature of reality in the metaphysical sense. I think that is arrogant and chauvinistic. How would you know that you have encountered the fundamental nature of everything?”

> “I had this epiphany that this physical reality is the body of Christ. In Christ, God becomes the physical reality. It is the process of God sacrificing himself to himself constantly. It is the process of creation.”

## Key Insights

**Physical models are not the physical itself.** Jussi’s central insight is that physics describes representations of reality, not reality as it exists independently of those representations. Consciousness cannot be derived from an abstraction because consciousness is part of the concrete reality that the abstraction describes.

**Worldviews are like transparent eyeglasses.** Physicalism, idealism, and theism can all become models that we mistake for reality. Philosophy and contemplative practice can make those models visible without replacing them with another unquestioned worldview.

**Mystical certainty exceeds mystical experience.** Even an expanded state of consciousness cannot establish that no reality lies beyond it. Jussi therefore rejects the move from an ineffable experience to certainty about the ground of being.

**Agnosticism was concealing part of the truth.** Jussi remained philosophically agnostic, but realized after the first recording that he had used that position to avoid discussing his Christian faith. The second recording explores the tension without pretending that either side simply cancels the other.

**Christianity gives the discussion a moral direction.** The Christian accounts of the Fall, kenosis, and the body of Christ offer ways to distinguish between possibilities and orient oneself toward the good. This becomes especially important after the first conversation reaches an impasse over morality.
