**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_Confessions_’ by_ Augustine of Hippo**

I have always known _Confessions_ as a classic of philosophy, but only recently did I discover its deeply biographical element. While somewhat obvious from the title, I had never paid it much attention. Comprising thirteen books written around 400 AD, the work is roughly divided into two parts: the first detailing his confessions and the second offering an analysis of Genesis. I did not read the latter, opting to save it for a later date, but the first part chronicles his early life and specifically his conversion to Christianity. I was expecting a mere recounting of sins and regrets, so I was surprised to find that is not the entirety of the book. While it is not a complete autobiography, it deals a fair bit with his life in a way that I had not expected yet thoroughly enjoyed. Even the most mundane details, such as his friends or his position as a teacher, were a pleasure to read and served the greater purpose of their inclusion. The narrative spans from his infancy to his teenage years, through his academic life, and finally to his conversion.

Some of the text serves as an actual confession in the conventional sense, such as the famous incident where he stole a pear from a neighbor at sixteen, not out of hunger (as he had better pears himself) but for the act of theft itself. However, as he ages, the confessions become rather theological, charting his belief in Manichaeism, his inability to convert, and finally his struggle to dedicate his life to God. The story of his life is incredible, remaining one of the most impactful narratives I have ever read. His journey reads like a novel, and near the end, his conversion to Christianity after an endless struggle is incredibly cathartic. It was not merely a gradual process, but a key event that sparked an immediate decision, and his thought process during this moment is described in beautiful detail. I offer only one caution: the book is, naturally, quite religiously heavy. While not overwhelming, those who are not religiously inclined, specifically toward Christianity, may not find the reading has the same effect. One does not need to be religious per se, but one must at least possess an interest in the subject. Even for me, the theology occasionally went deeper than I would typically find comfortable, but it was never too dense for too long, allowing for an overall smooth reading experience.

There are two major aspects of the book that stuck with me, both rooted in humility. The first is moral humility. One can sense how honest his confession is and how deeply aware he is of both the sins he has committed and the distance he has yet to travel in his moral improvement. It reminds me of when I was younger and considered myself a good person simply because I looked around and saw others acting poorly. It took a completely new worldview to see myself as a flawed person trying to improve against an ideal, but reading Augustine makes me realize how little I have progressed. Furthermore, his honesty is humbling. I have had a similar experience to the one he describes, a life-transforming realization by which I have tried to live ever since. Yet it is not easy, and whether you want to or not, you end up deviating from it. Reading his confession makes me realize how much I have to "confess" myself. There is a saying that the child is to a man what the man is to the sage, and reading Augustine feels like reading a sage while remaining the man, or even the child, in comparison.

The second aspect is intellectual humility. I cannot possibly describe how humbling it is to witness this man dealing with so many of the same theological problems that I have fought on my own. It is slightly surreal to see him asking these questions almost two thousand years ago. I do not necessarily agree with all the answers he gives, but it reminds me of my arrogance as a young new-atheist and materialist when I completely dismissed the questions themselves, acting as if the very act of questioning was a stroke of genius no one had ever conceived before. Yet here is Augustine, not only asking the same questions but debating different perspectives far better than I ever could. Being constantly reminded of my own ignorance was almost painful, enraging me to the point that I wanted to slap myself, which I certainly deserved. This connects to how long it took him to convert even after he felt the truth in his heart. He could not accept certain associations on an intellectual level, requiring a more nuanced understanding (such as realizing scripture is often symbolic or adopting a Platonic worldview) along with a leap of faith to finally call himself a Christian. Despite the gap in culture, space, and time, reading such a similar experience felt incredibly surreal.

This is one of the favorite books I have ever read, containing passages that are beautiful beyond description. It is not a book for everyone, for while it includes philosophy, it is not strictly a philosophy book, nor is it purely a theological or biographical work. Whatever it is, it is a masterpiece. I purchased the Penguin Pocket Hardback, which I recommend. The compact thickness makes it slightly difficult to keep the pages open, but that is a minor issue compared to its aesthetic appeal. Most importantly, the translation is fantastic. In order to copy some passages electronically, I used another translation and found it much harder to read. Had I read that version instead, my enjoyment would have been significantly reduced, and I am glad I picked such a good translation out of sheer luck.
