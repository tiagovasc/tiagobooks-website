**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_Mind Fixers: Psychiatry's Troubled Search for the Biology of Mental Illness_’ by_ Anne Harrington**

Anne Harrington’s _Mind Fixers_ provides a very in-depth history of psychiatry, particularly regarding the biological account of mental illness. Roughly divided into three parts, the book traces the roots of American psychiatry, the post-Freudian biological search for mental illness (focused mostly on schizophrenia, depression, and bipolar disorder), and concludes with recent developments in the 21st century alongside thoughts on how contemporary psychiatry can improve. The narrative begins in the 19th century with the obsession with brain anatomy, then moves into psychoanalysis, the first shock treatments, lobotomies, and obsessions with mothers and parenting. More recently, it delves into the first anti-anxiety medication, the hypothesizing of neurotransmitters like dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin as biological causes, the popularization of antidepressants, and the impact of the DSM. While some of this is a typical account of psychiatry, Harrington places a welcome emphasis on topics that are usually barely talked about or completely skipped, such as the anti-psychiatry movement with figures like Laing, Szasz, and Foucault, the controversies of the DSM, and the role of psychedelics in psychiatry in the 50s and 60s. Even with its biological perspective, it covers the field as a whole and effectively charts how the profession progressed.

What I really enjoyed about the topic is that I felt the book provided a fair narrative of how psychiatry developed without being pretentious about the views it describes based on post hoc knowledge. It is easy to mock why early shock treatments were implemented, the theories of psychoanalysis, or the serotonin hypothesis of depression, but at the time they were relatively reasonable positions produced by the evidence available. This book is also a great reminder of how science can be wrong and how it can overstep in our lives. While I was already familiar with much of it, re-reading the history is nevertheless incredibly depressing. The amount of suffering caused, despite the best intentions, is hard to fathom. We read of mothers who had their autistic children taken away because they were supposedly "bad mothers," mothers who were told their schizophrenic children were better off not having been born, or even the forced euthanasia of the mentally ill.

What I didn’t like about the book is that it focuses heavily on early history until the mid-20th century or so. Anything more recent, especially after the 80s and 90s, is mentioned at the very end, feeling very rushed and without much substance. It covers recent controversies of the pharmaceutical industry but without much depth, and recent developments in genetics and neuroscience regarding mental illness were completely skipped despite deserving some attention. Despite these modern shortcomings, it is nevertheless a strong book about the history of psychiatry, so I can’t place too much blame on the fact that it focused more on its roots. I was already familiar with the topic, yet I still learned tons of new things and looked at many subjects with a fresh perspective. The scale of this complexity is not something about which psychiatry needs to feel in any way embarrassed. After all, current brain science still has little understanding of the biological foundations of many, indeed most, everyday mental activities. This being the case, how could current psychiatry possibly expect to have a mature understanding of how such activities become disordered, and may possibly be reordered? In the early years of neurophysiology, Sir Charles Scott Sherrington predicted that when all was said and done, the effort to understand how different brain systems related to mental activity would likely "resolve into components for which at present we have no names." If we think Sherrington was right, we may well anticipate that the psychiatry of the future will have little use for diagnostic categories like "schizophrenia," "bipolar disorder," and "depression." The fact that we don’t know what terms it will use instead is just one measure of how far we still are from the promised land of real medical understanding of real mental illness.
