**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_Technological Slavery_’ by_ Ted Kaczynski**

**Trigger Warning**

This is the manifesto of Theodore Kaczynski, an American domestic terrorist who killed three people and injured twenty-three others in a bombing campaign designed to draw attention to his anti-technology writings. He was arrested in 1996 and is currently serving eight consecutive life sentences without the possibility of parole. I do not condone his actions, as what he did was unequivocally wrong and evil. However, that moral condemnation should not be a reason to ignore the philosophical substance of his ideas, much in the same way we should not ignore Plato because of his views on women or Heidegger because of his affiliation with the Nazi party. In fact, ensuring that these philosophical discussions occur might prevent such acts of terror in the future, as Kaczynski did what he did precisely because he felt no one was listening to his warning. Censoring his work is misguided and counter-productive. I have long held an interest in views regarding technology, which initially drew me to the book, but I was particularly curious about Kaczynski’s work because I have always had an odd fascination with people who are extremely smart yet commit insane acts for the sake of their beliefs. Of course, intelligence or extremism does not validate a view, but if there is anyone who might hold a messianic role in freeing us from a delusion, it would likely come in this form. Imagine you are in a dystopian society that no one else recognizes as dystopian, a system that creates immense suffering and threatens mankind. It is not hard to imagine the lengths one might go to in such a situation. I feel a compulsion towards such figures because, while you can easily argue they are crazy, it is possible that society is crazy instead. They could be the sane ones, and we might be ignoring the most important truth possible simply because it goes against the status quo. The fact that I have seen several credible people consider this work to be high-quality, rather than mindless reactionary ramblings, propelled me to read it.

The main work from Kaczynski is _Industrial Society and Its Future_, which was the original manifesto he sought to publicize. The edition I read is an expanded version that includes extra letters, notes, and appendices that clarify or expand his work. It is considered unfinished because he is constantly under the threat of having his communication with the outside world cut off, which causes some material to remain unpublished. While I enjoyed the extra material for its interesting additions and clarifications, I do not think it is necessary for most readers. The original text of _Industrial Society and Its Future_ contains his key ideas and is quite short, so I believe most people ought to read it as it requires little time investment. Reading the main text was honestly frightening because, given his reputation as a revolutionary madman, I expected his views to be incoherent. Instead, I found that I not only agreed with most of his ideas, but I had actually written about similar concepts a couple of years ago. I often write in a quasi-depersonalized manner where I try to express an idea that doesn’t necessarily reflect my true beliefs, yet I do hold a similar anti-technological sentiment that I have always considered a slightly delirious part of my personality. Seeing these beliefs confirmed in such an explicit light was deeply unsettling. His main thesis is that technology is inherently harmful to mankind, particularly the technology developed after the Industrial Revolution. This is a difficult concept to explain and appreciate in our technology-obsessed society, but it is a reasonable position if analyzed carefully. Kaczynski’s strongest point, and the one that shifted me toward a somewhat anti-technological stance, was the realization that while technology is antithetical to the core of humanity, its progression is incremental. What makes this truly frightening is the realization that technology cannot be stopped, not only because it is a slow progression that offers no meaningful point of resistance, but also because it comes with so many benefits regarding comfort and economic prosperity.

Many technological advances, often the most problematic ones, are advanced by claiming they help mankind, and in a way that is definitely true. The best case is genetic engineering, which is mentioned often and argued well in the text. Not only is technology beneficial, but one can even argue that not using it or developing it further is immoral as it has the ability to decrease suffering. The most horrifying cognizance is that this makes technological progression inevitable, allowing one to easily see Kaczynski’s perspective and despair. The more technological our society becomes, the harder it is to reverse it. His drastic actions were in part due to his belief that we are in a narrow period where reversing society is still possible, but that we will soon hit a point of no return. The way technology develops and the consequences of it are not easy to explain in a review, but Kaczynski does a fantastic job of presenting the case. If you decide to read this, I highly recommend reading the novel _The Machine Stops_ by E.M. Forster beforehand. It deals with the same theme but in a literary form that is incredibly well done, serving as a good non-political introduction to breaking the pro-technological view that we culturally inherit. If you think Kaczynski is just insane, I would consider Forster’s novella mandatory reading to understand Kaczynski’s worldview fully. Both texts combined would only amount to around 200 pages. The ideas in the book aren’t exactly original, as several philosophers have expressed similar concerns. However, what makes this text so valuable is that it is presented in an incredibly easy-to-digest format. There is no heavy philosophical jargon, and despite the work being uncompleted, it is surprisingly well-organized and coherent. Therefore, I believe it is the single best resource available with an anti-technological stance.

I was pleasantly surprised by how academic and well-thought-out the text was. Kaczynski is not only smart but incredibly knowledgeable, and his views are rooted in a deep knowledge of history, psychology, anthropology, and sociology. This does not mean he cannot be wrong nor that his interpretations are automatically correct, but it could easily be considered a solid academic work were it not for his actions and radical political views. I was particularly impressed by his study of previous revolutions, such as the Bolsheviks, to shed light on what type of action would be productive. Despite my compliments, I do not agree with everything. I dislike the political tone of the book, specifically where he heavily criticizes the left and its modern social justice forms. While I understand the criticism and partially agree with some of his analysis, I think it is not completely fair as a whole. It is over-simplistic and heavily distorted to fit his anti-technology ideology, though I do think some of his claims about "left psychology" are valid even if exaggerated, similar to points articulated by Thomas Sowell. If you consider yourself on the left, you will most definitely not enjoy this part, but I urge you not to let this anti-left sentiment invalidate his anti-technological stance. Try to be open-minded and see where he is coming from rather than dismissing everything outright because it critiques your political group. This is one of the major flaws of his work as it alienates the political left, who could be great allies in this endeavor given their environmental concerns. Kaczynski gives detailed reasoning for being anti-left, but I do not quite buy it. This political approach also has the consequence of attracting people who are strictly anti-left, meaning his ideas start to get infiltrated by the far right. While Kaczynski himself is not far-right in my opinion, his views can be misunderstood as being against social justice in general, an element that will inevitably be exploited.

There were other areas where I disagreed with the text. I think his view of "surrogate activities" (artificial activities beyond physical satisfaction) is misguided, and he greatly misrepresents how human beings pursue meaningful activities and how that relates to survival instincts and a life well-lived. It feels as though he is taking his own personality traits and turning them into a prescriptive philosophical position. However, I find his argument that the system artificially forces people into STEM fields exclusively due to technological and economic concerns very compelling. His erroneous view of human activities relates to how he views the identity and existential crisis of modernity. He seems to reject existentialism completely and implies that the desire to create meaning is awry, which is a misunderstanding of how human beings exist in the world. While I think his views have support from the lifestyles of more traditional societies, I believe that is caused by their particular culture which does not suffer the same existential problems we have due to our altered worldview. This does not necessarily mean the traditional worldview is correct or that the natural state of humanity, which does not seek to find meaning beyond survival and leisure, is the right one. This is related to his neglect of meaning in his philosophy. His worldview seems to have little concern for matters of spirituality and self-transcendence, except where it fits into his anti-technological ideology. Any spirituality is to be found in a pre-technological state, and that is all there is to it. I find this incredibly naive, and it causes his oversimplification of the existential crisis of modern man. He is absolutely correct about the existential threat from modern society and technology, but reducing everything to that is reductionist.

I also disagree with his revolutionary position. He thinks that mankind needs to reject post-industrial society and advocates for a revolutionary group to literally destroy society as we currently know it. I certainly do not agree with the bombings, but I would not even agree that we should reject technology completely. For one, I am unsure if the net benefit is worth it. While Kaczynski takes great care not to fall into the "noble savage" myth, addressing it at length, I think the drawbacks of a primitive life are very significant. He fully acknowledges the consequences but thinks they are worth it. I understand the position, but I think it is wrong. We would not just go back to a nomadic lifestyle; he admits that agriculture would eventually develop and concludes that medieval serfdom would likely emerge again. In fact, I am not even sure if modern society wouldn’t simply develop again with time. The fact that he doesn’t view serfdom as a big deal when compared to the conditions of a technological state only further convinces me that his analysis of the net benefit is crooked. Furthermore, I do not think such a revolution is possible in our current globalized society. I was amazed by his explanation of how our technological society could be disrupted, relying on the fragility of interconnected systems, but I still think such disruptions would always be fixed. For it to work, it would have to be a massive, global, coordinated effort, which will never happen.

I honestly did not expect the work to be so thought-provoking, and the sheer amount of insight presented is very underrated. I think it failed to gain more popularity due to its political anti-left stance, as I mentioned. I disagree with many points, but his fundamental analysis of the consequences of our technological society is not just good but completely spot on. I understand that many won’t think so, and I wouldn’t have either a few years ago, but as I have gotten older and learned more, I truly think his analysis is correct. A lot of it applies to our current situation, and if you take future developments in genetic engineering and surveillance seriously, his case becomes really strong. It is close to impossible to deny the dystopia that is coming. What makes the work so grim for me is that I think his diagnosis is correct, but his solution is incorrect. While I do not agree with his solution, I cannot really give an alternative either. It seems that I have no choice but to succumb to the system, to the machine. It truly makes me wonder if he isn’t correct after all, and if my disagreement comes from a subconscious cowardice regarding revolution, in addition to the fear of losing modern comforts even if they aren’t worth the existential cost we pay for them. It is a remarkable work, and if you are interested in the topic of technology, this is an absolute must-read. As mentioned, I recommend reading _The Machine Stops_ beforehand and keeping an open mind. While I think Kaczynski is wrong in many ways, he is definitely not mad. If anything, he is more sane than the common person by manyfold. His supposed diagnosis of paranoid schizophrenia during the trial, if anything, strengthens his position.
