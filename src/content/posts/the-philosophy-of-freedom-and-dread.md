**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_Existentialism from Dostoevsky to Sartre_’ by_ Walter Kaufmann**

This book deals with ten thinkers associated with the existentialist tradition, specifically Dostoevsky, Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Rilke, Kafka, Ortega, Jaspers, Heidegger, Sartre, and Camus. Organized by the American philosopher Walter Kaufmann, this collection is actually an anthology, which took me by surprise since it is not explicitly mentioned anywhere on the cover. While I believe anthologies are useful, it feels rather dishonest not to state this format up-front. I would have preferred a dedicated analysis on the topic, as I often find those work best as an introduction. The book begins with an introduction by Kaufmann attempting to answer the question of what existentialism actually is. This is difficult to answer because the very concept assumes uniformity among the thinkers placed within that label, yet ironically enough, many rejected such classifications. It is not easy to find a coherent philosophy among them. Topics like freedom, anxiety, individualism, authenticity, nihilism, and death are often touched upon, but they are emphasized differently and sometimes with a radically different take from each thinker. Rather than a specific philosophy, Kaufmann identifies the heart of existentialism as the refusal to belong to a pre-made school of thought (which often includes the entire philosophical canon) and a focus on bringing philosophy back to everyday life.

Following this overview, the book features selected writings from the mentioned authors, sometimes from a single body of work and other times from many. An introduction to the thinker is given by Kaufmann before the original text begins. Notably, some of these were translated for the first time by the author himself. I enjoyed the book, although at times it felt very dense to get through. Some thinkers, like Karl Jaspers, were incredibly hard to read. I often spent countless paragraphs with almost zero idea what he was saying. I think I grasped the main gist of it and often found great insights, but it was a hazardous task. I kept getting annoyed that I bought a book to help me better understand existentialism, and yet I was thrown into it with minimal help. Despite the struggle, I liked that I was introduced to thinkers I was not familiar with. Even for those I already knew, I appreciated reading a primary source, which is something I should do more of. Ortega y Gasset, in particular, was very enjoyable and seemed to have everything I liked about Heidegger but in more accessible language. I found something valuable in every chapter, though the difficulty curve was steep.

One specific aspect I disliked was the distribution of the different authors. A very large portion of the book was dedicated to Jaspers, which I found unnecessary. He occupied over 70 pages in a 360-page book covering ten different thinkers. Ortega, whom I really enjoyed reading, had a pathetic six pages. Sartre and Camus were both giants of the movement, yet Sartre had almost 100 pages while Camus had only four. If you are interested in existentialism, I am sure this volume is helpful. However, you must be mindful that it is an anthology. While the author does give an introduction before each thinker, it is rather short and not sufficient for a total beginner. Nevertheless, if you have an interest in diving into primary existential literature but would rather dip your toes into several philosophers than commit to a single one, this is a great resource.
