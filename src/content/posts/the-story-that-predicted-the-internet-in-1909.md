**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Machine Stops_’ by_ E. M. Forster &nbsp;**

**The Machine Stops** is a short science-fiction story published in 1909 that I recently decided to check out after seeing it referenced a couple of times. The fact that it was short made it particularly appealing for a quick deep dive. It is set in a future world where Earth’s surface is allegedly no longer habitable, forcing everyone to live underground in isolation. Every human being resides in a small, hexagonal room that automatically provides for every imaginable need, from food to music. The narrative focuses on two characters: Vashti and her son, Kuno. The conflict begins when the son calls his mother asking her to visit him in person, which is viewed as a bizarre and archaic request in their society. While physical travel is technically possible via airships, there is little interest in it, and people only travel out of obligation for specific work duties or extreme circumstances. Kuno questions the "Machine," which is the all-encompassing technological system that runs society. While the Machine personalizes technology for each user, it is not sentient. It is simply the massive, complex infrastructure upon which the entire world relies, yet the inhabitants have begun to treat its manual, the "Book of the Machine," with a reverence bordering on religious worship.

Kuno plays a messianic role in the story as the sole individual who wants to return to humanity’s roots and reconnect with nature. His mother, Vashti, fails to understand him completely and views his desires as almost an episode of psychosis or atavism. The plot serves to illustrate the possessive nature of technology, our increasing reliance on it, and how it effectively alienates us from the natural world. What the story does brilliantly is illustrate a dichotomy where the technology itself is not necessarily evil, but our relationship with it becomes pathological. While society is largely divorced from anything natural, this separation is technically voluntary, as the technology does not prevent exit in a direct sense, though social conditioning makes it taboo. We see how gadgets and increasing comfort lure us toward an ever-increasing desire for utility, neglecting our true needs and misaligning our values. It serves as a warning not against the existence of technology, but against its inherent "pull" on humanity, suggesting that comfort can be a cage we build for ourselves.

The topic of alienation is particularly interesting here, and it is something I have been trying to articulate more in my own thinking. There is always a hint that the Machine is hiding the "truth" or separating us from our "true" lives, though this is quite hard to explain in concrete terms. One might ask why a particular way of life is considered true, or why we need human intimacy and nature to be authentic. It is not a straightforward statement of fact, like saying a chair is made out of wood or steel. What the story tries to hint at, and what echoes throughout our culture, is that certain ways of living cannot be transcended without bringing us down or pulling us away from our potential. It is no coincidence that this society is literally located underground. We seem to have a specific nature that has embedded us with apriori values required by default. Their deficiency brings only misery, and we have no way of escaping the categorization of that deficiency as being "bad" or "untrue."

There are also significant religious undertones in the text, such as Kuno’s revelation against the Machine, his warnings to his mother, his attempted escape to the surface, and the realization that life exists outside the Machine’s dogma. Most noticeably, the quasi-prophetic nature of the story ends with the inevitable collapse of the Machine as the mending apparatuses fail. The story concludes with a hopeful note on the rebuilding of civilization, suggesting that humanity might learn from its past mistakes and correct its path toward the "truth." Overall, it was very enjoyable and well written. I highly recommend it, especially given that it is quite short and takes only one or two hours to read at most. The technological predictions were also impressive, as Forster basically predicted the internet and real-time video calls at a time when the radio had only just been invented.
