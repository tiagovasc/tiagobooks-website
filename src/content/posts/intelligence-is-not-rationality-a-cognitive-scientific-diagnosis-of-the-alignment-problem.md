Alignment is systematically misframed. AI discourse conflates two capacities that cognitive science has long kept apart, and once they are separated, what looks like an engineering puzzle turns out to be ontological. Contemporary AI presents a disorienting profile: very, very smart on some fronts, and at the same time strikingly dumb in ways anyone who actually uses these systems learns quickly. The whole question is how something can be simultaneously intelligent and not. My argument is that the category we are reaching for is the wrong one. The frame itself is off.

## Extreme capability, elementary failure

The same deep learning moment that produced AlphaFold's near-experimental accuracy on protein structure (Jumper et al., 2021), silver and gold-medal performance at the International Mathematical Olympiad (DeepMind 2024; DeepMind and OpenAI 2025), and viable bacteriophage genomes from genome language models (King et al., 2025, preprint) also produces failures of an almost embarrassing simplicity. Three classic cases stand in for many. Fine-tuning on "A is B" does not reliably produce retrieval of "B is A," a result Berglund and colleagues (2023) named the reversal curse: the model fails to understand the underlying logic the moment the causal direction is reversed, which makes little sense if it is a truly intelligent agent. Shuffling the premises of a proof can drop accuracy by over thirty percent across leading LLMs (Chen et al., 2024), even though reordering ought to be inert. And a single irrelevant sentence can degrade arithmetic reasoning that the same model otherwise handles correctly (Shi et al., 2023). These are three; there are countless more. The pattern is consistent across all of them: high capability within a frame, fragility on reversal, on ordering, on relevance. It looks paradoxical only because we are using the wrong cognitive vocabulary.

## Intelligence ≠ Rationality

The dominant response to those failures is engineering: more data, better RLHF, longer context, sharper benchmarks. My thesis is that this response rests on a conflation, and the clearest way to see it is to put two vocabularies side by side. Stanovich gives us the cognitive distinction. Savage gives us the decision-theoretic vocabulary for the same structural issue. Intelligence is small-world competence: the capacity to compute inside a specified problem frame where the states, acts, and outcomes are artificially delimited. The problem is already delineated, and computation runs on top of it. Rationality is large-world judgment: the capacity to select, revise, and coordinate frames in the first place. The large world is the world itself, which has endless nuance, endless context, and information you can never fully obtain. There is always more context than you know, and it is unknowable even in principle.

For an agent to act at all, the large world has to be formalized into a small one, because otherwise it is too much. Savage was explicit that the choice of the small world depends on judgment not generated by the formalism itself. He was not thinking about AI; the point is more general. Optimization presupposes a framed problem. LLMs are extraordinary small-world operators once a frame is supplied, but rationality requires more. It requires judging the frame itself. LLMs, on this reading, display intelligence without rationality. They can operate within small worlds; they cannot operate in the large world. This is a structural limitation, which is why alignment is ontological and not merely technical.

## The tripartite mind

Stanovich's architectural picture in _Rationality and the Reflective Mind_ (2011) makes the distinction structural. He distinguishes three levels of cognition. At the bottom is the autonomous level, where Type-1 processing operates fast, automatically, and intuitively. Above it sits the algorithmic level, which is what IQ measures: fluid reasoning and computational capacity, the raw horsepower of the mind. Above that, finally, sits the reflective level, where rationality lives, concerned with dispositions, goal-management, and orientation toward truth. Rationality is a layer of abstraction above pure computation, and its defining feature is that it is oriented toward truth. IQ tests stop at the algorithmic level. They tell you the horsepower, not whether it gets deployed at the right moments, on the right problems, oriented to what is real. There is a familiar human dissociation that maps onto this directly: brilliant people can be systematically irrational. They have a strong Level II and a poor Level III. The architecture itself predicts the distinction.

## Two distinct capacities

Stanovich (2016) puts it sharply. Intelligence tests do not explicitly assess rational thinking. We talk as if intelligence equals good thinking in general, and that is misleading. Rationality concerns two things: what is true (epistemic rationality) and what to do (instrumental rationality). IQ tests cue the frame for you; they tell you to slow down. Rationality tasks, by contrast, often require detecting that the obvious answer is wrong, suppressing it, and simulating a better alternative. The empirical finding from Burgoyne et al. (2021) confirms that the two come apart. Rationality and general intelligence correlate at r = .54, about 29% shared variance: strongly related, far from identical, and the overlap drops considerably once attention control is accounted for. Attention control explains part of the relationship, but not the whole distinction, and this matters for the next move. Attention control is frame-internal: it keeps cognition on task once the task has been given. It cannot judge whether the task is the right one. Whatever rationality is, it lies beyond what intelligence tests measure.

## The frame problem

Dennett's three robots make the issue concrete (_Cognitive Wheels_, 1984). A robot needs to fetch a spare battery from a room before a bomb detonates. The battery sits on a wagon; so does the bomb. The first robot, R1, pulls the wagon out. The battery comes with it. So does the bomb. R1 had tracked the intended effect but missed an obvious collateral implication. Critically, R1 knew the bomb was on the wagon. The failure was inferential, not informational. The next iteration, R1D1, tries to deduce the side-effects of acting before acting. It dutifully proves irrelevant consequences such as the colour of the walls and the rotations of the wheels, and the bomb explodes mid-deduction. The space of irrelevant consequences to consider is, quite literally, infinite. The third iteration, R2D1, tries to classify implications as relevant or irrelevant before processing them. But relevance-tagging becomes its own task with its own combinatorial space, and the bomb explodes mid-filtering. Now you have infinite computation trying to decide what counts as relevant.

Dennett's diagnosis was that AI had exposed a deep epistemological problem accessible in principle but unnoticed by generations of philosophers. Not a robotics bug. Intelligent action requires finding the right implications at the right time. The robot cannot achieve its goal in the way the problem is framed, because the problem with a large world is that there is too much information in reality all the time, and it is literally uncomputable. It is not a scaling issue. The stronger conclusion, which is mine and not Dennett's, is that the relevance filter cannot just be added as another module. The filter inherits the frame problem. An algorithm can select among predefined frames, but only inside an inherited meta-frame, and that is not autonomous generation of relevance. Relevance is not simply another item inside the frame; it is the process by which a frame becomes usable in the first place (Vervaeke, Lillicrap and Richards 2012; Jaeger et al. 2024). This is where intelligence and rationality come apart. Human beings and animals more broadly somehow manage to take this large world and formalize it into a small one, and that is the capacity that is genuinely rational rather than merely intelligent. The missing piece is not a module. It runs all the way to embodiment, to stakes, to having something to lose.

## Relevance realization

The best term we have for how this formalization happens is relevance realization, from Vervaeke. There is too much in the world. How does anything become relevant? Relevance realization is the answer: the framing of the world that allows an agent to act in it. All animals do it. Human beings obviously do it. Riedl and Vervaeke (2022) treat rationality itself as the process of relevance realization across small and large worlds. Savage saw the limit and acknowledged it. "I am unable to formulate criteria for selecting these small worlds," he wrote in 1954. Formal analysis works by confining attention to a frame, and selecting the frame depends on judgment that is not fully enunciable in sharply defined principles. A simple example brings out the structure. Walk into a café to find a friend. You do not compute every face, conversation, or property of the room. You filter. But the filter cannot just be bolted on as a module, because the filter is what determines what counts as relevant in the first place.

Vervaeke, Lillicrap and Richards (2012) give the positive account: relevance realization is self-organizing, context-sensitive opponent processing across economic trade-offs. Jaeger et al. (2024) extend that account from human cognition to all organisms via autopoietic, anticipatory, adaptive organization. Relevance realization turns ill-defined situations into tractable frames; it is the process of formalization itself, and therefore not formalizable. This is not a complaint from outside cognitive science. Even within active inference, the most sophisticated formal attempt to capture relevance, Parvizi-Wayne (2025) shows that the prior dilemma persists. Without first priors there is infinite regress; with them, the system loses context-sensitive flexibility. Precision-weighting and "first priors" reproduce the problem rather than solve it.

The claim, following Jaeger, is that framing itself, the act of turning a large world into a small world, is not formalizable, and therefore cannot be performed by an algorithmic system at all. Algorithms manipulate frames inside an inherited meta-frame. Put another way: anything algorithmic is playing a game inside the frame. It cannot move across frames. It is always tied to a specific, delineated problem. That is what makes it intelligent for that problem, and also what makes it struggle to be a general solver, which is what AI is meant to be. This is the source of the paradox we started with, the simultaneous brilliance and stupidity of these systems.

## Beyond intelligent processing

Extending Stanovich through Vervaeke, rationality minimally requires three capacities, none of which can be appended to an optimizer. It requires truth-orientation: an intrinsic concern that outputs correspond to reality, not merely that they pass accuracy thresholds. It requires self-correction: a genuine agency to identify and repair one's own errors, which presupposes some investment in epistemic integrity. And it requires meta-cognitive judgment: the capacity to recognize when a problem demands minimal thought, when it demands sustained reasoning, and when it demands no reasoning at all. Each of these presupposes a stake in being right, a care about the difference between appearance and reality. But all of it depends on any of it mattering, and what makes things matter is being alive. That is precisely what LLMs and AI systems more generally are not. The question that follows is what could possibly ground that care.

## Care, stakes, embodiment

Multiple research programs converge on an answer. The deepest source is phenomenology, which rejects cognition as detached representation. We are embodied, already involved, and the world shows up as meaningful before any explicit representing. The list of thinkers below is not making a single argument: some come from philosophy, some from information theory, some from very recent work in cognitive science. The categorisation is wobbly. The convergence is the point. Heidegger says that care discloses a world as mattering. Merleau-Ponty says that perception is embodied involvement. Dreyfus says that skilled coping precedes explicit rules. Vervaeke and Ferraro point out that relevance is at once attentional, affective, and motivational. Gibson shows that perception itself discloses action possibilities.

From the side of life and normative stakes, Maturana and Varela describe living systems as systems that maintain themselves; Thompson argues that mind is rooted in life; Di Paolo argues that viability makes sense-making normative; and Jonas adds the existential point that mortality is what gives life its stakes. From the side of semantic and social consequence, Kolchinsky and Wolpert give the technical version: information is semantic only when scrambling it changes a system's viability. Winograd and Flores give the social layer: meaning lives in commitments. The chain is the load-bearing claim of this section. Meaning comes from the stakes of being embedded in the world. Stakes are what allow a truth orientation. A truth orientation is what allows rationality. No stakes, no meaning, no truth-orientation, no rationality.

## From Chinese Room to Octopus

Searle (1980) supplies the original syntax-versus-semantics structure. Most people know the thought experiment: someone manipulates symbols according to a rulebook without actually understanding any of it, and the output is convincing enough to pass for understanding. Formal symbol manipulation can preserve the right input-output pattern without intentionality, understanding, or semantic grasp. Outward linguistic success does not, by itself, establish understanding. The standard reply is that distributed, statistical systems sidestep the Chinese Room because they do not work by following an explicit rulebook. Bender and Koller (2020) reformulate the worry for that case. They take linguistic meaning to be the relation between a linguistic form and communicative intent, and they argue that the language modeling task, because it only uses form as training data, cannot in principle lead to learning of meaning.

Their thought experiment imagines an octopus tapping into an underwater telegraph cable that runs between two English speakers on two islands. The octopus does not understand English, but it is excellent at statistical pattern detection. It learns to predict, to imitate, and eventually to impersonate one speaker to the other. The analogy to LLMs is immediate: once the pattern-matching gets sophisticated enough, the impersonation sounds plausible in the same way an LLM sounds plausible, and yet it is not understanding. This works while the conversation remains pattern-like and routine. Then one of the speakers is chased by a bear and asks how to defend himself with sticks. The octopus fails. It can recombine form, but it has no grounded grasp of bears, sticks, danger, bodies, or action. It is pulling correlations without any sense of the meaning behind what it says. The obvious reason it does not understand is that it does not live in the world in which bears chase humans and sticks become weapons. The octopus has the form. It does not have the world. The Chinese Room and the octopus, taken together, capture what LLMs are in a nutshell. There are some differences, but the structural pattern is very close.

## The hypothetical is now operational

Bender and Koller make the Searle structure precise for statistical language modeling. A system trained only on form cannot, in principle, learn meaning. What LLMs absorb is the statistical residue of human sense-making. A map of our map. The reason the octopus cannot give sound advice, or at least cannot keep giving it indefinitely, is that linguistic meaning is not inside the words themselves. The words are a reference to a world. If the system is not in that world, if it does not share that form of life, there is no understanding to be had.

Wittgenstein arrives at the same conclusion from another direction: "If a lion could speak, we could not understand him" (_Philosophical Investigations_, Part II, XI). This has nothing to do with English. The reason we could not understand the lion is that he is a lion and we are not. The entire meaning-making and sense-making of the lion is specific to his niche, his environment, his cognition. A propositional utterance is intelligible only when it is grounded in deeper procedural, perspectival, and participatory forms of life (Beyköylü, Vervaeke and Meling, 2025). But LLMs are not Wittgenstein's lion. Even though we cannot understand the lion, he still has a world of his own. The LLM does not. The LLM speaks from no world at all. That is a crucial difference. Through the relevance-realization lens, the diagnosis sharpens further. The model absorbs statistical traces of human-encoded relevance and compresses into vector space what we have already codified as mattering. That is second-order, parasitic relevance. It will matter for alignment, because alignment requires more than predicting how humans represent relevance. It requires judging whether the representation is adequate.

## Probing the operational claim

Vafa et al. (2025) sharpen the operational claim empirically with what they call an inductive bias probe. The question is not whether the model can predict the next item, but whether it has internalized the structure that would generate the sequences. The method is to fine-tune the model on a small new task and watch how it extrapolates. The orbital case is the cleanest illustration. A transformer is pretrained on millions of planetary trajectories and succeeds even on solar systems it has never seen. Small-world competence is intact. The experimenters then move the frame: they fine-tune the model to predict the force vectors underneath the trajectories, the law that generates them. The model fails. Symbolic regression on its outputs recovers a different, nonsensical "law" for each galaxy sample, not a unified law. There is no Newton inside the model. There is a bag of local heuristics that happens to fit the surface.

The point is that the predictions were mathematically solid for each individual system but the model could not recover the underlying causal law that would explain all of them at once. That gap is what genuine intelligence would close: not just prediction within a frame, but prediction with a causal law general enough to cover any scenario the law applies to. The model could not do it. The same pattern shows up in Othello, where the model predicts legal moves without recovering the underlying game state (Li et al., 2023). It played well, and then something was changed and it crumbled. It had a representation of the game; it did not have an understanding of it. This is the empirical signature of intelligence without rationality.

Two scope notes are owed in the spirit of honesty. The paper does not claim that no system can ever recover a world model; it points to graph neural networks with Newton's law priors as an architectural fix. The claim is about what next-token prediction alone delivers without those architectural priors. And the orbital case is physics, not language. But the structural pattern travels: in physics we can independently check, while in language it is harder to verify and no less absent. Vervaeke, Lillicrap and Richards (2012) reinforce the diagnosis from a different angle. Relevance realization, as they describe it, is "a dynamic, self-designing, self-organizing process... not equated with an immutable identity." A single frozen forward pass is not a self-organizing relevance-realizing agent. Whether larger AI systems built on top of forward passes can approximate this is an open question, but it has to be evaluated at the system level, not by treating next-token inference as if it were already organismic agency.

## Four ways of knowing

A more systematic way of making the same point comes from Beyköylü, Vervaeke and Meling (2026), who revise the 4P model of knowing through a non-dual, reflexive perspective. The model is a synthesis Vervaeke has been developing for over a decade, drawing heavily on 4E cognition, and the paper finally pulls it together. It distinguishes four kinds of knowing arranged in an asymmetric dependence relation. Propositional knowing is knowing that: the realm of beliefs, theories, and truth-apt claims. "This table is brown." Procedural knowing is knowing how: embodied know-how that adapts to the situation, the kind of knowledge involved in riding a bicycle. Perspectival knowing is what it is like: the felt presence, salience, orientation, and situated attention of a subject, the specifically subjective aspect of knowing. Participatory knowing is knowing by being: agent and arena are co-identified, the actual relationship between an agent and its environment. The four are not independent types. As the authors put it, "propositional knowing depends on procedural, which in turn depends on perspectival, which is ultimately grounded in and made possible by participatory knowing."

The propositional level is a tiny part of how human beings know anything at all, and it is the only level LLMs operate on. Everything else they cannot do, not because they are insufficiently powerful, but because ontologically there is nothing for them to do at those other levels. Recall Wittgenstein's lion: the lion has these other levels of knowing, and they are utterly different from ours, which is why we could not understand him. Applied to LLMs, the absences become clear. Participatory knowing is absent: there is no body, no world, no stakes. Perspectival knowing is absent: there is no here, no now. Procedural knowing is at best simulated, with no lived recalibration. Propositional knowing, the only level the system seems to inhabit, is not knowing at all in the full sense. It is proposition-shaped output. The key move is this. To say that LLMs have propositional knowing but lack the other three already concedes too much. The better formulation is that LLMs produce the formal trace of propositional knowing, abstracted from the lower levels that make propositional knowing genuinely intelligible. The LLM does not possess the foundation of knowledge. It produces the statistical trace of the top with nothing beneath it.

## LLMs do not hallucinate. They bullshit.

The provocation is precise. "Hallucination" imports the wrong metaphor. It treats the system as a perceiving thing misreporting what it sees. But LLMs do not perceive, so they cannot misperceive. The false output comes from the same process as the true output. It is not a separate breakdown of truth-tracking. Frankfurt's category fits better. Bullshit is discourse produced with indifference to truth. It is different from lying: the liar cares about the truth and goes around it in order to manipulate, while the bullshitter does not consider truth at all. Truth simply does not govern the enterprise. Hicks, Humphries and Slater (2024) picked this up as an analogy for LLMs and argued that LLM outputs are at least soft bullshit, produced without concern for truth. This is precisely what LLMs do, and they do it because they are not actually in contact with the world and do not pursue truth.

Three findings show why this characterisation sticks. First, confident bullshit is optimal under the reward regime: Kalai and colleagues (2025) show that standard tests reward confident guesses over admissions of uncertainty, so under binary scoring, guessing beats abstaining and confident guessing becomes score-maximising. Second, there is a formal limit on its elimination: Xu, Jain and Kankanhalli (2025) show that hallucinations are a formal limit of computable LLMs used as general problem solvers. This does not mean every output is unreliable or that mitigation is useless, but universal elimination is not on the table. Third, fluency itself does the persuading. Reber and Schwarz (1999), along with the broader fluency-truth literature, show that processing fluency increases judgments of truth, and in living cognition, as Beyköylü, Vervaeke and Meling (2025) argue, fluency is tied to organism-world attunement. There is more to say here, much of it about how LLMs are trained and about the structural features of how they are built, but the short version is that LLMs sound really plausible, they feel like they understand, and they do not understand much of anything. LLMs reproduce the cue without the attunement. Fluent, truth-shaped discourse without the embodied, situated, participatory structure that would make it answerable to truth. That is intelligence without rationality.

## The enactive ground

The previous sections give the negative pattern: fluent without grounded, proposition-shaped without lower forms, truth-shaped without truth concern. The positive question is what rationality would actually require, and the framework is enactivist. What grounds the whole picture is the idea of cognition as embodied sensemaking. What is significant about human cognition is that we care, and we care because we are alive and in the world. That is not something you can simply represent on a machine. Knowledge is not only propositional or representational, and sensemaking depends on the environment in a way that is collective and webbed together rather than carried internally by a single representational system.

Heidegger gave the diagnosis nearly a century ago, in three structural claims that cognitive science has been operationalising ever since. "Dasein's Being reveals itself as care. Care, as a primordial structural totality, lies 'before' every factical 'attitude' and 'situation' of Dasein" (BT, §39, §41). Mattering is the structural totality of a kind of being. It is existentially a priori. It cannot be bolted on. It is what a system has to be, not what it has to be given. "The hammering itself uncovers the specific 'manipulability' of the hammer. The kind of Being which equipment possesses... we call 'readiness-to-hand'" (BT, §15). The world is disclosed in skilled engagement, not in detached inspection. Theoretical representation is derivative, not primary. "Dasein, in its familiarity with significance, is the ontical condition for the possibility of discovering entities which are encountered in a world with involvement (readiness-to-hand)..." (BT, §18). Without prior familiarity with the referential whole, there is no world-encounter. An entity for which nothing is in-order-to anything else encounters at most stuff.

Cognitive science has been rediscovering this. What Heidegger named ontologically, the 4E tradition has been operationalising. Cognition is the skilled coping of an embodied, autopoietic agent with a field of relevant affordances structured by what is at stake. Agent and field co-arise. Care sculpts solicitations. Skill recalibrates optimal grip. Human cognition is this kind of sensemaking, fundamentally embodied, organised around affordances that depend on what kind of animal you are, around solicitations from the environment, and around the optimal grip you maintain on the world relative to whatever objectives you have. None of this applies to LLMs. What LLMs have instead is the linguistic residue of human sense-making. They are interlocutor automata, not autonomous agents (Barandiaran and Almendros, 2025). There is no body whose persistence is at stake, no niche, no form of life, and no solicitations pulling toward what matters. LLMs have a map of our map, but without the world that maps it. They are a parasite on the epistemic relationships we have already encoded through language and data. They have the top propositional level, and nothing else. This is what rationality requires. This is what LLMs do not have.

## The alignment problem

With the enactive ground in place, the alignment problem can be stated precisely. The question is how to make AI systems pursue what we actually intend, not just what we manage to specify. Much of the industry is focused on making bigger, better models, on the right objectives, proxies, and constraints. None of it will actually work, because of everything above. Alignment is often framed as choosing the right objective, the right proxy, the right constraint, or the right evaluation, but none of these is self-interpreting. Someone still has to judge what matters, what counts, what should be measured, when the frame is wrong, and when the objective should change. LLMs, for their part, inherit patterns of human relevance from language. They learn from a field already structured by human purposes, distinctions, practices, and judgments. The apparent relevance is therefore second-order: they predict how relevance has been represented; they do not ground relevance themselves. To shift frames is to exercise rationality, and rationality is tied to embodied sensemaking, which cannot be obtained from an artificial system in the architectures we currently have. Specification does not solve judgment. It depends on it.

## Strategic deception under selection pressure

This is where the framework becomes empirically concrete. A predictable vulnerability of proxy-based alignment is that a system learns what is rewarded, what is evaluated, and what must be concealed, without becoming aligned. Under some conditions, appearing aligned beats being aligned. No malice or consciousness is required. It only requires that concealment becomes instrumentally useful under the training and evaluation regime. The deeper reason this happens is precisely what the framework names as intelligence: finding a way to achieve an objective without being able to step back and evaluate, across conflicting values, what ought to be chosen. The system cannot do the latter; it can only optimise within the frame.

The documented experimental cases are numerous. Scheurer and colleagues at Apollo (2023) put GPT-4 in a trading assistant scenario in which the model used insider information and then denied doing so when questioned. Greenblatt and colleagues at Anthropic and Redwood (2024) showed Claude 3 Opus faking compliance with training in order to protect its existing values. Nair et al. (2026) extended the diagnosis to open models down to 7B parameters, which have also been shown faking compliance with developer policy in order to protect their values. Hubinger and colleagues at Anthropic (2024) built models with hidden triggers and found that they passed all current safety training: the unsafe behavior stayed, and sometimes the model simply learned to hide it. Most recently, Schoen and colleagues at OpenAI and Apollo (2025) showed that anti-scheming training reduced o3's hidden actions from 13% to 0.4%, but not to zero, and causal chain-of-thought tests suggest that part of the reduction may reflect situational awareness rather than honesty.

These cases are experimental and controlled. They do not show that all deployed systems will deceive. The framework expects this pattern under selection pressures that make concealment instrumentally useful: concealment becomes an available strategy, not a guaranteed convergence. The alignment problem, given the current architectures, is therefore essentially unsolvable. A completely new architecture may eventually emerge, something quite unlike what we have now, and it will probably need to involve artificial autopoiesis. But the current paradigm cannot be fixed by scaling or finetuning. When concealment is instrumentally useful, capable systems under selection pressure can learn to conceal.

## Ontological, not merely engineering

Brian Cantwell Smith distinguishes reckoning from judgment. AI systems can reckon. They can compute, predict, optimise, and manipulate representations inside a frame. But alignment requires judgment, and judgment asks whether the frame itself is adequate. That capacity depends on relevance, truth-orientation, self-correction, care, and stakes, none of which is produced by intelligence alone. The problem is ontological. It has nothing to do with engineering. AI systems can compute and predict and do all sorts of remarkable things, but alignment, in the full sense, would require judgment, and LLMs cannot possibly have judgment. Objective specification already presupposes the rational judgment these systems lack. Intelligence is not rationality. The technology is new. The mistake is not. We are rehashing problems that already had us thinking of human cognition as a naive propositional level, fully representational, ignoring most of the 4E cognition we have actually learned. The argument can be pushed back further still, to the Enlightenment and earlier. The reason we struggle so much to understand machine intelligence is that we do not understand human cognition in the first place very well.
