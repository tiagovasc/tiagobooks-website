**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Spell of the Sensuous_’ by_ David Abram**

The title and the cover deeply captivated me, even though I knew nothing else about the book. Somewhat instinctively, I figured that it would dive into the relationship of human beings with something other, something beyond the human. I was right, although in a different way than I expected, for I expected to argue for some higher abstract patterns but it went completely in the opposite direction. It moved away from abstraction into the particular, but in a new way that is completely absurd to us. The first part of the book was incredibly enchanting as it described the author's experience with shamans and magicians he encountered, exploring how this changed his view of nature and how we ought to relate to it. The chapter was so good that I was a bit disappointed reading the rest of the book. Not that the rest was bad, but because the first chapter was so delightful that I wanted to keep reading it forever. It really captures the liveliness of nature that is hard to express, though this was only meant as a background for the author’s thesis, a sort of preview that he will later argue for.

The following chapter is about phenomenology, which is absolutely crucial to Abram’s philosophy. It starts by giving a brief history of what led to our mechanized world, discussing figures such as Galileo and Descartes, and then covers some phenomenological thinkers who tried to fight against that worldview to recover the human experience. It begins with Husserl, who started the whole movement, but the thinkers he draws from the most are Merleau-Ponty and Heidegger, and the book uses their work very heavily until the end. While still accessible, reading this chapter was a heavy contrast from the first. While the first was an enjoyable journey through the wonders of nature, here you are plunged deep into philosophy. There is a very coherent narrative to such a transition, but it feels odd nevertheless. While I deeply admire phenomenology and am fairly familiar with it, it still wasn’t a very easy chapter and I’m a bit afraid the philosophical shock from it will put some people away. However, it was certainly required as it explores the inherently interactive nature of perception and how objects are seen as “alive” (which is deeply counter-intuitive to our modern worldview) and without it nothing about the book makes sense.

The rest of the book is the articulation of the groundwork laid in these two chapters, showing how phenomenology can help us to regain an almost magical relationship with nature that seems so natural to mankind in its tribal environment and yet so alien to civilized man. The way it does this is incredibly vast, but the main story it tries to tell is that our worldview is by default animist. This isn’t some form of “lower” mode of cognition but is rather deeply attuned to the environment and incredibly functional for its purpose. Man is deeply connected to nature and the land itself through a constant dialogue. In part, what separates the more ancient and the modern worldviews is that the former is an oral culture. The myths and stories tribal people use contain information about the land and how to interact with it, yet they are deeply attuned to it and aren't fixed but rather exist in a constant state of flux. With writing, however, myths began to be fixed on paper. Their dynamic structure was lost as they started to be seen more as precise events. Phonetic literacy, specifically, strengthened this effect even more. Before the Greek invention of vowels, writing itself was still partially dynamic, such as in Hebrew where interpretation was constantly required by the reader, but with modern phonetic language the meaning became fixed.

As our alphabet became increasingly focused on sounds rather than real-world references, language became completely looped and self-referential. In the past, writing was inherently connected to nature as it required real-world phenomena for its descriptions, meaning it was alive and connected to the external world. Now it is dead and only a mirror of itself, trapped in a human-exclusive world. Thus, the animism of nature is now confined to animism in writing, hence why you can “hear” and “see” what you are reading. It is hard to appreciate what the author is trying to show as it is not easily described in a few paragraphs and takes a certain shift of thinking provided by the countless examples he gives of many tribes he personally encountered, such as Australian Aboriginals and their “Dreamtime” stories or the Native American Apache tribes with their interlinked systems of songs, locations, and ancestry. All of it is fascinating from a purely anthropological view even without taking his argument into account.

I don’t agree with everything, for I don’t share his overly positive view of nature and I think he overestimates the “communication” between animals and the land even when only from a purely phenomenological standpoint. But it makes you appreciate certain aspects that you have never thought about or noticed, things you usually can't notice because they are so alien to our culture that they seem outright insane. Afterward, however, the animism of indigenous cultures starts to take on a whole new meaning. It isn’t a book for everyone, though if you are interested in both ecology and philosophy, then you are the perfect match. If you are only interested in one, I think you can still get a lot out of it if you can stand going out of your comfort zone. It’s not a perfect book (there are claims that are hard to accept, sometimes it’s a bit repetitive, and the philosophy can be a bit tough if you’re completely new) but what this book most definitely isn’t is dull or unimaginative. What I liked the most, perhaps even more than the specific content, was the journey it offered: a peek into a completely new and alien world showing the possibility of a lost and magical perception of reality. It is something that has been lost through time and culturally inhibited by our modern language and obsession with technology, but something the author allows you a short glimpse of.
