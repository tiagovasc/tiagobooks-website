**_A book review of&nbsp;_The Discovery of the Unconscious _- Psychosis and Human Nature’ by_ Henri Ellenberger**

It is truly mind-blowing that such a book even exists. Ellenberger set out to do a seemingly impossible task, yet somehow succeeded in tracking the discovery of the unconscious from its beginnings in prehistory up to the late twentieth century. This narrative covers not only the history of dynamic psychiatry itself but expands into the surrounding sociological, economic, political, cultural, medical, and philosophical background. At times it feels like overkill, but it nevertheless provides an incredibly helpful context to understand how and why certain ideas developed. In many chapters, I felt I had just read an entire book on that historical period alone, even outside of psychiatry. And in a way, I did.

The evolution of the unconscious can be tracked culturally from shamanism to exorcism, animal magnetism, hypnotism, prototype dynamic psychiatric systems, and finally full schools of psychotherapeutic thought. The book can be divided into two parts, pre-Freudian and post-Freudian, with roughly half of the book dedicated to each. This means Freud only appears roughly halfway through the volume (which is one thousand pages in total). That alone speaks volumes about the importance of the roots of dynamic psychiatry. It beautifully describes the progression of mankind’s awareness of its biases and projections. While generally not recognized as such, it is a great contribution to scientific thinking in itself, particularly regarding magnetism and hypnosis and their progression through different theories and methods over time, slowly detaching supernatural phenomena and realizing their psychological source.

After the pre-Freudian period concludes, the text mostly covers the four big names of modern psychiatry: the doctrines of Janet, Freud, Adler, and Jung. The fight between the main dynamic systems often had an underlying axiom based on either the Enlightenment or Romanticism, which made a complete union difficult. This was particularly true of Janet and Adler against Jung and Freud. The latter were more theoretical, whereas Janet and Adler made their discoveries in more or less "objective" clinical research by the standards of their time. The book also features beautiful descriptions of Freud and Jung’s creative illnesses, which, if I am not mistaken, is a term coined by Ellenberger himself. By always connecting their thought to the context of the time, the work proves itself a masterpiece. It sometimes dives quite deep into philosophy by explaining the influence of Schopenhauer, Nietzsche, and Heidegger to show how deeply interwoven everything is. If you are passionate about psychiatry or the unconscious in general, this book has ineffable value. It is indeed quite long and takes time and effort, but the writing is excellent and rarely tedious. While the background of individual thinkers can get lengthy, often going over a dozen pages simply describing the family or personality of a single individual, I found it still provided interesting insights. Fortunately, these sections are always easily skippable given that the book is well structured with sections within each chapter.
