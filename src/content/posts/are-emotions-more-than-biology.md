**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_How Emotions Are Made: The Secret Life of the Brain_’ by_ Lisa Feldman Barrett**

In _How Emotions Are Made_, Lisa Feldman Barrett argues for a theory of constructed emotion, suggesting that emotion is not about biological circuits per se—what she calls the classical view—but rather consists of concepts that rely on culture and upbringing. This opposition is based on evidence that Barrett explains early in the book, such as the fact that different emotion categories cannot be specifically and consistently localized to distinct populations of neurons within a single region of the human brain. She points out that instances of an emotion category need not share a population of neurons that are necessary or sufficient to implement them, suggesting instead that the brain constructs these experiences through prediction to make sense of sensory inputs. She provides alternative explanations for evidence that seems to support the classical view, noting that while adult monkeys with amygdala lesions are more likely to explore novel objects, this is not necessarily caused by the lesion of a fear circuit but perhaps because the amygdala simply helps to regulate exploratory behavior. Similarly, experiments where rats learn to freeze in response to a shock might be described as fear circuitry passed to humans, but Barrett argues this is simply Pavlovian conditioning where the "fear" is merely a collection of behaviors appropriate for the situation, and categorizing it as the complex mental state of fear would be a mental inference fallacy.

This is not to say that emotions are not real, but Barrett argues that they do not have distinct essences that are objectively real in a non-human sense. She claims that believing otherwise is a form of essentialism, the idea that things have an innate essence that makes them what they are, and this anti-essentialist stance is a core argument throughout the book. Drawing on Darwin, she notes that before evolutionary theory, variation within a species was considered an error or deviation from the Platonic ideal, whereas Darwin argued that a species is a conceptual category or a population of varied individuals united by a goal rather than an essence. Barrett applies this same population thinking to emotional categories like anger, sadness, fear, and disgust. I found myself struggling to reconcile this with the work of Jaak Panksepp, which claims primary emotions originate primitively from subcortical regions of the brain and are similar across all mammals. I have written a review of his latest book, which you might want to check out, and the interesting part is that Lisa Feldman Barrett and Jaak Panksepp are both world-renowned scientists in their fields, yet their claims clash significantly.

There is a battle in some sense within the field regarding whether emotion exists as biological circuits or as socially constructed concepts. Honestly, I cannot know which one is correct given that even the experts cannot agree among themselves. I am more inclined towards a Pankseppean approach, given that it seems to make the most sense evolutionarily, and I find that a lot of the emotional constructivism camp seems to have ideological roots, a tendency that is shown many times even in Barrett's book. Nevertheless, there are definitely good arguments and lots of evidence on both sides. It is a fascinating topic, and if you are interested in the science of emotion, it is worthwhile to hear both perspectives. While I was trying to puzzle this together, Joseph Carroll, a scholar in literature and evolution, was kind enough to provide me with some resources on how this conflict is playing out in the scientific community. At the end of this article, there are some papers that you might want to check out if you want a deep dive into the topic.
