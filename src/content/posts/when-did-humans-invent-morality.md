**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_Moral Revolution'&nbsp;_by_ Jason Vallotton and Kris Vallotton**

Between 800 and 200 BC, almost every major civilization on the planet underwent a shift that forever changed societies and gave birth to modern man. This period saw the rise of figures ranging from Zarathustra in Persia to Buddha in India, Confucius and Lao-Tse in China, the prophets in Israel, and King Numa in Rome, alongside much of early philosophy involving Zeno and Socrates. Humans became increasingly interested in speculative thought and longed for transcendence, while religion became increasingly abstract, monotheistic, prophetic, and centred around morality. This era is known as the Axial Age, a term coined by the German philosopher Karl Jaspers in the late 1940s. In this book, however, Eugene Halton attempts to restore credit for this discovery to John Stuart Stuart-Glennie in 1873, who originally coined it the "moral revolution."

The book begins by offering evidence and context for Glennie’s discovery before moving into his specific ideas. Most importantly, it covers Panzooinism, which is humankind’s primitive conception of the world and how we related to it. In this view, the Cosmos is alive and one is "immersed in the fabric of life" rather than viewing reality through an isolated individual lens. Other concepts, such as the 500-year cycles of history and the bioticon, are introduced, and further important thinkers in the Axial revolution, such as Mumford and Eric Voegelin, are explored. As the book progresses, it shifts from Halton’s commentary to his own views, culminating largely in the final chapter. Drawing on ideas from Robert Bellah and D.H. Lawrence, it addresses the problems of modern global civilization. At times, the text feels like a denunciation of civilization itself, obsessed with environmentalism and the increasing mechanical view of the universe. While the author claims he isn’t invoking a nostalgic idea of the "noble savage," he seems instead to invoke an image of a noble, even divine, primitive society.

While I disagree with his overall outlook, I found much of his commentary and ideas quite interesting and worth my time, particularly regarding how our perception of the world has evolved throughout history and how different ideas led us to our particular culture and worldview. This "macro" historical analysis of consciousness is incredibly difficult, but it explains large trends in societies over aeons. Despite liking the content of the book, I do regret buying it. I had just learned the concept of the Axial Age and wanted to read more about it, and this was the only promising book I found that wasn’t gigantic. However, it is incredibly expensive, costing £50 for a book of little over 100 pages. In addition, it provided an extensive historical context of the idea itself which was not really my goal and is likely of interest only to historians. If you are in a situation similar to mine, _Our Axial Age_ by Augustson, _Axial Age and Its Consequences_ by Bellah, or _The Three Axial Ages_ by Torpey will likely provide the needed background.
