## Abstract

Kierkegaard is the philosopher who most refuses to stay inside philosophy. His life keeps bleeding into his work, his domineering father, his broken engagement with Regina Olsen, his gift for indirection, and Alexander Jack argues this is precisely the point. In this conversation, Jack traces how Kierkegaard’s three defining personal crises generated his most important ideas: indirect communication, the leap of faith, and the teleological suspension of the ethical. He corrects the dominant misreading of Fear and Trembling, Kierkegaard did not break with Regina because he was like Abraham; he broke with her because he lacked the faith Abraham had, and writing Fear and Trembling was his reckoning with that failure. The conversation moves from textual translation disputes (why “intend” not “want” changes everything in Problema I) to the live question of whether faith is a rejection of practical reason, and ends on Kierkegaard’s relevance for addiction, identity transformation, and why some leaps cannot be argued into.

## Who Is Alexander Jack?

Alexander Jack is a philosopher and Kierkegaard scholar at the University of Notre Dame. Originally from Seattle, he came to Kierkegaard through an accidental summer teaching assignment in existentialism, a moment he describes as classically Kierkegaardian. He subsequently learned Danish and has published a new translation of Fear and Trembling, notable for its 200-page glossary and a series of corrective translation choices developed in response to student needs. His research focuses on Kierkegaard’s ethics, indirect communication, and the philosophy of faith.

## Top 3 Thesis

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Kierkegaard’s philosophy is inseparable from his personal suffering. His relationship with his father, his broken engagement with Regina Olsen, and his lifelong practice of indirection are not background biography, they are the engine of his thought. The universal emerges, as Kierkegaard himself noted, precisely because he worked through what was most personal.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; The dominant interpretation of Fear and Trembling is backwards. Kierkegaard did not see himself as Abraham sacrificing Isaac. He saw himself as the man who lacked Abraham’s faith. Had he possessed it, he would have married Regina. Fear and Trembling is not a self-justification but a confession of failure and a map of what he should have done.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; The paradox of Problema I cannot be dissolved, only correctly understood. Translating the key Danish word as “want” rather than “intend” collapses the distinction between temptation and sin, and makes Abraham’s situation incoherent. Once “intend” is restored, the paradox holds: Abraham does not want to kill Isaac, does not wish to, but fully intends to, and believes simultaneously that it will all work out.

## Key Insights

**1. Kierkegaard’s philosophy is autobiography elevated to the universal.**

Three biographical factors shape everything: his father’s oppressive religiosity (which robbed Kierkegaard of childhood while giving him an acute sense of sin), his engagement and breakup with Regina Olsen (the crisis that generated his greatest books), and his lifelong practice of indirection (first a defense mechanism, then a philosophical method). Jack argues these are not incidental details but the generative pressure behind the entire authorship. Kierkegaard himself noted that what was most personal had become, through providence, universal, the same way Socrates’ private intellectual problems became the birthplace of Western philosophy.

**2. The correct reading of Fear and Trembling inverts the popular one.**

The dominant interpretation holds that Kierkegaard saw himself as Abraham: he had a higher calling (to become an author), so he sacrificed his relationship with Regina the way Abraham sacrificed Isaac. Jack dismantles this. Kierkegaard’s own journals state plainly: “I lacked faith in my relationship with Regina.” Had he possessed faith, he would have married her, that would have been the Abrahamic move, trusting that God would bless an impossible situation. Fear and Trembling is not a defence of what he did. It is a map of what he failed to do and a reckoning with that failure. Writing it was itself a form of indirect communication aimed at Regina, who saw through it entirely.

**3. “Intend” not “want”, a translation mistake that breaks Problema I.**

Nearly every English translation of Fear and Trembling renders a key Danish word as “want”: “as soon as the individual wants to assert himself in his singularity, he sins.” Jack shows this creates an impossible muddle. Abraham does not want to kill Isaac; he loves Isaac. And if wanting to sin equals sinning, then temptation and sin become identical, which is incoherent. Jack’s translation restores the word as “intend.” Abraham intends to perform the act (attempted murder is a crime just as murder is), but does not want to and does not wish to. This distinction is not pedantic: it is what makes the paradox of Problema I coherent and preserves the genuine difficulty of the Abrahamic situation.

**4. The teleological suspension of the ethical cannot be dissolved, only inhabited.**

The paradox of Problema I is that Abraham intends an act that looks like murder, without wanting it, wishing it, or having any felt desire for it, while simultaneously believing it will all be fine. Practical reason always works by causal probability: raise a knife, kill a child. Abraham is not operating within practical reason. He is trusting that God will act in a way no prior experience could justify. Jack contrasts this with the doctor who stops a child’s heart to perform surgery: the doctor’s action is quantifiable, probabilistic, and legible to reason. Abraham’s is not. The link between the act and the blessing simply cannot be seen, and that invisibility is the structure of faith itself.

**5. The leap is not only into faith, it is the structure of all identity transformation.**

Jack connects Kierkegaard’s leap to a problem the host identifies from his time as a personal trainer: incremental habit change fails for some people because the step is too small to justify itself. What those people need is not a better plan but a transformation of identity, becoming the kind of person who exercises, rather than deciding to exercise. Kierkegaard thought all sin enters through a leap, and that once you leap into an identity (including addiction), you cannot simply leap out. You need an external resource. The model he returns to in his late writing is the unnamed woman who is a sinner in Luke 7: she comes forward not because she has been argued into it, but because she senses she is loved, and that possibility of being loved opens the capacity for transformation. Philosophically, what is needed is the recognition of a possible self. Theologically, what enables that recognition is encounter with love.
