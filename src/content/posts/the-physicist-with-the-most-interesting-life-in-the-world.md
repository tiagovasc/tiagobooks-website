**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_Surely You’re Joking, Mr. Feynman!_’ by_ Richard Feynman**

I picked this book up some time ago and promptly left it unread on my shelf. When I finally decided to crack it open, I didn’t even remember why I had acquired it in the first place, or for that matter, who the hell Feynman was. For those in my position, Richard Feynman was a famous American theoretical physicist who received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1965 for his work in quantum electrodynamics. Ignorant of this at the time, I found myself confused about why I would be interested enough to buy it, and I almost decided to skip it entirely. However, a quick check of its high rating on Goodreads convinced me to trust the judgment of the masses. I am glad I did, because while this is roughly an autobiography, it is structured in a far better and funnier way than the genre usually permits. These are primarily stories transcribed from recorded audio conversations Feynman had with his friend Ralph Leighton, and the result is incredible because his life was bafflingly interesting. One tends to imagine a theoretical physicist as a super-intelligent nerd who does nothing except surround himself with math papers for hours on end. I am sure he did plenty of that, but he also managed to do a whole lot more.

It almost makes me feel bad about my own life reading this because he has done and experienced so many things across so many different disciplines that it seems impossible that this was lived by a single person. While a few stories relate directly to physics—where one can marvel at his genius—many are completely random to his career. Throughout the book, we see him conducting experiments with ants as a kid, causing confusion in a philosophy lecture regarding the nature of "essential objects" like bricks, and learning how to pick locks until he could open safes containing secrets to the atomic bomb during his time at Los Alamos. He temporarily switches careers to biology just for the fun of it, learns traditional Brazilian music and African drumming well enough to perform, takes up nude drawing, attempts to decipher Mayan hieroglyphs, and even experiences hallucinations in a sensory deprivation tank aided by weed and ketamine. Some of these tales are so bizarre that they initially feel made up, but once the context and history are explained, you get a sense of how his relentless curiosity led him into these situations. It really made me eager for new experiences, a desire which does not come naturally to me at all.

All the stories were incredibly interesting, but most of all, his personality is delightful. He seems authentic and manages to be unique without really trying to fit a specific mold. His passion for science and general curiosity were fascinating and contagious, even more so taking into account the time it was written, long before being "pro-science" became a common cultural trope. The fact that he lived in the middle of the 20th century also increased his appeal to me, as it was captivating to read stories that would now seem out of place and to observe how our culture and social norms have changed. I do have a couple of reservations about the book, primarily regarding the technical depth. While many stories are outside the realm of theoretical physics, some are not, and those sections can get technical to the point where I often had no bloody idea what he was talking about. Personally, that didn’t bother me because the technical details weren’t required to enjoy the narrative, but I predict this may bother some readers if they dislike reading something they cannot fully grasp.

While I found his personality delightful, I admit that at times it can feel a bit extreme. I saw some reviews from readers who found him arrogant or annoying, which ruined the book for them. I totally disagree with that assessment, but somehow I completely understand the sentiment. It is likely a very personal reaction where you either like him or you don’t, and you have to try to immerse yourself in his life to know for sure. Ultimately, it was tons of fun to read, and it is one of the few books I wouldn’t mind re-reading a few years down the line. For people who like to learn, play pranks, or most importantly, for people who like to solve problems and tinker with things, Feynman will look like the most interesting person in the world. You will be hard-pressed to stop reading page after page.
