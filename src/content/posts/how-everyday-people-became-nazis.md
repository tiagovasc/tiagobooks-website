**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_Ordinary Men: Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the Final Solution in Poland_’ by_ Christopher Browning**

Christopher Browning’s _Ordinary Men: Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the Final Solution in Poland_ focuses on a Nazi Germany paramilitary formation involved in both the killing and "resettlement" of Jews in occupied Poland. Unlike many other accounts that chronicle the horrors of the Holocaust through the lens of fanatical SS officers, this book offers a disturbing perspective on how average individuals become perpetrators. It is often easier to explain the Holocaust when the perpetrators are members of police battalions comprising highly Nazified age cohorts—young men steeped in the ideology—or products of selective recruiting and intense indoctrination. However, Reserve Police Battalion 101 possessed none of these characteristics. They were middle-aged, working-class men from Hamburg who were too old for the regular army. Consequently, their atrocities cannot be blamed on years of brainwashing, even if that contributed. This is what the title refers to. They were simply "ordinary" men without a predisposition to commit such violence. What I found most baffling about the battalion is the polarity it displays. During their first major assignment—the massacre at Józefów—the very knowledge of their task was disturbing to them. Major Trapp, the commander, had tears in his eyes as he explained the job they were about to perform. He even made an extraordinary offer: those who did not want to take part could step out. Yet very few outright refused. They did not want to leave the "dirty" work to their colleagues or appear weak in front of their peers.

The psychological evolution of the unit is terrifying to witness. After that first killing, many men felt physically nauseous, and some started to miss targets intentionally. In subsequent assignments, alcohol became an increasing coping mechanism, and there was a heavy sense of shame and horror among the men. However, this distress did not apply to everyone, and more importantly, it did not last. As they undertook more assignments and the methods of killing changed, often becoming less personal, the officers became increasingly detached. A psychological distance grew between "us," the humans, and "them," the Jews, until it eventually became purely routine work. Within the battalion, distinct groups emerged. A very tiny minority refused the job outright, fewer than 12 in 500, while others avoided it when possible. Alarmingly, others volunteered, and sometimes there were so many volunteers that some had to be turned away. I was not expecting the book’s explicit mention of social psychology experiments, particularly those by Stanley Milgram regarding obedience to authority. While these studies are obviously inspired by the events of the twentieth century, there is an undeniable parallel. I was happy to see that the book did not only stick to history but tried to make connections with psychological science for a more holistic and deeper understanding of human behavior.

In the afterword, Browning dives into Daniel Goldhagen’s criticisms of this book which appeared a few years after the publication of _Ordinary Men_. Goldhagen argued that the Holocaust was built upon a specifically German "eliminationist" anti-semitic culture and viewed Browning's thesis as fundamentally flawed. The author gives robust counterarguments to this criticism. Perhaps the strongest is that there were countless cases of atrocities by the battalion committed not only against Jews but also against Poles. There was no unique "cultural-cognitive" prerequisite to commit genocide. It would be comforting if that were the case, but unfortunately, the capacity for evil runs much deeper than a specific population or culture. The ultimate thesis is that the massacres were largely driven by obedience to authority and peer pressure. While this section felt less narrative and more like a discussion among historians, becoming more academic than what I initially signed up for, I started to enjoy it as it progressed. It emphasized the difficulty and importance of interpreting historical events. It asks crucial questions that are far from obvious, such as the degree to which antisemitism was fundamental to Nazism versus how much it was already present in German culture and merely weaponized. The text navigates the dialogue of where the evil of the Holocaust originates: human nature or ideology. Both play a role, but it is difficult to map exactly how much space they occupy without overshadowing one another.

As obsessed as humanity rightfully became with these atrocities after the twentieth century, and considering how deeply this impacted psychology and philosophy, I couldn't help but wonder if that scrutiny was enough. This is especially true when observing so many parallels with current far-right politics. I am not necessarily saying modern actors are Nazis just because they are on the political right, but it is impossible to neglect the similarities, just as it is important to see the key differences. Even within Nazi Germany, the ideology had layers. Not all Nazis were antisemitic, and people who were antisemitic were not necessarily pro-genocide. It is wrong to assume a single continuum of ideology, yet it is somewhat naive to think these levels are independent and fixed. I really enjoyed the book and highly recommend it. It covers an important topic that one should visit often, not just to know what happened historically but to fight the potential for such darkness within your own soul. It is not light reading by any means, though it is not the worst of its kind since it maintains a strong narrative flow and avoids shock for its own sake. If you do not want to dedicate too much time to the dense academic debates, you might skip the preface and the afterword. This would reduce the book to about 200 pages while still delivering the most important insights. I also suggest the paperback instead of the audiobook, which I used, as the latter lacks the organization for the accompanying images.
