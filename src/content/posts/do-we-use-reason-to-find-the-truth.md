**_A book review of&nbsp;‘The Enigma of Reason’ by Mercier and Sperber_**

The standard view, going as far back as the Ancient Greeks, is that we humans possess the unique ability of reason. It is the defining characteristic of human nature, according to Aristotle. Through reason, we make sense of the world and remain objective. It is almost a theological view of rationality that views it as divine. This perspective still permeates the current culture to a large degree, where rationality is viewed as a sort of evolutionary blessing that separates us from animals. It is considered a general purpose intelligence that allows the individual to better understand the world and make better choices. Even though we know we have an irrational side, it is believed we also have the ability to think clearly and rationally, illustrated by Kahneman’s famous System 1 and System 2. This is what the authors call the intellectualist model.

Hume went against the tide and famously said that reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions. This is more in line with what modern psychology has shown us, as the list of cognitive biases is endless. While reason can indeed be deeply flawed, it is an incomplete picture, and the goal of the book is to view reason in a new light. Reason is a mechanism of intuitive inference. It is one module of inference among many. It is not a logical system, as most view it. The first half of the book explains in great depth what logic is and how it relates poorly to rationality. It is also not a probability calculus. It is a metacognitive module that works in a narrow domain, making unconscious inferences based on regular patterns in the world.

Humans use reason to convince themselves and others. It is made and used for social consumption rather than in a scientific sense of epistemology. It is an adaptation to our rich social lives and powerful language, where individuals have competing interests. The authors call this an interactionist model. Its function is epistemic vigilance, meaning to evaluate the claims of others and improve our own reasons to better convince them. While reason is indeed flawed on many levels, just as pop psychology claims, it is mostly because we don’t understand its function. An example given in the book is that someone might think a pen is broken while he is using it upside down.

Humans are biased and produce reasons almost exclusively for their side. The quality control is poor. It is lazy and seeks the least amount of effort to justify one’s intuition. Yet in a social context, this changes. We are much less biased when evaluating other people’s reasons. We can accept challenging reasons if they are strong enough, and people tend to demand good enough reasons from others. While it seems flawed in isolation, once we take a group view, this allows us to divide the cognitive labour by each person arguing their own side and be pragmatic about reasons, accepting them if they are good enough. It is a cost effective way to solve social disagreements.

It is a fantastic book, although hard to summarize and at times very dense, particularly in the first half which is quite heavy on logic. It balances out both the overly optimistic and overly pessimistic views of reason in a new paradigm that makes evolutionary sense. Finally, it is worth noting that this book is involved in some controversy. It has been claimed that it was stolen in large part from the work of Gregg Henriques. I happened to already own the book when I learned this, and I didn’t know any other book that touches on the same topic at this level of depth, so I decided to use it regardless. If the claims are true, it is likely better to learn this topic elsewhere.
