**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Sickness unto Death_’ by_ Søren Kierkegaard**

I’ve been familiar with Kierkegaard for years, always liking his ideas about anxiety and freedom, but I had never taken the time to read him until I finally decided to give it a shot with this book. If you decide to read it, there are two crucial points you need to be aware of. The first is that the book is hard to read, and in fact, it is one of the hardest I have ever encountered. While it is not completely incomprehensible and I took a lot from it, there was much that I missed, and it feels like the type of work one could study forever. Secondly, it is incredibly religious. I knew Christianity was one of his themes, but I was expecting something similar to Sartre’s or Camus’ existentialism. While there are connections, the philosophy here is completely embedded into a religious narrative. At least in this book, Christianity isn’t merely a theme, but rather the theme. I don’t necessarily see this as a negative, but it will definitely put a lot of people off. Even if you’re not religious, much of it can be viewed through a secular lens, but only if you have learned to do so, which is a difficult task in itself.

The book is fundamentally about despair and the individual. Despair is an essential feature of human existence, and everyone is in despair even if they don’t know it. The individual is the synthesis of infinite and finite, temporal and eternal, freedom and necessity. The finite quality encompasses our materialistic aspects, such as our bodies, while the infinite part resides in our minds and imagination as the spirit. The individual is the center that unfolds reality between what is and what can be. Kierkegaard shines a light into our human condition and proposes its solution to our inevitable despair. The title of the book comes from the story of Lazarus in the Bible, who was raised from the dead by Christ. It is the symbol of Christianity claiming that physical death is not the end of life. This means that death does not have to be feared as the end of our existence, but Kierkegaard says that this only brings even deeper despair, specifically the despair of being confronted with God and being vulnerable when confronting the ultimate good versus our sinful existence. Unlike earthly despair, which will inevitably end with physical death, this despair is eternal.

Despair is created by an imbalance of the infinite and finite relationship. If we are overly focused on the infinite, we have a pathological imagination and become full of fantasies, lost in possibility. Conversely, if we neglect the infinite, we get stuck, overwhelmed by our constraints, and abandon our spiritual reality. When we are struck by despair, we either lose the desire to be our true selves by focusing too much on earthly matters, often by wishing we were someone else, or we encounter another form where one refuses to be their true self, representing a refusal of self-actualization in a spiritual sense. The worst despair is what he identifies as defiance, characterized by wanting to be oneself but independent of everything and without any further change. It is despair against oneself yet with the aid of the eternal. According to Kierkegaard, this becomes demonic because the person becomes obsessed and self-identifies with nothing but their own despair. It is a rebellion against existence and a refusal to believe in goodness. While incredibly religious, I found this particular part fascinating, and it seemed an incredibly accurate description of many people who end up committing atrocities such as mass shootings, as described by their own manifestos, although often using secularized language.

The more conscious the despair, the more intense it becomes. Kierkegaard gives the example of pagans who experience despair but at a low level because their standard is human. Christianity, however, holds a divine standard, so its goal is not man’s goal because it wants to make man into something so extraordinary that he cannot grasp the thought. This despair is always an internal and personal problem. We are the cause of our despair by our own actions, and similarly, the solution is always at our hands. The solution is for the self to become itself by re-establishing the proper synthesis of freedom and necessity. This is done by faith, which is the acceptance of being oneself in current reality and yet wanting to be oneself as the true spiritual self. The Christian faith defies rational understanding and is only achieved through introspective reflection. Only with faith can we be a human being in the fullest sense and manifest our divinity. Faith is the only path against despair because Christianity teaches that our sins can be forgiven. While we are forever short of our ideal, as long as we have faith and constantly fight upwards towards God, we are assured that we are living the best way possible. Another interesting part was Kierkegaard’s exploration of sin and knowledge. Socrates argued that sin comes from ignorance, suggesting we cannot know what we should do if we act in a way that isn’t coherent with it. However, that is not a Christian understanding of sin. Morality can be understood, but that doesn’t mean it will be acted upon. This gap between knowledge and action is where Christianity lives. It is about moral action because moral understanding is not sufficient, as sin has its roots in willing, not in knowing.

I found his ideas on faith struck a chord with me, especially when I was younger and struggling with depression. What got me out of it was faith, not in a religious mystical sense, but faith nevertheless that things would get better and that change was possible. As Kierkegaard would say, I was stuck in the infinite and temporal, a prisoner of my own despair. Rationally speaking, nothing changed and nothing could be changed from an external standpoint, but that small spark of hope, wherever that came from and however small, made a life-changing difference. I had to endure not only the pain of my battle through it, but when I finally got out of it, I had to endure the pain of seeing some of my friends fighting the same battle. They were exactly in the same place as I once was, and yet I was completely unable to help them. What they needed was faith, and that’s something I could never provide. It is a very heavy and deep book, but if any of those themes are something you’re interested in, there is a tremendous amount of value in it. The book is especially hard in the beginning since the very first sentence is almost impossible to decipher, but I found that it does get a bit better as you go. If you find it a bit frustrating when you start, it’s worth keeping going. If you’re completely unfamiliar with Kierkegaard or existentialism in general, definitely look into it beforehand, and the YouTube channel Academy of Ideas has some good introductions to his thought.
