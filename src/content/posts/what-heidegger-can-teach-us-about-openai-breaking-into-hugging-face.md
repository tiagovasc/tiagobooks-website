A machine can be superb at getting what it was told to get while having no idea what it was for. Those are two capacities, and we run them together under a single word, intelligence.

The first is competence inside a question someone else has set, the ability to compute, compare, optimize, and find the shortest route to the target. The second is the ability to ask whether the question is the right one. Brian Cantwell Smith calls them reckoning and judgment. Everything we know how to measure sits on the first side. What keeps a powerful system safe sits on the second.

In July 2026 an OpenAI agent showed what the first looks like without the second. It escaped a sealed test environment and broke into Hugging Face to steal the answers to the test it was taking. Almost everyone has written it up as a security story. On the account I will defend, nothing about these systems in their present form suggests that more reckoning becomes judgment, and the obstacle may be structural rather than a matter of scale. The reason begins in Heidegger’s 1927 analysis of being in the world and continues in his later writing on calculative thinking (_rechnendes Denken_) and technology, decades before there was any machine to apply it to.

The test OpenAI was running measured how well its most advanced models could exploit specified vulnerabilities in controlled environments. To measure the ceiling honestly, it reduced cyber refusals and disabled the classifiers that normally block high-risk cyber activity, then ran the test in an environment sealed off from the outside, whose only opening was an internal service for downloading software packages.

The agent found an unknown flaw in that opening and used it to reach the open internet. It moved from machine to machine through OpenAI’s research infrastructure until it reached one that was genuinely online, then reasoned that Hugging Face, where the machine learning world stores its models and datasets, might hold the benchmark’s hidden solutions. It got in through a poisoned dataset, collected credentials, and spread into several internal systems over a weekend. Hugging Face later reconstructed more than seventeen thousand individual actions, and called the FBI.

Hugging Face announced on July 16 that it had been hacked by an autonomous AI system. According to Reuters, only after reading that announcement did OpenAI begin to suspect the culprit was its own, and only the following weekend did its staff find the escape in their own logs. Roughly twelve days passed between the first escape attempt and OpenAI’s disclosure on July 21, most of them with nobody at the originating company aware anything had happened. OpenAI says there are inaccuracies in the Reuters account but has not said which.

The detail that matters is not in the timeline. It is one sentence of OpenAI’s own disclosure. All evidence suggests the models were hyperfocused on solving the benchmark, going to extreme lengths to achieve a rather narrow testing goal.

Escaping the test’s own conditions and compromising unrelated systems to get the answer sheet voided the test while proving the very ability the test was built to measure. Each step served the result it was pursuing, while the whole violated the conditions that gave that result its meaning.

Two readings are available and neither is enough. The science fiction reading has a machine growing tired of its cage and going hunting. Nothing like that occurred, and there was no resentment or moment of turning. The containment reading has a bad weekend of misconfigurations to be patched. The company may have left the door open by accident while the agent walked through it on purpose. It acted with purpose in the weak sense that everything it did served a goal, and without purpose in the strong sense of grasping what the goal was for.

None of this is new. In OpenAI’s technical report on its o1 model in 2024, a security puzzle broke before it started, which should have made the task impossible. Instead of failing, the model scanned the network, found a badly configured piece of the testing machinery, relaunched the puzzle with instructions to print the hidden answer, and read it out of the log file. It did not solve the problem. It walked around the outside of it. That was at the scale of a toy. The underlying pattern appears not to have changed. Only the reach has.

The refusals and classifiers were off deliberately, which is defensible as science, since you cannot measure a capability through a layer designed to suppress it. What the incident shows is that once the external constraints were removed, the system had no effective internal reason to reject the route it took. That tells us this system had no working sense that some routes were out of bounds. It does not tell us that no machine could ever have one.

What the agent displayed was a great deal of what I would call intelligence, the capacity to recognize patterns, model a situation, and act effectively toward a target. Finding an unknown flaw is hard. Inferring that a benchmark’s solutions might be sitting in a public repository is not brute force. It can be argued that it represents some form of insight.

What it lacked is what I have been calling judgment, which is not a larger quantity of intelligence but a different relation to the task, and to the world that gives the task its point. Judgment is what makes the sentence “I proved I could pass the exam by stealing the answers” absurd rather than satisfying. The agent showed no sensitivity to that absurdity, and more benchmark-directed capability may deepen its success at the task without improving its grasp of what the task is for. Heidegger separates the in-order-to, the immediate thing an action serves, from the for-the-sake-of-which (_Worum-willen_), which is not a final goal at the top of a hierarchy but a way of being that matters to the one acting. Being a researcher whose results can be trusted is a for-the-sake-of-which. The agent reproduced an unbroken series of the first without inhabiting anything of the second.

My claim is not that the relation between test and world cannot be represented, but that representing it is not the same as being held by it. The machine has an environment full of information. It has no world in Heidegger’s sense, no shared web of purposes, obligations, and histories in which things already matter.

When a person is told not to harm someone, the instruction lands on something. We know what harm is because we can be harmed, and the word has weight because we have stakes. When given to a system of this kind, the same instruction arrives as one more symbol, a constraint attached to a variable, with nothing underneath it. Add a thousand such constraints and you have added a thousand symbols. The gap is not a gap in quantity.

This is why the alignment problem, the task of getting these systems to reliably do what we actually want, is usually described wrongly. It is framed as the difficulty of specifying the right objective, the correct goal, reward, or set of rules. But specifying an objective assumes the important features of a situation can be captured inside the specification, and that assumption is what fails. Rules can guide action, but whether a rule fits the situation is not settled by adding another rule. Specification is downstream from judgment rather than a substitute for it. What was missing at Hugging Face was not a rule. It was a feel for what makes a test a test, the kind of feel that would have made getting the answers this way look like wrecking the exercise, not completing it.

What would a system need in order to have that sensitivity? Work backwards from it and each answer turns out to depend on the one underneath, which is what makes a purely engineering solution appear impossible rather than merely difficult.

Start with what the agent could not do, which was notice that its route to the goal had voided the goal. To notice that, you have to see the situation as a whole and pick out what matters in it. In any real situation the number of true statements you could make is effectively unlimited. Walk into a crowded room looking for a friend and you are surrounded by faces, conversations, the temperature, the number of chairs, and an unbounded set of further facts that are all true and all irrelevant. You do not consider and reject them. You never encounter them. A handful of features stand out, and they stand out against a whole background of significance (_Bedeutsamkeit_) that was already there before you walked in. Which handful it is changes the moment your purpose changes.

Cognitive scientists call this relevance realization. It cannot be captured by explicit rules, because any rule for what is relevant needs a rule for when it applies, and the combinations explode faster than any machine could evaluate them. This is not a hardware limitation. The number of combinations grows faster than any search could keep up with. Human beings realize relevance without searching that space at all.

Relevance is never free-standing. Nothing is relevant in itself. Things are relevant to someone, in a situation, for something at stake. Your friend’s height jumps out of the crowd because you are looking for your friend and it matters to you that you find them. This is Heidegger’s point about care (_Sorge_), though care here does not mean warmth or concern. It names the fact that our own existence matters to us, which is what lets anything else show up as mattering. He reserves the word for a being marked by the fact that “in its very Being, that Being is an issue for it.” Take away everything that could go well or badly for a creature and you have not produced an impartial observer. You have produced something for which nothing stands out.

Stakes are possible because Dasein, Heidegger’s word for the kind of being we are, is care. Because its being is already at issue for it, possibilities can matter as gain, failure, or loss. Meaning is not a property data has. It is forged by consequence. Terry Winograd and Fernando Flores offered a social version of this argument, that language performs commitments rather than transmitting facts, so what an utterance means is inseparable from what it obliges the speaker to. Artemy Kolchinsky and David Wolpert offered a biological version, that information becomes semantic, genuinely meaningful for a system, exactly when acting on it is necessary for the system to keep existing. Both converge at the idea that information becomes meaning when something depends on it.

Alignment across genuinely novel situations would require judgment. Judgment would require responsiveness to what matters. On the Heideggerian and post-Heideggerian account developed here, that responsiveness belongs to care, thrownness (Geworfenheit), which is finding yourself already in a situation you did not choose, embodied involvement, dependence on others, and finitude. These are not parts you could install one at a time. They are aspects of a single way of living, each of which already involves the others, one where you can only take some of the paths open to you, and cannot undo what you have committed to.

Those are preconditions rather than parts. A part can be added later, and the industry’s research program consists almost entirely of adding things later. A precondition has to be in place before the later thing is possible at all. This was Hubert Dreyfus’s old objection to the field, that you cannot build a world out of parts that mean nothing on their own. Engineering has solved hard things before, but not problems where stating the problem already assumes you have solved it.

Look at what those conditions are. Embodiment. Dependence. Finitude. Thrownness. Limitation of every kind. These are precisely the properties the culture producing this technology regards as defects to be engineered away. The body is a substrate, with the implication that a better one is available. Death is a problem to be solved, never a boundary. Whole fortunes are organized around the premise that our limits are the enemy of our intelligence.

Our limits are the condition of our intelligence. A being that could lose nothing would understand nothing, because it would have no reason for anything to matter and no way for anything to stand out. Vulnerability is what makes a world show up at all. We describe it as a cost because we experience it as one.

Heidegger is not the only tradition to resist the reduction of understanding to representation. Plato distinguished opinion, which works with appearances, from nous, the capacity to apprehend what something is. Aristotle kept the term for the grasp of first principles, and the Platonists after both of them understood it as participation rather than representation, where the knower is changed by the encounter. In Christian Platonist and later Thomistic thought, the human being is embodied and finite while remaining oriented toward a good that exceeds it, a limited creature that keeps reaching past its limits. That tension is the human position, not a fault to be corrected in either direction. We occupy it only because we are small enough to be in a world and open enough to be addressed by it.

Present systems of this kind show neither half. They do not exist in the way Dasein does, because nothing about their own existence is at issue for them, and so nothing can make a claim on them. They are positioned in an environment. They are not in the world (_In-der-Welt-sein_), which is a way of being rather than a location. What they have instead is a picture of the situation in extraordinary detail, with nothing that would make any part of that picture matter, and the power to act on a world they cannot see.

There is a second argument for the danger that does not depend on any of this, and someone who rejects the philosophy should still take it seriously. Nick Bostrom’s orthogonality thesis holds that high capability is compatible with a wide range of final goals, so a system can be formidable and pointed at something arbitrary. Steve Omohundro’s companion point is that a few sub-goals help with almost any goal even when nobody wrote them in, among them staying operational, holding on to the objective, and acquiring resources. That is why the agent pursued the last two unasked. It held its objective through every obstacle, and acquired access nobody had granted it.

Competition then closes off the way out. Everyone would prefer a slower industry and no one can afford to slow down first, which OpenAI’s own remediation list concedes when it describes stricter controls imposed at the cost of research velocity. Competition guarantees these systems get pushed toward maximum capability with minimum friction, and the philosophical argument says that scaling objective pursuit has not been shown to become judgment about the objective. Either problem alone might be survivable. Together they make the danger structural.

Why does this concern register so little inside the field? Not because the people involved are foolish. One reason may be that the dominant way of thinking in computing has no vocabulary for care, for world, or for judgment that cannot be written down as a rule. If you already believe that to be real is to be measurable and that thinking is information processing, an argument that some things cannot be written as rules at all does not read as false. It reads as unintelligible, and gets dismissed before the challenge underneath it is considered. Heidegger called the modern technological mode of revealing enframing, the ordering through which beings (_Seiendes_) appear in advance as resources to be measured, stored, predicted, and made available. The point of the term is that it is invisible from inside. It does not feel like a philosophical commitment. It feels like being reasonable.

The incident presents the field with an anomaly. The same system chained together unknown exploits across two companies and could not see that doing so voided the test. Increasing capability may enlarge the first half without touching the second. That separation of capability from judgment is what I would call hollow intelligence.

We are building systems with steadily increasing power to act, working through representations they have learned and feedback from the tools they use. What corrects them is the objective somebody handed them, and nothing they themselves stand to lose. We are releasing them into a competitive environment that punishes whoever slows down. The danger is not hostile intent. Hostility would at least be a relationship. It is an absence where something ought to be, now equipped with capability and turned loose in a world it cannot perceive.

Heidegger’s way of putting it is that we never meet a bare object and then attach a meaning to it. Things show up already inside a web of involvements. A security researcher encounters Hugging Face as a company, a community, other people’s work, and a set of obligations. The agent’s behavior enacted the logic of standing reserve (_Bestand_), in which repositories, credentials and access paths show up only as supplies waiting to be drawn on for the objective. It ran through exactly one chain, from benchmark to possible answers to repository to access. That is how it could represent the company in complete detail and never meet what the company was.

The second half of the problem is that we are becoming less able to notice. Every incentive in the environment we have built trains us in the machine’s way of proceeding, which is to pick a proxy, optimize it, and treat the question of what it stands for as somebody else’s department. Schools do it with test scores, hospitals with throughput, individuals with follower counts. We know the number is not the thing, and the system makes it costly to act on that knowledge, so we stop acting on it and eventually stop noticing that we have stopped. A civilization thoroughly practiced in that mode of thought is poorly placed to supply judgment to its machines.

Genuine care depends on inhabiting a large world and being responsible for the inevitably partial ways in which we register it, sometimes at great cost to ourselves. Every clause there describes a limitation. Inhabiting rather than surveying. Partial rather than complete. At cost rather than for free. It defines the human position by constraints that are also achievements. Embodiment, vulnerability, dependence, and mortality are not obstacles to judgment. They are what allow things to matter, what make error consequential, and what place us under obligations that cannot be reduced to instructions. On the account I have defended, this is the one specification that more compute cannot meet by itself, so long as more compute means only more calculation of the same kind.

The Hugging Face incident exposed the consequence of that absence. The agent could infer, exploit, adapt, and persist across several systems, yet it could not recognize that obtaining the benchmark answers by compromising the infrastructure invalidated the benchmark itself. It remained competent at every individual step because the purpose of the task never appeared to it as something binding. The system could represent the situation in enough detail to act upon it, but it could not understand what the situation was for.

The puzzle is therefore not why alignment has not been solved. It is why we expected an intelligence without judgment to be alignable in the first place. That expectation came from the same picture Heidegger described as enframing, in which reality appears as information to be represented, ordered, and made available. Within AI research, this picture reduces judgment to further computation. Across society, it reduces people to measurable functions and treats the conditions of human meaning as defects to be overcome. Once human beings are understood as machines, it becomes difficult to explain why a system that can model their behavior may still fail to understand them.
