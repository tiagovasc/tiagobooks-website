What if magic is not superstition but a coherent system of psychotechnologies waiting for cognitive science to catch up? Juensung Kim, PhD candidate at the University of Toronto, argues that the toolbox of a practising magician, ritual, trance, meditation, divination, maps onto established research programmes in psychology, anthropology, and cognitive science, and that the real project is getting those disciplines to talk to each other. The conversation moves from the etymology of magic and its contested relationship with sorcery and shamanism, through the rubber-hand illusion and the concept of the astral body, to divination systems as externalised pattern-recognition tools, the dangers of unstructured magical practice, and the open question of what makes people aspire to genuine self-transformation in the first place.

## Who Is Juensung Kim?

Juensung Kim is a PhD candidate at the University of Toronto whose research focuses on the phenomenology and mechanisms of self-transformation, with particular attention to practices, including magic, meditation, and ritual, that facilitate that process. His work bridges cognitive science, philosophy, and the study of esotericism, and sits within an emerging fourth camp in the academic study of magic that seeks to integrate the psychological, sociological, and anthropological traditions into a unified research programme. He is also a practitioner of historical European martial arts, tai chi, and sword-based disciplines.

## Top 3 Theses

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Magic is not a single phenomenon but a family of practices that every major civilisation abstracted from shamanism and ran with in different directions, and when you strip away the cultural theoretical layer and apply a cognitive-science lens, Eastern and Western practical magic look remarkably similar, which is exactly what a unified cognitive science of magic would predict.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Randomised divination systems such as tarot and the I Ching function not by revealing a hidden cosmic order but by externalising the practitioner's own pattern-recognition capacity: the symbolism acts as flyaper for thoughts, allowing the practitioner to examine intuitions they could not otherwise hold at arm's length.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; The deeper problem beneath both magical practice and self-improvement culture is not how to make people better once they want to change, but how to make people want to change at all, and almost no research addresses that prior question.

## Key Insights

**1. The cognitive science of magic is not a new project, it is an integration problem.**

Kim’s central methodological claim is that the academic disciplines that study magic, psychology, sociology, anthropology, and now cognitive science, have largely produced their research in isolation. His argument is that the practitioner’s actual toolkit (ritual, trance, visualisation, divination, meditation) already has established scientific literature behind each component. The work remaining is synthesis: taking what psychologists know about ritual and mindfulness, what anthropologists have documented about magical toolboxes, and what cognitive scientists know about imagination and absorption, and making those findings speak to one another.

**2. Practical magic looks the same across cultures; theoretical magic does not, and this is evidence for something real underneath.**

When Kim compares Taoist magic, Shinto rites, and Western Hermetic practice at the level of underlying mechanics rather than cultural symbolism, the systems converge. The symbols differ, the cosmology differs, the theoretical explanation differs, but the practical operations are structurally similar. He reads this as confirmation that cognitive science is the right frame: cultural variation produces different theoretical magic, but shared human cognitive architecture produces convergent practical magic.

**3. The rubber-hand illusion is the laboratory version of the astral body.**

Western magical traditions describe a ‘subtle body’ that can be manipulated independently of the physical body, and which is the actual locus of magical operation. Kim notes that cognitive science has replicated this in the lab: the rubber-hand illusion, the full-body ownership illusion, and the invisible-body illusion all demonstrate that the brain’s sense of embodiment and the experienced centre of selfhood are not fixed to the physical body. Synchronous stimulation can relocate the locus of experience entirely. What magicians called the astral body, neuroscientists call the body schema, and it turns out to be genuinely malleable.

**4. Randomised divination works by externalising pattern recognition, not by revealing cosmic truth.**

Kim draws on his published paper on I Ching divination to offer a cognitive account of why divination systems feel useful. Humans are pattern-recognition machines, but we struggle to hold our own intuitions at arm’s length long enough to examine them. A randomised divination system, tarot, casting lots, yarrow-stalk hexagrams, introduces controlled chaos into a fixed symbolic vocabulary. The symbols are loose enough to invite projection and specific enough to resist anything. The result is that the practitioner’s own implicit pattern recognition gets externalised onto something physical, making it inspectable. The Barnum effect, usually cited as evidence that divination is fake, is on this account a feature: precisely because the symbolism is applicable, it becomes an effective scaffolding for self-reflection.

**5. The real research gap is not how to cultivate virtue but how to make people want it in the first place.**

Kim’s thesis research is an attempt to identify what he calls aspirations, not goals with a fixed endpoint, but ongoing orientations toward self-transformation of the kind Agnes Callard describes. His finding from working with student populations is that very few people spontaneously report these; most projects cluster around grades, degrees, fitness, and career steps. The more interesting question, what creates the aspiration to become a wiser or more virtuous person at all, before any particular method is applied, is almost entirely unstudied. The only demographic predictor he and his team found in their pandemic study was religiosity, which suggests that institutions oriented toward transcendence are doing something the secular world has not yet found a replacement for.

**J:** The way that randomized systems work involves basic human capacity for pattern recognition. But humans are unfortunate creatures in that we are very cognitively slippery.
