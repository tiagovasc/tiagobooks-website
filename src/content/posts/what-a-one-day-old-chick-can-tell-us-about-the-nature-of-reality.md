## On symbols, cognition, and the bouba-kiki effect

Say the word “bouba” out loud. Now say “kiki.” If I showed you two shapes, one rounded and blobby, the other jagged and spiky, and asked you to match those words to those shapes, you’d almost certainly assign “bouba” to the round one and “kiki” to the spiky one. So would about ninety percent of people. This is the bouba-kiki effect, first noticed by the psychologist Wolfgang Köhler in the 1920s and replicated many times since.

The explanation for _why_ the mapping works is fairly intuitive. When you say “bouba,” your lips round softly, the sound rolls. When you say “kiki,” your tongue hits the roof of your mouth in a sharp stop, the sound cuts. The shape of the sound matches the shape of the shape. Your brain notices this correspondence across two completely different sensory channels, hearing and seeing, and treats it as meaningful. Not after deliberation. Instantly, automatically, as if it couldn’t be otherwise.

For a long time, however, it was easy to treat this as a quirk of English speakers, or of Indo-European languages more broadly. Perhaps there was something about the phonological structure of English, the specific sounds its alphabet happens to contain, that made the association feel natural to people who grew up speaking it. On this view, bouba-kiki would be interesting but ultimately parochial: a window into the sound-symbolic properties of a particular language family, a simple cultural artifact.

This deflationary reading took its first serious hit when researchers began testing the effect across very different linguistic populations. Ramachandran and Hubbard, in the early 2000s, replicated the finding with Tamil speakers. Tamil is a Dravidian language with a completely different phonological system from English. It uses different sounds, different articulatory patterns, different writing systems. And yet Tamil speakers showed the same mapping at comparable rates. Other studies followed, extending the finding to speakers of languages from unrelated families across multiple continents. But whatever was driving the bouba-kiki mapping, it did not depend on the particular phonological inventory of any one language. It held across linguistic boundaries that share almost no structural features.

This cross-cultural robustness changed the conversation. The effect could no longer be written off as an accident of English phonology. But it did not yet settle the deeper question, because a subtler version of the deflationary explanation was still available. Fine, the skeptic could say, it's universal. All humans do it. But that just means all humans share the same perceptual wiring. The mapping is a feature of _us_, not of the world. It tells us something about how human brains happen to be built, perhaps because of shared developmental experience, perhaps because of evolutionary contingency, but it says nothing about the structure of reality itself. Roundness and softness are not _actually_ related. They just feel related to creatures like us, because of the kind of nervous system we happen to have. On this reading, the bouba-kiki effect would be universal but still subjective.&nbsp;

This was the state of play until February 2026, when a study published in _Science_ by Maria Loconsole, Silvia Benavides-Varela, and Lucia Regolin changed the picture considerably. They tested the bouba-kiki effect not on adult humans, not on children, not even on human infants, but on baby domestic chickens. Chicks that were one day old. Chickens are a precocial species, meaning they’re mobile almost immediately after hatching, which lets researchers test them before they’ve had time to accumulate significant sensory experience.&nbsp;

The researchers controlled the chicks’ pre-test environment carefully. And the result was clear. In the first experiment, three-day-old chicks that had been trained to find food behind a neutral shape were then presented with separate round and spiky panels while a speaker played either "bouba" or "kiki." They went to the round shape when they heard "bouba" and to the spiky shape when they heard "kiki," with no training linking sounds to shapes. In the second experiment, chicks less than a day old, with no training at all, were shown moving round and spiky objects on video screens while the sounds played. They showed the same mapping.

This finding puts serious pressure on the learning hypothesis. A one-day-old chick has not spent months handling round objects while hearing soft sounds. It has no language. It has no culture. It has had almost no post-hatch sensory experience at all. It has had prenatal sensory experience inside the egg, and that caveat cannot be entirely dismissed, but the range of cross-modal correlations available to an embryo in a shell is vanishingly narrow. And yet it maps form onto form across modalities, spontaneously, as if the mapping were part of the basic equipment of perception itself.&nbsp;

The trajectory of the research, from Köhler’s original observation to the cross-cultural replications to the neonatal chick study, traces a progressive closing of escape routes. First it was a cultural artifact; the cross-linguistic data ruled that out. Then it was a product of embodied learning; the chick data makes that very difficult to maintain. What remains is the conclusion that cross-modal structural resonance, the capacity to find meaningful correspondence between form in one sensory domain and form in another, is not something acquired. It is part of how an embodied mind is organized before any particular content has been laid down.

This is a small finding in one sense. Shapes and sounds, chicks and nonsense words. But in another sense, it is not small at all. To see why it matters philosophically, you need to take a step back. There is a dominant way of thinking about the relationship between the mind and the world that goes roughly like this: the world is out there, neutral, without inherent meaning. The mind is in here, receiving raw sensory data and then organizing it, interpreting it, projecting meaning onto it. On this picture, any meaning we find in the world is something we put there. Symbols, metaphors, the sense that certain forms or patterns resonate with certain experiences: all of that is subjective, culturally constructed, or at best the residue of learned associations.

This way of thinking shapes how most educated people today approach questions about symbolism, myth, and religion. When a religious tradition says that ascending a mountain brings you closer to the divine, or that immersion in water represents dissolution and rebirth, the standard modern response is to treat these as arbitrary metaphors. Poetic, maybe. Culturally interesting, sure. But not tracking anything real. Just local conventions dressed up as cosmic truths.

The bouba-kiki effect in neonatal chicks puts pressure on this picture in a specific and important way. If cross-modal structural resonance is not learned but is constitutive of how embodied cognition works, then the foundation on which symbolic meaning rests is not cultural. It is biological. It is part of what it means to be a mind encountering a world at all.

This connects to a body of work in cognitive science known as the 4E framework: the view that cognition is embodied, embedded, enacted, and extended. The core idea is that thinking is not something that happens inside a brain in isolation. It happens through the active coupling of an organism with its environment. Meaning does not arrive pre-packaged from outside and get decoded by an internal processor. It arises in the interaction itself. When you reach for a cup, your perception of the cup and your motor engagement with it are not separate processes, one providing data and the other acting on it. They are aspects of a single cognitive event.

If this is right, then the structures through which we make sense of the world are not arbitrary representational schemes that could in principle be swapped out for different ones. They are grounded in the specific way that our bodies and our environments fit together. The philosopher George Lakoff and the linguist Mark Johnson have argued for decades that all abstract thought is built on metaphorical framework rooted in bodily experience. We speak of “grasping” an idea, of an argument having “weight,” of the future being “ahead” of us. Lakoff and Johnson’s claim is that these are the only way we have of thinking about abstract things at all. There is no non-metaphorical layer underneath.

The chick study lends biological force to this claim. If the cross-modal mappings that underlie metaphorical cognition are present in a one-day-old animal with no language and no culture, then they are not learned correlations. They are part of the cognitive architecture itself, upon which all abstract thought is built is not assembled through experience. It is there from the beginning.

Religious and mythological traditions across the world, many of them with no historical contact, converge on a strikingly similar set of symbolic structures. Verticality is associated with the divine and with transcendence. Descent and immersion are associated with dissolution, chaos, and transformation. Light maps onto intelligibility and goodness. The center maps onto identity and stability. These patterns show up in Hindu cosmology, in the Hebrew Bible, in Greek mythology, in Indigenous traditions across multiple continents, in Buddhism, in Christianity, in Islam. The standard modern explanation is cultural diffusion or coincidence. Or, if pressed, a shrug: humans are pattern-seeking animals, and sometimes they find similar patterns independently. Nothing deeper is going on.

But consider what the bouba-kiki finding, and especially the chick extension of it, actually implies. If the capacity for cross-modal structural resonance is pre-cultural, pre-linguistic, and present even in non-human animals, then when traditions across the world independently associate height with transcendence or dissolution with transformation, it is worth asking whether they are converging on the same arbitrary metaphor by accident. The alternative is that they are independently discovering and systematizing structural resonances that are built into the cognitive architecture of any embodied mind. A mind that orients itself in a gravitational field will find "up" and "down" non-arbitrarily meaningful. A mind that perceives the difference between bounded form and unbounded dissolution will find these non-arbitrarily linked to experiences of identity and its loss.

The art historian and philosopher Ananda Coomaraswamy once distinguished between two ways of relating to symbols: the secular mind “reads meaning into” things that originally had none, while the symbolist “reads meaning out” of things in which meaning is already present. The bouba-kiki findings give empirical traction to this distinction. The structures of meaning that symbolic traditions articulate are not projected onto a blank world. They are drawn out of structures that are already there in how embodied cognition organizes experience.

The usual way of dismissing symbolic and ritual knowledge is to contrast it with propositional knowledge, the kind of knowledge expressed in clear, testable statements. Propositional knowledge is “objective.” Symbolic knowledge is “merely subjective,” a matter of feeling or cultural conditioning rather than fact. This distinction is supposed to settle the matter.

But if Lakoff and Johnson are right that propositional thought is itself built on a metaphorical structure, and if the chick study is right that this is part of the innate architecture of cognition, then the supposedly objective standpoint from which you dismiss symbolic knowing is itself grounded in exactly the same cross-modal, metaphorical structures that symbolic traditions systematize. The propositional is not the ground floor. It is a higher story built on foundations it does not acknowledge.

This means that the dismissal is self-undermining. To say that symbolic and ritual modes of knowing are epistemically inferior to propositional ones because they are “merely metaphorical” is to rely on an epistemology that is itself metaphorically constituted. You are sawing off the branch you are sitting on.

None of this means that every religious claim is true, or that symbolic traditions are infallible, or that we should abandon critical thinking in favour of mystical intuition. What it means is something more precise: that participatory, symbolic, and ritual modes of engaging with reality operate at a level of cognitive architecture that is more foundational than propositional analysis, and that dismissing them as “merely subjective” requires an objective/subjective distinction that cannot sustain its own weight.

A one-day-old chick, matching a nonsense sound to a shape it has never seen before, is doing something that looks trivial. But it is demonstrating, in the simplest possible case, the same capacity for cross-modal structural resonance that underlies every symbolic system humans have ever built. The machinery of meaning-making is not added to cognition by culture. It is part of what cognition is. And if meaning is part of what cognition is, and cognition is part of how any embodied creature encounters the world, then what we call reality was never the neutral, meaningless substrate that the modern picture requires. It was always already structured by the same resonances that a one-day-old chick navigates on instinct. Religious traditions have known this for millennia. Cognitive science is beginning to catch up.
