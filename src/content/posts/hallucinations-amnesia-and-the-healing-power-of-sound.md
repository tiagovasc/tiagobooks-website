**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_Musicophilia: Tales of Music and the Brain_’ by_ Oliver Sacks**

I have long been an admirer of Oliver Sacks, having previously devoured _Awakenings_, _An Anthropologist on Mars_, _The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat_, and _Hallucinations_. Consequently, I was eager to finally dig into _Musicophilia_, especially as I am a huge music fan myself. The book catalogs various patients of Sacks who experience specific "musical misalignments" or distinct neurological relationships with sound. At the extremes, he recounts cases of individuals with Williams syndrome, who possess a hyper-musicality despite facing significant intellectual disabilities, and conversely, those with amusia who cannot grasp the emotional or structural meaning of music at all. My favorite anecdotes involve sudden onsets of musical passion, such as the man struck by lightning who subsequently developed an insatiable urge to become a pianist. For many in these situations, this shift is not merely a hobby but feels like a divine calling or a new duty toward God expressed through composition. You will also encounter fascinating accounts of musical hallucinations and synesthesia, where the senses overlap in bizarre and beautiful ways.

Despite the fascinating case studies, I found the book occasionally drifted too far into the neurology of the conditions, sometimes seemingly deviating from the topic of music entirely. This heavy scientific focus, combined with my familiarity with his other works, made portions of the text feel somewhat repetitive, though this reaction may be specific to my own reading history rather than a flaw in the book itself. A significant portion of the text is dedicated to the restorative power of music for those with declining mental faculties, such as dementia, where melody seems to breathe life into those who might otherwise appear to be empty vessels. Sacks writes poignant passages on this, noting that while there are specific cortical areas for musical intelligence, the emotional response to music is widespread and sub-cortical. He argues that even in diffuse diseases like Alzheimer’s, music can still be perceived and enjoyed. As he beautifully puts it, music is no luxury to them but a necessity, and it can have a power beyond anything else to restore them to themselves and to others, at least for a while. While the book lacks a cohesive overall narrative, serving instead as a collection of clinical tales, it remains a wonderful entry point for anyone lucky enough to dive into Sacks’s writing for the first time.
