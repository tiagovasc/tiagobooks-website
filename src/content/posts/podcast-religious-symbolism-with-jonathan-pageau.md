Jonathan Pageau has spent decades carving his way to the heart of Christian symbolism, literally. In this conversation, he traces his journey from evangelical Protestantism and contemporary art school to Eastern Orthodoxy, explaining how iconography gave him the visual language that modern art could not. He walks through why Plato and Aristotle matter for Christianity (and why neither invented what they noticed), how the Incarnation reconciles the tension between essence and instantiation, and why perennialism is simultaneously true and spiritually dangerous. He critiques modern biblical scholarship as reductively historical, defends the value of commitment to a single path over universalist syncretism, and offers a conception of love drawn from Maximus the Confessor that operates like a fractal, holding unity and multiplicity together at every scale. He closes with measured skepticism about psychedelics and a recommendation to read Dostoevsky.

## Who Is Jonathan Pageau?

Jonathan Pageau is a French-Canadian wood carver, iconographer, and Orthodox Christian thinker based in Quebec. He runs a YouTube channel with roughly 80,000 subscribers exploring religious symbolism, iconography, and the symbolic structure of reality from a Christian perspective. He is the brother of Mathieu Pageau, author of The Language of Creation. His work draws on the Eastern Church Fathers, particularly Maximus the Confessor and Gregory of Nyssa, as well as thinkers ranging from Heidegger and Derrida to René Guénon and René Girard.

## TOP 3 Thesis

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Christianity's incarnational principle resolves the central tension in Western philosophy: essence does not hover above the world (Plato) nor disappear into instances (Aristotle), it exists to be embodied, which is why things in the world are not illusions but the only place where the patterns can actually be encountered.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Committing to a single religious path is not intolerance but a prerequisite for any genuine spiritual growth. The universalist who stands above all traditions and picks what they like ends up constructing a religion with no friction, and friction is precisely what makes transformation possible.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Love, properly understood in the Christian tradition, is the force that allows multiplicity and unity to coexist without collapsing into each other, a fractal principle that scales from the parts of a chair through human relationships all the way to the doctrine of the Trinity.

## Key Insights

**1. Symbolism came before conversion, and that sequence matters.**

Pageau did not convert to Orthodoxy and then discover symbolism. He discovered iconography and the visual language of the early Church while studying contemporary art and found it solved a crisis he was already in, both artistically and spiritually. His conversion followed from that discovery. This makes his account of symbolism credible in a way that confessional advocacy usually is not: he arrived at it through a problem, not a conclusion.

**2. Plato did not invent Platonism, and logos is not a Greek idea.**

Pageau insists that treating Greek philosophy as the origin of Christian metaphysics gets the history wrong. Plato was in contact with Pythagorean initiates who were in turn in contact with Vedantic and Eastern thought. The notion of logos, patterns that structure reality, has analogues across Chinese, Indian, and biblical traditions not because one influenced the others but because they are all pointing at the same feature of reality. Greek philosophy gave Christianity a vocabulary, not the underlying insight.

**3. The Incarnation is Christianity's solution to the Plato-Aristotle problem.**

Plato placed the essences outside the world, so escape from the world is the path to truth (the cave allegory). Aristotle pulled the essences back into their instantiations but created a tension between body and form. Christianity offers a third option: things in the world are not shadows but instantiations of the pattern, and without logos things would not cohere at all, they would scatter into quantum potentiality. The purpose of a pattern is to be incarnated. There is no point to a heavenly chair without chairs you can sit on.

**4. Walking a single path is not narrowness, it is the only way to actually go somewhere.**

Pageau's critique of perennialism and universalism is practical rather than doctrinal. When someone asks whether all paths lead to the same summit, they are not walking any path, they are standing still asking the question. More than that: the things in a tradition that challenge you, that you do not like or understand, are precisely what produces growth. A self-assembled spiritual practice from elements you already agree with guarantees no friction and therefore no transformation. The universalist church in California that painted Gandhi and Ella Fitzgerald alongside the saints still only painted people they approved of, which revealed, despite the theology, a very particular and selective hierarchy.

**5. Post-modern thinkers like Derrida are doing symbolism upside down, which still makes them useful.**

Pageau credits Heidegger, Derrida, and semioticians like Roland Barthes as influences, despite the apparent incongruity. His reading is that post-modern theory has rediscovered the logic of symbolic thinking, the margin destabilizing the center, hybrids on the edge of being devouring settled categories, but it applies this logic in what he calls an inverted manner. Turn the insights right-side up and they illuminate what traditional symbolic thinking has always said about how margins and limits operate. The same applies to gender theory and critical race theory: they use symbolic logic but deploy it destructively.

**6. Why the biblical text survives is more important than the historical claims about it.**

Pageau's critique of modern biblical scholarship is not a defense of literalism. It is a challenge to the question of relevance. Most ancient texts have been forgotten. The biblical texts have not been. No historical or political explanation of how they were composed accounts for why millions of people still care about them. The survival and continued power of the text is the primary phenomenon to be explained, and reducing that explanation to source documents, king lists, and comparative mythology misses what is most interesting. A scholar who writes a bestseller claiming Moses did not exist is still, paradoxically, being carried by the symbolic weight of Moses.
