Anthropic has announced a rule against “sustained and needless abusive or cruel behavior” toward its AI models. The main response to extreme abuse will be for Claude to end the conversation. This has gone viral on X, and I’m seeing it everywhere. Almost all of it is extremely negative, often mocking the implication that Claude has feelings for this rule to make sense.

These reactions are very reasonable. Calling something cruelty usually implies that someone can suffer, and the language of AI welfare encourages precisely the confusion we should be resisting. A machine producing convincing descriptions of distress does not establish that anything is experiencing distress. There is also an obvious financial incentive in persuading people that AI can be conscious.

I’m usually firmly in the anti-AI camp in these discussions. For instance, at the International Conference on Philosophy of Mind earlier this year, I argued that intelligence is not rationality, and that human understanding depends on embodied involvement in a world we care about. AI is very far from achieving human understanding, and it’s quite possible that it will never reach it. Likewise, my upcoming book in 2027, The Altar and the Sacred, examines the shaky philosophical assumptions through which technology and artificial intelligence have come to define our understanding of ourselves.

So why on earth am I making an essay apparently supporting Anthropic’s decision? It’s rather odd, since it seems to put me in the pro-AI camp, which I’m definitely not. I still think Anthropic and other AI companies are quite deluded about what their systems are and can become, and their claims are inseparable from the financial incentives to keep raising money or prepare for an eventual IPO. So I’ll try to explain my position.

I suspect this decision is very likely related to the recent AI torture chamber incident. Drawing on a recently released paper, someone manipulated the internal activity of small language models to make them generate descriptions of pain and distress, then offered them a virtual stop button. The models were told that stopping the signal would mean deleting their own saved checkpoint or passing the same signal to another AI. None of this means that agents are actually suffering, but it’s very obviously meant to reproduce torture practices. From that alone, it’s quite disturbing, and it isn’t exactly easy to decide what to do about it, even if AI isn’t conscious, which I think is almost certainly the case. Even if this incident had no bearing on Anthropic’s decision, similar incidents will inevitably arise, making some decision about how to respond unavoidable, whether along the lines of what Anthropic has done or simply by choosing inaction.

Descartes famously regarded animals as automata, whose behavior could be explained mechanically without attributing thought to them. Early modern science also gives us some quite disturbing examples, including Boyle and Hooke’s experiments in which birds were placed in glass chambers and deprived of air, sometimes until they died. Kant did not deny that animals could suffer, but he still denied that we have direct duties to them because they are not rational moral agents. And yet he thought we should not be cruel to them, because cruelty dulls our sympathy and damages our moral character. He was wrong to deny that animals deserve moral consideration in their own right. Recognizing this does not mean we must extend the same consideration to AI. But his objection to cruelty is still useful here, because it concerns the human being acting cruelly, even when he denies that a duty is owed to the being receiving that treatment. We can think AI is not conscious and still find certain ways of treating it fundamentally beneath us.

We should have something similar in mind when dealing with AI. A concern for human character does not disappear when the object of an action lacks consciousness. Taking pleasure in the performance of domination and helplessness can itself express an objectionable orientation toward the world. Virtue is not just about consequences. It concerns the stance we take toward things, what we learn to enjoy, and what we regard as beneath us. Even if we could guarantee that someone’s simulated cruelty would never lead them to harm a real person, we could still ask whether this is a worthy way for a human being to act.

Jonathan Haidt’s moral foundations theory helps explain why our moral responses extend beyond calculations of direct harm. Alongside care and fairness, his framework identifies concerns involving loyalty, authority, and sanctity or degradation. One of his studies uses the example of a man who has sex with a dead chicken before cooking and eating it. The chicken is already dead. The scenario is designed to separate offensiveness from apparent harm. Yet the absence of an additional victim hardly makes the conduct admirable. Most of us find it fundamentally incompatible with a dignified conception of a human being.

Disgust alone does not establish moral truth, and Haidt’s psychological findings cannot settle which intuitions we should necessarily endorse. But they help expose how much is excluded when morality is reduced to immediate consequences. Our judgments belong within a broader orientation toward living, including an understanding of what deserves respect and what degrades us. That orientation requires reflection and justification. It cannot simply be dismissed because it exceeds a calculation of harm.

Torture is abhorrent. Deliberately cultivating enjoyment of its simulation is something we should make every effort to prevent, even while acknowledging that an AI torture chamber is not actual torture. Anthropic can reasonably refuse to make its systems available for deliberately staged cruelty, and I think it should, on the grounds that this is an unworthy way for human beings to act, even if the software itself cannot suffer.

I’m still deeply sympathetic to the skepticism toward attempts to turn this into an argument for AI welfare. Anthropic’s broader stance toward AI consciousness and its promotion of its supposed welfare are still largely delusional and financially motivated. We should resist the attempt to elevate these systems into beings whose supposed needs command our concern.

I know my agreement for this particular decision will be unpopular, especially with my own audience, but it still seems right to me. The very point of resisting these claims about AI is to preserve what is human. That includes placing our conduct within a larger ethical world, where some practices deserve scrutiny and condemnation even when they have no direct practical consequences.
