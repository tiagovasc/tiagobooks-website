Graham Harman argues that philosophy has been too long trapped between two impoverished moves: reducing things downward to their parts, or upward to their effects. Object-Oriented Ontology (OOO) breaks this by insisting that objects, from hammers to corporations to fire burning cotton, always withdraw from full contact with everything else, including other objects. In this conversation, Harman traces that idea through Husserl, Heidegger, and Aristotle, explains why aesthetics and metaphor do philosophical work that literal knowledge cannot, and makes the case that matter is a fiction, there are only forms, and every translation loses something. The discussion moves into ethics, essence, the limits of flat ontology, and why architecture became the testing ground for whether art can remain art while being used.

## Who is Graham Harman?

Graham Harman is Professor of Philosophy at the Southern California Institute of Architecture in Los Angeles. He received his doctorate from DePaul University and previously taught for many years at the American University in Cairo. He is the founder of Object-Oriented Ontology and one of the key figures associated with Speculative Realism. His books include _Tool-Being_, _The Quadruple Object_, _Object-Oriented Ontology: A New Theory of Everything_, _Art and Objects_, and _Architecture and Objects_.

## Top 3 Thesis

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Objects always withdraw from full contact with everything else, not just from human perception, but from each other. Fire does not access the smell, color, or price of the cotton it burns. This universal withdrawal is the core claim of OOO, and it applies symmetrically to every relation in the cosmos.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; There is no such thing as matter, only forms. Every translation of a thing into another medium (a person into a photograph, a reality into a theory) changes the form. Nothing is extracted intact. There is no translation without information loss, which is why a perfect mathematical model of a person is not the person.

•&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Aesthetics is not decorative but philosophically essential. Metaphor, wine-dark sea, Picasso's Guernica, demonstrates that objects and their qualities have a loose, non-identical relationship. This is why aesthetics does work that literal knowledge cannot, and why art is not just culturally valuable but metaphysically revealing.

## Key Insights

**1. Heidegger's tool-analysis already points beyond the human, but OOO goes further.**

Heidegger showed that for the most part objects are invisible to us, we only notice the hammer when it breaks. Harman's first move beyond Heidegger is to say this isn't just about the difference between human theory and human practice. Both are merely human ways of relating to the world, and any human relation oversimplifies. The real gap is not between theory and practice but between human anything and reality itself. OOO extends this: objects withdraw not just from humans but from each other. Fire doesn't access all of cotton; it only meets what it can interact with.

**2. Literalism is philosophy's defining error, and aesthetics is the cure.**

Harman defines literalism as the belief that an object is exhausted by all the true facts about it. Hume is the textbook case: he denied that apples exist as such, thinking 'apple' is just a nickname for a cluster of experienced qualities. OOO holds the opposite: the object is primary and always exceeds any description of it. Aesthetics demonstrates this because a metaphor like 'wine-dark sea' is not a fact about the sea, it forces you to actively produce a strange new object by transplanting wine-qualities onto ocean. The discomfort of that effort shows the gap between objects and their qualities.

**3. There are no knockdown arguments in philosophy, only new possibilities.**

Harman distinguishes justification (what you can publicly defend with evidence) from truth (your lived conviction about what is really going on). Science works in justification; it tells us what we are warranted in saying, not what is ultimately real. Philosophy is similar, what makes a philosophy a classic is not that it makes fewer mistakes or solves problems definitively, but that it unearths a new possibility nobody had seen before. This is why Plato remains relevant while Ptolemaic astronomy does not. Problems like mind-body don't get solved; they get more sophisticated. What disappears are solutions that become implausible given new knowledge.

**4. Essences exist, but you can never know them, only approach them.**

Harman recovers essence against postmodern anti-essentialism, but with a crucial limit: you cannot know an essence directly. The postmodern critique targets the claim to know essences ('Arab peoples are inherently incapable of democracy'), not essences themselves. OOO agrees you can't build a thing out of words or concepts, individual things are always more complex than any description. The Scandinavians example is instructive: a thousand years separates the Viking raiders from today's welfare-state builders, yet a real expert could trace continuities. Essence is what endures beneath all particular manifestations, which is precisely why you can't pin it down.

**5. Architecture is where Kant's aesthetics finally breaks down, and OOO takes over.**

Kant argued that genuine aesthetic experience requires disinterestedness, the artwork must be separated from any human use or interest. This demotes architecture to a lesser art because it is always contaminated by utility. Harman's counter: even pure artworks cannot be disentangled from the human beholder. The encounter is always part of the aesthetic object. Architecture is simply forced to confront this honestly, while painting and sculpture can pretend otherwise. That is why architecture has become theoretically productive for OOO: it cannot escape the question of how human use and aesthetic withdrawal coexist in the same object.

Aristotle does not think you can define an individual thing because definitions use universals and individual things are concrete. So you cannot define Socrates. The point being you cannot build a thing out of words. You cannot build a thing out of ideas or concepts or even of truths. The concrete thing is always much more complex than anything you can say or know about it. That includes the essence of the thing. The essence of the thing is infinitely complex and you can only get a partial cross-section of it at any point.

In postmodern philosophy there is this crusade against essentialism. Essentialism is a dirty word. It is associated with imperialism and patriarchy and all of these other things. I see why that happens. Consider the Scandinavians. This is one of my favorite examples. They were rapists and marauders who burned monks and nuns and sacked cities and did human sacrifices and had the most inhumane forms of torture for traitors you can imagine. Does that mean that Scandinavians do not have an essence and it is just historically performative? No. What I think it means is that Scandinavians have an essence that is deeper than any of those particular manifestations. If you look at Scandinavian history a real expert on Scandinavian history could probably point to certain personality traits that have endured over a thousand years and longer.

Here is another example. Europeans sometimes complain that America vacillates back and forth across the decades between isolationism and interventionism. American moralism can manifest itself as we are too good to get involved with the Europeans and we do not want to get involved with their corrupt dynastic quarrels. That is the nineteenth century or twentieth century view. Or it manifests as Europe needs our help because we do not want to let them have all these fascist rulers arise and America is political good and we need to help the Europeans find that. That is another form of moralism. I think that shows you the way in which a thing has an essence that does not manifest itself the same way at different times.

Look at a case like women. Women have not really had the opportunities that men have had. It is way too early to say what women are capable of and what women are not capable of. I would reject any such statements as Pat Buchanan made. The same is true for all other generalizations. Are the Germans a philosophical people? We would all say of course now. You have to let wine age before you know what a person or a culture is capable of. That is why I think you can talk about an essence. But speaking of the knowledge of an essence is a very bad idea. Nobody really has it. The world surprises us.
