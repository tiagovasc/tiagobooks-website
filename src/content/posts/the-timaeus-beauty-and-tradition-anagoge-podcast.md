## Abstract

How much of what you read are you actually missing? Comparative literature scholar Piero Boitani makes the case that most of us are functionally illiterate when it comes to the Western canon, not because we lack access to the texts, but because we lack the layered knowledge required to read them. He demonstrates this with a single word in Tolstoy that links _The Death of Ivan Ilyich_ to Christ's crucifixion through Tolstoy's own Gospel translation, a connection invisible without Russian, Greek, and biblical literacy working simultaneously. From there, the conversation expands into the thin border between philosophy and poetry, why both originate in Aristotelian wonder, and what exactly poetry can reach that philosophy cannot. Boitani traces how every major Western intellectual revival has been an attempt to recover antiquity, and argues that modern culture's refusal to look backward is not progress but a form of blindness. He closes with an unexpectedly blunt reflection on dying, fame, and whether literature offers any real defense against either. &nbsp;

## Who Is Piero Boitani?

Piero Boitani is an Italian literary critic and Professor Emeritus of Comparative Literature at Sapienza University of Rome. He earned his PhD from Cambridge, is a Fellow of the British Academy, and won the 2016 Balzan Prize for Comparative Literature. His work spans Chaucer, Dante, Homer, classical myth, and biblical rewritings across a fifty-year career. He directs the Fondazione Valla's Greek and Latin classics series.

## Top 3 Ideas

1. **The unity of knowledge is fundamental.** The same human mind that grasps atomic physics can become ecstatic before a painting; the division of knowledge into siloed disciplines impoverishes all of them.

2. **The classics are not dead relics but living foundations.** Every major literary and intellectual achievement in the West is a rewriting or rediscovery of forms and ideas first articulated in antiquity, and cutting ourselves off from that past makes us culturally shallow.

3. **Poetry and philosophy share a common root in wonder.** Their modes of expression differ, but both arise from the same Aristotelian impulse toward knowledge, and the border between them is far thinner than modern academic categories suggest.

## Memorable Quotes

> "If you learn Greek, you will discover many more things in Homer than you ever will by only reading it in Italian."

> "Perhaps the greatest philosopher of the West, Plato, is essentially a poet. Aristotle is not. Perhaps Aristotle is right and Plato is wrong, but Plato is undeniably a poet."

> "Not seeing the past is like going to the beach and not seeing the waves breaking on the shore."

## Key Insights

**Reading in the original language reveals hidden connections.** Boitani demonstrates this with Tolstoy's _The Death of Ivan Ilyich_, where a specific Russian word links to Tolstoy's own Gospel translation, revealing that Ivan Ilyich dies like Christ. That connection is invisible in any translation.

**Wonder is the shared origin of all serious inquiry.** Aristotle's claim that wonder drives the love of knowledge applies equally to myth, philosophy, and natural science. The person who loves stories and the person who loves wisdom are closer than they appear.

**Poetry reaches where philosophy cannot fully go.** Using Eliot's "stillness between two waves of the sea" and Dante's vision of Neptune beholding the Argo, Boitani argues that the greatest poetry touches the ineffable in ways that conceptual argument can only approximate.

**Modern culture's fixation on the present is a form of blindness.** Boitani's students in Rome had never visited the Roman Forum or the Sistine Chapel. The loss of historical depth strips away the "geological stratification" that gives meaning and perspective to experience.

**Literature serves as a defense against death, but ambiguously.** It distracts, it inoculates through repeated exposure to mortality, and it offers a version of immortality through lasting works. But Boitani is skeptical about lasting fame, noting that even distinguished critics are forgotten within a few years of dying.

But even outside of that, it infiltrates the mind into other aspects of life that become completely utility-driven as well. Speaking about these ancient texts and traditions, I would love it if you could elaborate on what you think are some big misunderstandings of what the ancient world was actually like. I feel that's often a massive barrier to appreciating it. I only recently started diving deeper into Christianity and the Gospels, and I realized I actually don't know anything. There's no way I can truly understand what these texts are telling me because they live in a completely separate world. I had to learn about Judaism just to place myself historically, because the way they thought is so alien to us.

What do you think is the best place for people to find that footing? What are some fundamental concepts essential to properly understanding the ancient world?

P: I don't know if there are ways of completely knowing the ancient world. There are technical ways, of course, like archaeology and philology. You can meticulously reconstruct an ancient text. But if you truly want to know what the ancient world means, you just have to read what they produced, which is only a very small portion of what they actually wrote.

Sophocles wrote about a hundred plays, and we only have seven. Aeschylus, the same; we have seven. It's like going to a museum and finding one painting, or scraps of leather with some faded color, or small pieces of marble instead of a whole magnificent sculpture.

The Western world suffered an incredible trauma, which the East hasn't had in the same way. China enjoys an incredible continuity. But in the West, between the end of the fifth century and the beginning of the sixth century, it all collapsed. They didn't even know how to build a road or keep it up. The Romans were able to build baths and aqueducts everywhere, and suddenly that knowledge was completely gone.

On a different level, the knowledge of Greek was almost entirely gone in the West, and the knowledge of Latin gone in the East. This is an incredible, dark moment in the history of Western civilization—the fall of ancient civilization.

Then they had at least three or four renaissances in the Middle Ages: the Carolingian Renaissance, the Ottonian Renaissance, the Renaissance of the twelfth century, and finally the major Renaissance of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Every single renaissance is a rebirth of something that was there before, a desperate attempt at recovering antiquity. They thought they were dwarves standing on the shoulders of giants, which is a wonderful image because it means they see further than the giants precisely because they are elevated on top of them.

It's highly interesting that the word 'modernity', _modernus_, is actually a medieval word. They thought of themselves as being the _moderni_.

T: The _moderni_, they called themselves.

P: What does the word itself actually mean? It means they are fashionable, in the mode. They are today's people. One generation is always more modern than the past generation. By the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, the distinct difference between the ancients and the moderns became very clear.

Despite this fascination with history, I do want to dive more specifically into your book, which was recently released: _Timaeus in Paradise: Metaphors of Beauty from Plato to Dante and Beyond_. I highly recommend everyone get it. It was very enjoyable and heavily aligns with the themes we've discussed so far, a bit more on the literary side but well within the same continuity of thought.

For those not familiar, maybe introduce the book, what drove you to write it, and the context of your previous book that inspired this new one.

P: I can tell you the story of exactly how the books were born. A few years ago, the Valla Foundation that I chair published Plato's _Timaeus_ in a wonderful new edition with a new critical text and commentary. I was invited to a book launch in Pavia where several people were talking about the _Timaeus_.

Since I'm not a philosopher, I had something ringing in my ears: _"Quel che Timeo delle anime argomenta"_—what Timaeus argues about the soul. That line is from Dante's _Paradiso_. It is Beatrice speaking to Dante, saying that what Timaeus tells about souls returning to the stars after death is obviously false. There are no souls becoming stars again, unless, she says, Plato is speaking by metaphor, much like the Bible and the Church, which attributes hands and feet to God but means something entirely different.

This started me thinking. I wrote a longish fifty-page essay and sent it to two friends of mine, Dante scholars in Cambridge and Harvard. Their response was enthusiastic; they told me to complete it. I sent it to a publisher friend here in Rome, who suggested publishing it alongside the text of the _Timaeus_. I declined, saying I wanted to make it a more substantial book.

Then I had a terrible accident. I fell in the street, broke everything I could possibly break, and was hospitalized. One morning, as I was lying in bed in pain, a few lines danced in my head in English. I wrote them down and tried to put them into Italian but couldn't. I sent them to a poet friend in Northern Ireland, Michael Longley, who called it a cascade and encouraged me to go ahead. That became almost three thousand lines in English, which was eventually translated into Italian by a very good contemporary poet. That is the poem, entitled _The Timaeus as Plato's Poem_.

At the same time, I was expanding the original essay into a scholarly book. The Italian version of the scholarly book came out and was a great success. An English friend of ours loved it and translated it into English, and Princeton decided to publish it.

What I was deeply interested in was the ways in which the _Timaeus_, which is a view of the universe and man's place in it, gets transformed through the ages. When Christianity won Europe, they thought the _Timaeus_ was perfectly attuned to their beliefs about creation. Kepler, a brilliant seventeenth-century scientist, even said the best commentary on Genesis was Plato's _Timaeus_.

It's fascinating to see how late antiquity philosophers like Proclus saw Plato as both a Pythagorean inspired by images and a precise, logical scientist. This dual perspective goes all the way to Heisenberg and Whitehead. To see how these complex ideas get transformed by the Fathers of the Church, pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, John Scotus Eriugena, the School of Chartres, and Dante is absolutely fascinating.

It involves not just philosophy, but poetry and magnificent painting—like the medieval frescoes in the crypt of Anagni Cathedral, which are absolutely riveting. That's essentially what I did. It's a walk through the millennia.

T: Right. And I'll just show this beautiful book to the camera as well. It has beautiful illustrations too. What's the title of the book in English so that people can check it out if they want?

P: In English, it was published in two separate volumes. One is called _Looking Upwards_, with a subtitle like _A History of Poetry and Astronomy_, going down to the fifteenth century. The second volume covers modernity from the sixteenth to the twentieth century, called something like _The Modern Cosmos_.

T: Something that I really enjoyed about your book was precisely this continuity of culture, story, and worldview. I find that deeply fascinating.

You don't just discard the past; it gets actively incorporated, and that's how you progress history forward.

Obviously, there are breaks, but there is this continuity and aggregation. Your book shows that very strongly. You see this very strongly in Dante as well, because he masterfully synthesizes the entire known world into a coherent system within his Christian worldview.

P: The chief imagination of Christendom, as W.B. Yeats called him in a famous poem.

T: Yeah, beautiful. I'm wondering how you see this concept of universal history progressing. Do you think it's a healthy dynamic of culture? One historical critique is that it can act as a kind of colonization of other cultures, flattening them entirely. What do you think is the right balance, and how do you think it progressed throughout history?

P: A philosophy or theology of history is one thing; universal or world literature, _Weltliteratur_, is something completely different because it regards literature, not history.

World history, as a sequence of events, takes place all over the world. If it has a _telos_, a final purpose, it becomes a theology. Christians have done it that way: creation, incarnation, and the end of the world. That is exactly what Dante believes.

_Weltliteratur_ is different. I believe it has always existed. In the ancient Mediterranean world, there were two cultures, Greek and Latin. It was the entire world to them. People like Lucian were from the Middle East, and St. Augustine was from Africa. It existed dynamically in Greek and Latin.

The real modern _Weltliteratur_ starts with Goethe, who invented the expression and said we should talk about world literature rather than national literatures. But he wisely added that when in doubt, we should always go back to the Greeks. We tend to forget that nowadays.

As literacy, publishing, and language spread through global colonization, regions like South America, Africa, India, and Australia started publishing heavily in English or French, and it really became _Weltliteratur_. History is far more difficult. Our history became more world history with the two World Wars, especially World War II, because it involved so many countries and soldiers from all over the world. If a war were to break out now between two superpowers, it would instantly become a world war.

Dante was trying to create a great myth of everything encompassing the past toward a great vision.

To avoid going down that rabbit hole, I would like to start wrapping up with some personal questions. What is something that you understand now that you couldn't understand when you were younger? Something that took decades to develop?

P: Life and death. Or more accurately than death, dying, which is a different thing. The older you get and the sicker you get, the closer you are to dying, and you think about it all the time. You think about it in connection with poetry, literature, religion, philosophy, and yourself. It becomes an urgent matter.

Thinking about death also means thinking about life, because the two are intertwined. The horizon of expectation at the age of seventy-eight or seventy-nine is obviously restricted. You think about the final moment—not death as such, but dying. Dying starts when you're born.

Hamlet says it well: "And makes us rather bear those ills we have."
