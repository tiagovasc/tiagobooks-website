**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_On Bullshit_’ by_ Harry Frankfurt**

I was already familiar with Harry Frankfurt’s concept in _On Bullshit_, but I wanted to read the original work, and I am glad I did. It is, first of all, quite a funny book. When it arrived and my girlfriend asked what it was, I said it was a philosophical take on bullshit, causing her to look confused as she thought I was joking. Humour aside, I consider it a great display of how philosophy can be done well by clarifying and articulating the rather "banal" conceptions we have rather than just building grand metaphysical schemes. The book is incredibly short, which I value a lot, as some books offer great insights but require a huge time investment. When you have to put in little time to get a decent amount out of it, that is a gold nugget. The specific hardcover version I bought was high-quality and robust yet only 67 pages with small dimensions and huge font. You can read the whole thing in an hour or less, so I highly recommend it.

Frankfurt introduces bullshit as a concept distinct from lying. He starts by tracking its etymology but soon shifts to a story in which Wittgenstein gets annoyed with someone for saying they feel "like a dog who was run over." The annoyance stems from the fact that the speaker does not actually know how a dog feels after being run over by a car. While this situation might feel stupid, bizarre, or perhaps taken out of context, it nicely illustrates Frankfurt’s point. Wittgenstein, being obsessed with the objectivity of language, took issue with this simple expression not because she was lying, but because she lacked a connection to the truth. Lying implies that I know the truth and am trying to deceive you, meaning a lie requires me to care about truth values. Bullshit, however, occurs when I do not care about the truth. In the anecdote, it does not really matter how a dog feels. The speaker is not lying about the differences in feeling, but rather implying that whatever the differences are, they do not matter.

A bullshitter is neither misrepresenting the world nor trying to affect your beliefs about his beliefs, which is what a liar does. The deception lies in the fact that we expect people to care about the truth—a requirement for both truth-tellers and liars—but bullshit goes beyond this concern entirely. This distinction is quite relevant for today’s culture as we approach a state of "post-truth." It is not merely that people lie or get things wrong, but that there are people for whom the truth is not even a factor. This is particularly evident in modern advertisements, which is what drew me to Frankfurt’s concept the most. While there are certainly statements in ads that are not true, the creators are not necessarily trying to lie. They simply do not care because what matters is the salience of the ad. Not only do they not care about the truth, but often you do not care either because the disconnect is too obvious. Few bother to refute an ad because it exists in a post-truth realm where it is pure bullshit.
