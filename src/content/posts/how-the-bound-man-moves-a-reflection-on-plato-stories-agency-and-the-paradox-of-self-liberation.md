I recently shared a short narration on [<u>Instagram</u>](https://www.instagram.com/p/DNfaXRNIxQN/) from my book [_<u>In Search of the Infinite</u>_](https://tiagovf.medium.com/my-seven-year-lsd-writing-project-30656c66089e), a psychedelic memoir chronicling my experiences with LSD over a span of seven years. Each chapter takes the form of an essay, written during the experience itself. The book explores themes of ultimate reality, morality, meaning, and religion. What began as a search for truth, in whatever form it might take, gradually became a personal journey from a secular to a religious worldview, tracing the philosophical ground that led me there.

This post shares a few thoughts that came to me while I was editing this clip. It is not meant to be rigorous but simply to lay out some trains of thought. In the future, I will have more systematic expositions.

Rereading my own book is a strange experience. I am often deeply drawn to it. This is not out of vanity. There is much I wish were better written, both philosophically and stylistically, especially in the older first two volumes. The pull feels more like gratitude mixed with nostalgia. It is like watching an old VHS tape from childhood. A snapshot in time, grainy, unpolished, but intimate. I see who I was, what I was wrestling with, what mattered to me then. It is all there, captured in motion, even if on some issues, I no longer think or feel the same way.

However, beyond this aspect, there is another, more profound attraction. When I read it, I notice themes that I find strikingly insightful and true now, but at the time, I barely understood them. They were vague, symbolic, often little more than intuitions that I struggled to articulate with the language I had. Reading these passages today is immensely satisfying because it validates my entire project, showing that the path I chose to live was the correct one and that one can grasp truth in myth, images, and stories long before one can spell it out logically. My life has been a journey towards a truth that I could not always name, and in many ways, it still is. I certainly do not have all the answers now. But with more knowledge and lived experience, I can understand things a little better, and it is rewarding to see my past self grappling with the hints available at the time. I can only hope to continue doing so until my last breath.

The passage I shared on Instagram was from a chapter where I had a vision during one of the experiences. It is abridged here for convenience and conciseness:

> While meditating on these concerns, I experienced something I can only call a vision, though it felt closer to a divine epiphany or a moment of religious ecstasy.
> 
> In it, I saw a cave just beneath the earth's surface. At its center, a man was bound by strings, restricting him from top to bottom. With tremendous effort, he grasped one of the strings and ripped it apart. The act caused him unimaginable pain, yet despite the agony, he reached for another, and then another.
> 
> Eventually, he stood free, drenched in blood and trembling. As he climbed from the cave, a faint aura began to radiate from him. He emerged to behold the outside world for the first time, where a powerful light enveloped him—a radiant, divine brightness. By breaking his confining strings and escaping this cave of human limitations, he had entered a realm of goodness and truth.
> 
> What truly perplexes me is how he was able to break free in the first place. If you are bound by your own limitations, how can you possibly develop the capacity to transcend them? This is the paradox: overcoming your constraints while being shaped entirely by them.

This particular vision of the cave is a powerful reminder of that process. Even if you completely disagree with my take, the greater lesson is that you should grapple with what calls to you. Even when the attempt to think it through yields nothing but a messy, cloudy puddle of images and metaphors, that is the very essence of thinking.

The vision I had is, of course, a very direct echo of Plato’s Allegory of the Cave. At a structural level, the parallels are intentional. Both employ the cave as a powerful metaphor for a state of ignorance and constraint. In both, a central figure is bound, representing the forces that inhibit our full being. Each liberation is depicted as an arduous and painful struggle. Both culminate in a difficult but successful ascent towards a brilliant light, the ultimate symbol for goodness, truth, intelligibility, and ultimate reality.

There are also some divergences worth exploring. The first and most significant departure concerns the locus of agency. In Plato’s allegory, the prisoner is a passive recipient of an external force. He is compelled and dragged from the cave. This externalist, pedagogical model is deeply tied to Plato’s political philosophy, which posits that the unenlightened must be guided by a philosopher educator who has already attained enlightenment. My vision, on the other hand, proposes an internalist, autodidactic model. The man frees himself. The impulse for liberation originates entirely from within.

This leads to the ontological status of the constraints themselves. The vision places a particular emphasis on the image of strings. At the time, influenced by Buddhist thought, I was very concerned with the problem of free will, accepting that our actions and thoughts are conditioned by genetics, environment, and causality more broadly. Logically, I could not see any way out of it, although I also had this visceral, undeniable sense of free will that I found hard to reconcile, especially given that to me this felt so fundamental to morality.

The strings symbolized these very constraints, representing the restricting fabric of our determined way of being. Unlike Plato’s chains, which bind the prisoner to a world of shadows and illusions, the strings are not an epistemological error per se but are restrictions to one’s freedom. I believe epistemology is not necessarily a separate problem that can be disjoined from agency, but this is a useful distinction to make for the time being.

The phenomenology of liberation in both stories is also distinct in its bodily character. The struggle is a profoundly visceral event, rather than a primarily cognitive and contemplative one. The path to freedom is not merely the discomfort of a new idea but the embodied agony of life, of continuing to live, of accepting suffering, and of a constant, eternal self making. A pain that must not only be endured but also self inflicted to achieve true liberation. It is a theme that resonates with both one of the most common aspects of the phenomenology of the psychedelic experience, such as one’s death for a ‘higher’ rebirth, and also a core theme of Christianity.

During the session, I immediately recognized the paradox at the heart of this vision. Liberation in Plato’s version is causally straightforward because an external agent acts upon the prisoner. But in my version, we free ourselves. How is this possible? The very condition of this unfreedom, of being so tightly bound, is precisely the inability to initiate movement. I posed the question, but at the time, I had no answer.

My problem at the time was that my thinking was oriented entirely from the bottom up. I saw man as a being who, through his own isolated effort, had to reach for the ultimate Good. But this is an impossible task if one is truly bound. It took me a long time to realize that there is also a "top down" element, an intrusion of grace into the world. Herein lies the solution. You are not entirely restricted, because you can move if you _believe_ you can move.

This is the recognition that genuine transformation requires an act of faith, a Kierkegaardian leap. It is a belief, however faint, that you are destined for the light precisely because you are, in some essential way, constituted by it. To put it another way, one must already be free in some sense in order to become free. This idea is already present in Plato’s concept of anamnesis, the soul's innate, forgotten knowledge of the Good. Yet, for Plato, this dormant potential must be activated by an external teacher. My vision suggested something different, proposing that this innate connection could be the very source of our own initiating agency.

Without knowing it at the time, this intuition pushed me toward Gnostic and Neoplatonic thought and language. This grammar, parts of which evolved into Christian theology, felt more reflective of my experience than the Buddhism I had been trying to articulate. I intuited the necessity of something akin to what Christianity calls grace in transformation, but I could not articulate it beyond recognizing the paradox. Sometimes, being unable to answer is the answer. It is a sign that one’s entire framing of the problem is flawed. This notion of the leap, of following a small, intuitive hint that grows towards total transformation, is what I later explored in the book with the metaphor of chasing the white rabbit. It is that esoteric call, that glimpse of a deeper possibility, that one must have the faith to follow.

I was utterly lost about how liberation was achieved. How I would explain it now to my younger self is that the cave is never absolute. Fissures of light always penetrate the gloom, no matter how deep one is buried. Human beings, by their nature, possess a capacity to recognize that light. Once it is noticed, it can be sought. In that search, a new possibility dawns, revealing that one can, in fact, move. The motion is infinitesimal, the pain immense. But it is possible. And so, fueled by a kind of divine discontent, an insatiable hunger for that pure light, one begins to rip the strings. One at a time. I am still pulling at those strings, still bathed in blood. A million more may remain, but the number does not matter. The struggle is the path. I will keep struggling, and in doing so, become ever more free.

I recognize that for some, much of what I have said here will sound empty. Metaphors upon metaphors with seemingly nothing underneath. A shallow poetic form speaking religiously about things that have no actual meaning. “Light,” you might ask, “what light?” The language is indeed poetic and metaphorical, but that does not make it less true. It may, in fact, be the most reasonable and useful way to speak about certain approaches to human experience that resist literal definition.

Language is fundamentally metaphorical in the first place. We simply take it for granted. When I say something is “empty” or “shallow,” I am already speaking in metaphors. These terms do not describe literal containers or bodies of water, but rather degrees of meaning. Words are imagined as vessels, and when they lack content they are “empty.” To call something “a shallow poetic form” draws on the metaphor that depth is significance, where depth stands for seriousness, richness, or substance. When I say “nothing underneath,” it relies on the vertical schema in which truth or reality lies beneath the surface, hidden from immediate view. And, of course, when I invoke “light,” this continues the pervasive metaphor that truth and understanding are illumination, while ignorance is darkness. All language is built like this.

A significant part of my book is a personal exploration of precisely these religious metaphors and ways of speaking. Coming from a scientific background, this type of language once seemed to me insane at worst, misguided at best. Slowly, however, I learned how to see what this symbolic language was pointing towards. I am not sure how well I managed to document that journey. It is much more a memoir than a systematic work of philosophy or theology. But perhaps for someone in similar shoes, feeling a calling to understand what value might lie in religion beyond the labels of make believe and superstition, it might serve as a useful testimony.
