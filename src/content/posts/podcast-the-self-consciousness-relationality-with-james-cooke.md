James Cooke is a neuroscientist whose intellectual journey began not in a laboratory but at age thirteen, when an intense rumination on Catholic theology collapsed into a spontaneous mystical experience, what Zen Buddhism would call kensho. That experience, he argues, revealed nothing supernatural. It revealed that reality is fundamentally groundless, relational, and whole. In this conversation, Cooke walks through the central thesis of his book The Dawn of Mind: that a framework called non-dual naturalism, drawing on relational quantum mechanics, Buddhist philosophy, and the free energy principle, dissolves the hard problem of consciousness by refusing to treat consciousness as a separate thing. He argues that mind is not a computation running in a skull but the epistemic relationship between a living organism and its world, present in all life from single cells upward. The conversation covers the no-self insight and its link to ethics, the difference between persons and selves, why LLMs are "fancy napkins," and the strange wisdom that reality may be neither real nor unreal.

## Who Is James Cooke?

James Cooke is a neuroscientist and author of The Dawn of Mind (2024), in which he develops a philosophy of consciousness grounded in non-dual metaphysics, relational quantum mechanics, and contemplative science. Trained in neuroscience, he draws on the free energy principle, predictive processing, and Buddhist philosophy of emptiness, particularly the Madhyamaka tradition of Nagarjuna, to argue that consciousness is inherently tied to life itself and is best understood as an epistemic relation rather than a separate phenomenon. He is also an advocate for integrating contemplative practice into mainstream mental health and scientific contexts.

## Top 3 theses

- Reality is fundamentally groundless, relational, and whole, all apparent separations are the product of object perception, not features of reality itself. This is the core of non-dual naturalism, and it makes better sense of quantum mechanics, contemplative insight, and the hard problem of consciousness than any framework that starts with separate things.

- Consciousness is not an extra ingredient added to physics, it is the epistemic relation a living organism bears to its world. You cannot have it without life, and you cannot locate it in a brain alone. The hard problem dissolves once you stop thingifying consciousness and recognize it as a process of knowingness inseparable from the dynamics of self-persistence.

- The no-self insight does not destroy ethics, it generalizes suffering automatically. Once you see that the self is a thought and that separateness is illusory, caring about your own suffering extends naturally to caring about suffering as such. The jump from insight to compassion is not an argument; it is a recognition.

## Key Insights

**1. A spontaneous mystical experience at 13 set the entire intellectual agenda.**

Cooke was a Catholic teenager terrified of hell, unable to believe without evidence. Ruminating on the paradox, he experienced what Zen calls kensho, a collapse of the conceptual frame in which a self exists in time. What remained was an immediate, unseparated present moment. Nothing supernatural. The experience felt like insight rather than delusion precisely because it relieved suffering viscerally, the way discovering there was never a snake in the room relieves fear. That conviction that the insight was genuine, not that it proved itself logically, but that everything stacked up to support it, became the epistemological method of his entire scientific career.

**2. Non-dual naturalism: reality is relational, not made of separate things.**

The central thesis of The Dawn of Mind is that the apparent world of separate objects, atoms, brains, selves, is a product of object perception, not a feature of reality itself. Reality is fundamentally relational: nothing exists with its own independent essence. A weed is only a weed from the gardener's perspective; remove the perspective and the essence of "weedhood" vanishes. Carlo Rovelli's relational quantum mechanics, Cooke argues, encodes precisely this insight at the level of physics. And the Buddhist philosopher Nagarjuna worked it out as emptiness, shunyata, centuries ago. When you adopt this relational picture, the mind stops being a ghostly extra inside the skull and becomes what it always was: a way of interacting with the world.

**3. Consciousness is life's epistemic relation to the world, not a brain trick.**

Cooke's most contested claim is that consciousness is coextensive with life. He distinguishes himself carefully from both enacted cognition (enactivism) and panpsychism: he is not saying rocks feel, and he is not saying only nervous systems do. His claim is that the teodynamics of self-persistence, the goal-directed drive to avoid death that characterises all living systems, is what generates genuine semantic meaning and, with it, experience. A single-celled organism holding a model of its world and acting on it is already doing something fundamentally different from a spreadsheet or an LLM. The presence of death as the horizon is what makes the model matter. Remove that horizon and you have a representation without stakes, ink on a napkin.

**4. The hard problem dissolves once you stop thingifying consciousness.**

David Chalmers's zombie thought experiment assumes consciousness is a separate thing that could, in principle, be pulled apart from physical processes. Cooke, following Buddhist philosopher Jay Garfield, argues that this assumption is itself the error. Just as "flying" cannot be conceptually separated from "the airplane" without ceasing to be about anything real, experience cannot be separated from the epistemic dynamics of life without being turned into a ghost. If you genuinely cannot conceive of pulling them apart, not as a failure of imagination, but as a recognition of their identity, there is no hard problem. There is only the process of knowingness, which is consciousness, which is what living things do.

**5. The no-self insight generalises suffering automatically, and that is the link to ethics.**

The question of why no-self entails compassion is one Cooke tackles with alien hand syndrome as his analogy: if two hands believed they were genuinely separate beings and went to war, and then discovered they were part of the same organism, the hand on fire would be extinguished by the hand holding water without any moral argument being needed. Once the delusion of selfhood lifts, suffering is just suffering, not my suffering. Cooke does not claim this can be argued to a psychopath. He claims that for living beings who have glimpsed the insight, the generalisation happens naturally and spontaneously, the same way you do not need a rule to tell you to put out a fire on your own hand.

**6. LLMs are artifacts, not organisms, and the distinction is everything.**

When Tiago raised the fact that LLMs build internal geometric models of spatial relationships, Cooke's response was precise: a model only acquires genuine meaning when it is embedded in a system that does not want to die. Life has to bootstrap its own epistemic relations from the ground up, anchored to the world through the stakes of survival. An LLM is constructed from the outside by an external intelligence; it has no teodynamics, no death horizon, and therefore no semantic anchoring. Writing the equations of predictive processing on a napkin does not make the napkin conscious. An LLM is a more sophisticated napkin. A driverless car is a model of life, not life itself.

**7. Reality is neither real nor unreal, and anti-realism is where the philosophy leads.**

Pushed on Kant and the gap between phenomena and noumena, Cooke revealed that his reading of emptiness leads him toward a position called irrealism, developed by Oxford Buddhist scholar Jan Westerhoff, which holds that there is no determinate world "behind" appearances to anchor the phenomena. Everything exists only conventionally and relationally, without essence. This does not mean nothing exists; apples still nourish you because the causal power lies in the whole relational web, not in any atom of the apple in isolation. But nothing is "actual" in the sense of being solid, self-grounded, and independent. The contemplative traditions land here too: potent void, emptiness, neither real nor unreal, not because they are being mystical, but because the logic of relationality leads there.
