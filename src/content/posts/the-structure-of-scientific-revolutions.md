**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Structure Of Scientific Revolutions_’ by_ Thomas Kuhn**

If you are interested in science or philosophy, you may have heard of Thomas Kuhn, and if you happen to have an interest in both, he holds an almost mythical status. Before Kuhn, the philosophy of science was mostly based on Popper’s ideas of falsification, but that view, while important, faded into the background after the publication of _The Structure of Scientific Revolutions_ in 1962. I finally decided to give the original book a shot without knowing if it would be worth it, given that I already possessed a general understanding of the concepts. The main theme fights against a view of continual scientific progress as a linear, ever-accumulating list of facts that lead up to where we are today. This view is still incredibly common, even among scientists, which is largely what prompted me to dig deeper into the idea. Despite the incredible popularity and success of Kuhn’s work, it seems to have remained more in philosophy than in science, and sometimes it appears the world has moved on without taking its insights seriously.

Kuhn distinguishes two modes of scientific endeavors: normal science, which is the typical view, and scientific revolutions. Normal science occurs when the paradigm is stable, and its assumptions are so strong that one can hardly understand how reality could be otherwise. This is not only an accepted theory of how the world works, which impacts our perception, but it also impacts the perception of how we ought to further gain knowledge. It is a shared cognitive framework with a specific set of values, tools, methods, and techniques. In this normal mode, science tends to develop its paradigm, to dig deeper, and to add detail. This is the main counterpoint to Popper. While falsification sounds like the correct approach in an abstract philosophical mode of conducting science, Kuhn describes how science is actually done in the real world. Rather than trying to prove something wrong, most science is done to add details and clarification. However, in this process of normal science, abnormalities often appear. All paradigms have abnormalities, phenomena that do not fit into our model, including today’s paradigms in many fields. These are often brushed aside with post-hoc rationalizations or the hope that they are irrelevant and that we will be able to fit the abnormality into our model in the future with better methods or technology. When such abnormalities start to accumulate, especially if a pattern starts to emerge in them, the stability of the paradigm begins to shake. In this situation, the paradigm itself is questioned, and the fundamentals are revised.

Extraordinary research tends to be conducted when the goal is not only to add detail to the accepted paradigm but to widen the scope in a less traditional manner. Philosophy is revived and works alongside science while alternative paradigms are proposed and fight against each other. The paradigm that can explain the most abnormalities and make the best predictions eventually becomes the accepted paradigm, constituting a scientific revolution. This is the second way science operates, a completely different mode than the normal science previously covered. The change of paradigm is hardly a soft transition, however. New paradigms are often fiercely opposed for a long time, and scientists tend to hold on to the previous paradigm as much as possible. As Max Planck said, “a new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents and making them see the light, but rather because its opponents eventually die, and a new generation grows up that is familiar with it.” After a scientific revolution, normal science resumes. This is why it is hard to recognize revolutions, as we generally tend to live in a period of normal science, and revolutions of the past are not emphasized. Even if not explicitly hidden, they are often not perceived as revolutions at all but incorrectly as simply significant points of the accumulation of knowledge. Kuhn claims this is often reinforced by textbooks, which have the practical goal of teaching students the methods and knowledge needed to participate in the normal science they are about to enter. Therefore, history is of little importance, and many scientists go through their education without knowing the historical turmoil of their field. He does not condemn this _per se_ since it has little practical value for students to know it, but it nevertheless provides a false impression of how science has progressed.

Kuhn gives several examples of scientific revolutions, ranging from the shift from Ptolemaic cosmology to a Copernican one, Aristotelian mechanics to classical mechanics, Euclidean to non-Euclidean geometry, Lavoisier’s theory of chemical reactions, and Newtonian gravity to general relativity. The important point that is often missed, even by people familiar with Kuhn’s idea, is that these aren’t just discoveries of new knowledge. They are complete overthrows of worldviews in their specific domains where how scientists think, view the world, and conduct research becomes completely new. My favorite example from the book is how people viewed pendulums. Under an Aristotelian paradigm, a swinging object was viewed as falling because it is its nature to do so, but it was constrained by the chain or string. Therefore, its swinging is a falling motion. With a Galilean paradigm, however, it is a different phenomenon entirely. Galileo saw a body almost succeeding in repeating the same motion over and over again but unable to do so and eventually stopping. They are the same phenomenon but vastly different interpretations. This perception element of scientific frameworks is one of the biggest takeaways from his work. Regarding the book itself, beyond Kuhn’s ideas, I have mixed feelings. On the one hand, I felt it dove into details that aren’t commonly known even to those aware of the concept of scientific revolutions. On the other hand, the message could have been better delivered. While the writing is good, the content often feels repetitive. For example, Newton’s _Principia_ or Lavoisier’s _Elements_ are mentioned dozens of times, which is odd considering the author keeps mentioning that many other examples could have been used but that he lacked the time to go in-depth into them. I felt that this could have been vastly improved, but nevertheless, it was still a great read. Even if you’re familiar with his work, you will get some extra insights, and if you’re not, then you’re in for a treat.
