_This essay is a slightly expanded version of a talk I gave at the International Interdisciplinary Conference of Psychedelic Studies (IICPS 2026) organized by the University of Bucharest in June 2026.&nbsp;_

https://www.youtube.com/embed/kS0zMHOjHoY?rel=1

People take psychedelics and report meeting God, dissolving into the universe, becoming love, dying and being reborn, or seeing reality for the first time. Whatever we make of those reports, they are particularly philosophically interesting, because they are not merely strange experiences. They often seem to matter deeply to the people who have them.

Psychedelics seem genuinely therapeutically useful for conditions like addiction and depression. But their therapeutic promise is bound up with the strangeness of the experience itself. The therapeutic and the mystical are not independent features that happen to co-occur. They seem to be connected and interdependent.

In the landmark Griffiths studies, 67% of participants ranked their psilocybin session among the 5 most meaningful experiences of their lives, and 71% among the 5 most spiritually significant at 2 months, with the ranking still holding at 14 months (Griffiths et al., 2006, 2008). Neitzke-Spruill and colleagues (2024) describe transformative experiences that change identity, values, beliefs, desires, and behavior in nearly all participants. And the mystical quality of the experience, rather than its raw intensity, is the best candidate for the active ingredient in the therapeutic outcome (Yaden & Griffiths, 2021; Roseman et al., 2018). Across 34 studies, religion and spirituality rank highest among durable positive life changes following psychedelic use (Schutt et al., 2024).

So the mystical element clearly matters, and therefore we need to take the mystical seriously. Often there is a reflex not to. Mysticism has a bad reputation in science, so there is a tendency either to recast it as neuroplasticity, where the subjective experience becomes epiphenomenal (Olson, 2020; Ly et al., 2018), or to demolish the category of the mystical altogether (Sanders & Zijlmans, 2021). These are the typical mysticism wars. Yet through all of it, the mystical experience remains one of the strongest predictors of therapeutic benefit. Explaining it away leaves the most interesting fact on the table.

The way the field tends to explain it away follows one of two directions. In the philosophy of psychedelics there are, broadly speaking, two gravitational pulls. This is a bit of a false dichotomy and I am oversimplifying, but it is a helpful frame. On one side, especially from the neuroscience-adjacent camp, the talk is of brain dynamics: predictive processing, relaxed priors, a destabilized self-model. Everything happens in the brain, and the mystical experience gets explained that way. It is real as an experience, but it discloses nothing. Chris Letheby’s monograph&nbsp;_Philosophy of Psychedelics_&nbsp;(2021) is the best developed version of this deflationary position, though he has shifted somewhat since.

On the other side is the view that the experience is evidence, whether proof or suggestion, that we need to revise our metaphysics from the default position of assumed naturalism. Peter Sjöstedt-Hughes (2021) is a good example, and he has done serious work on panpsychism along these lines. Either way, both poles put the question into camps: either it is purely in my mind, or it is a brute fact of reality out there. And both treat the experience as a proposition. Did people encounter something real? Yes or no. As Meling and Scheidegger (2024) point out, both positions share the same root: the tendency to grasp after a ground, whether out in the world or inside the head, and to treat the experience as true or false of that ground. The deflationary reading misses the radical context-dependency of the experience; the revisionary reading misses its observer-relativity. I think this shared assumption is a mistake, and not a helpful one. It is not how human beings operate in the world. It is not what cognition is, and I would argue it is not what reality is either.

Heidegger made this point explicitly. There is an element of disclosure of the world that comes before we even start asking about correspondence between what is in my brain and what is out there. In “On the Essence of Truth” (1930), he writes:

> _"truth is not a feature of correct propositions that are asserted of an 'object' by a human 'subject' and then 'are valid' somewhere, in what sphere we know not; rather, truth is disclosure of beings through which an openness essentially unfolds."_

Before any proposition can be true or false, a meaningful world must already have shown up for someone. There is a large lineage here: Heidegger drawing on Husserl, then on through Merleau-Ponty and Dreyfus.

How I think we should put it now, with the benefit of 4E cognitive science, is that meaning is not simply found, ready-made in the object, but it is not projected from inside the subject either. Jaeger, Vervaeke and colleagues (2024) call this the&nbsp;_transjective_, which I find a genuinely helpful term: meaning is enacted in the coupling of agent and world, with no location, neither out there nor in here. There is always a co-construction between self and world. The worked case is the affordance, from Gibson’s ecological psychology. Gibson (1979) makes the point explicit in his original writing:

> _“An affordance is neither an objective property nor a subjective property"; it "points both ways, to the environment and to the observer."_

It is a fact of the environment and a fact of behavior at once. The cliff really affords falling; the cup really affords drinking. But it affords these things&nbsp;_to an agent_&nbsp;with a particular body and particular concerns. The affordance is real, out there, and yet always specific to the coupling. And this scales up: from bodily grip on a cup to what Bruineberg and Rietveld (2014) call the search for&nbsp;_optimal grip_&nbsp;on a whole field of affordances, all the way into higher cognition (Rietveld & Kiverstein, 2014; Beyköylü, Vervaeke & Meling, 2025a). On this view, the psychedelic experience is a&nbsp;_participatory disclosure_&nbsp;of a meaningful world. Participation names the mode; disclosure, in the sense of&nbsp;_alētheia_, names what happens.

This matters especially for psychedelics because context matters enormously. Set and setting are king. Just before the conference I came across yet another paper showing that you can reproduce many elements of the psychedelic experience through set and setting alone, without any substance whatsoever. The molecule is a cause, of course. But an autonomous organism does not passively receive it as an instruction. By what enactivists call operational closure, the organism takes up the perturbation according to its own ongoing organization, history, needs, and environment. So the meaning of the experience is not simply in the molecule, nor simply in the brain, but in the whole coupling between drug, organism, world, and context. As Meling and Scheidegger (2023) put it, the information&nbsp;_“does not belong to the psychedelic molecule... it must belong to the coupling.”_&nbsp;Hence the title of their paper: not in the drug, not in the brain.

Early in the neuroscience of psychedelics there was a tendency to treat set and setting as side ingredients, nuisance variables to be controlled in clinical trials. That turns out to be wrong for many reasons. Kiverstein, Beyköylü and van Elk (2025) argue that set and setting have&nbsp;_“the status of causes, no less real than the cellular and molecular causes.”_&nbsp;Sanneke de Haan's (2020) enactive framework is useful here. In psychiatry, and in psychedelic experience specifically, the relevant factors are not separable ingredients that each contribute a fixed, additive effect. They are more like the components of a single bake: the substance, the person's psychological state, and the environment combine into one interdependent process, so changing any one of them changes what all the others do. You cannot hold set and setting constant and vary the drug in isolation, because the drug's effect is partly constituted by the set and setting it occurs within.

Set maps onto embodiment, setting onto embeddedness (see also Smith, 2025). You can see the causal power directly in the outcomes. In the Griffiths (2006) study, with a carefully optimized living-room setting, 61% of participants had a complete mystical experience. In Liechti’s (2017) work, in a standard hospital room, the figure was 12.5%. Same drug class, wildly different settings, wildly different results. If you optimize the whole context, the difference is dramatic compared to hoping the molecule does something on its own. The upshot is that the experience reorganizes a meaningful world. It does not detect an object.

If the experience reorganizes a world rather than detecting an object, then the pressing question becomes what kind of reorganization we are actually after, and here the current narrative runs into trouble. It talks overwhelmingly about loosening and dissolution of the self, which is connected to the first, deflationary pole. And it does happen. We can intuit it phenomenologically, and there is decent neuroscience behind it. The self can be pictured as a deep, carved basin in a landscape; psychedelics flatten the landscape. This flattening is plausibly part of the therapeutic benefit. The conditions that respond to psychedelics are precisely those where people get fixed on something, where experience loops in on itself. In depression the world closes in: rumination, collapsing self-worth, everything narrowing and narrowing. Something similar holds for addiction and trauma. There is a pathological structure, and the experience breaks it down. In complex systems terms, a flattening of the basins.

But flattening is not the complete picture, because there also needs to be reorganization, and this part is not given its due. Beyköylü, Vervaeke and Meling (2025b) describe four ways the opened state can settle. First, positive change: the system lands in a more stable, healthier basin. Second, reversion: it slides back into the old maladaptive state. Third, and this is the one the literature does not like to talk about, a novel but still pathological configuration. Anyone familiar with psychedelic culture has encountered this anecdotally: people who get into a rut of repeated use, do not get better, sometimes get worse, and all the while believe they are getting enlightened. That is exactly what a novel-but-undesirable reorganization looks like from the inside. Fourth, collapse.

Even REBUS, the model from which most of the dissolution talk descends, concedes the point. Carhart-Harris and Friston (2019) admit that whether model reduction leaves a robust, reliable representation is an open question. Safron and colleagues (2025) go further with their ALBUS model: relaxation of priors is only one arm of what psychedelics do, and beliefs can also be strengthened depending on dose and context. The same strengthening yields healing insight and delusion alike. And McGovern et al. (2024) show that felt certainty cannot arbitrate, because false insights arise from the same relaxed-prior machinery as true ones, and conviction tracks fluency rather than truth. There is a lot of concern in the literature about false insights, but pathological and healthy meaning-making run on more or less the same machinery. From a purely neuroscientific or complex-systems perspective, there is no way to tell them apart. Hipólito and colleagues (2023) draw the structural conclusion: flattening is not enough, because health is multistability, and the opened system must settle into some new stable state. Directionality matters. So the question we should be asking much more insistently is:&nbsp; **reorganization toward what?**

To answer that, come back to the Heideggerian point, because the world we reorganize toward is never a neutral one, and it is never neutral for principled cognitive-scientific reasons rather than merely psychological ones. The problem of relevance realization is the key (Vervaeke, Lillicrap & Richards, 2012). Reality offers far too much to take in raw. It would be uncomputable to look at the world neutrally even if that were possible in the first place. To perceive anything stable at all, cognition must relentlessly select what is relevant. This is close to what Heidegger meant by&nbsp;_care_&nbsp;in&nbsp;_Being and Time_: we never meet neutral matter. Things show up as useful, threatening, inviting, possible. They show up in a particular way and for a particular reason, and this is built into how a world appears at all. Our categorization itself, which is much of what cognition is made of, is purpose-driven. A thing becomes intelligible through the good it serves. Take the coffee mug I had with me on the podium. The cupness of the cup, its mugness, is bound up with the goods it affords me. Holding and drinking gather it into meaning. It is good&nbsp;_for_&nbsp;something. But many goods claim us, and they conflict. So we need a frame that orders them, a hierarchy of goods. And once you think in terms of a hierarchy, you necessarily get a peak, an apex of care. Tillich (1957) called this our&nbsp;_ultimate concern_: the highest concern, which orders all the goods below it. My overall stance toward what ultimately matters has downstream effects on how every lesser thing shows up. It structures my entire being. And here is the bridge to lived experience: at the scale of perception, a hierarchy of goods becomes a hierarchy of attention.

What I think is really missing from psychedelic research and philosophy is the recognition that attention is morally structured. People avoid this because morality is awkward territory for science. But it follows from everything above, and it can be stated in rigorous cognitive-scientific terms. None of this is woo. Kiverstein, Beyköylü and van Elk (2025) describe attention as precision weighting that&nbsp;_“sculpts the agent’s moment-to-moment relationship with its environment, structuring the field of relevant affordances.”&nbsp;_If the world appears through relevance, care, and purpose, then attention is not neutral selection. Attention is how some goods become vivid while others fade. And since we are social beings, the field of affordances includes other agents with their own cares and their own ways of seeing. Attention always operates within a moral world.

Two philosophers should be talked about far more in this context: Simone Weil and Iris Murdoch. Weil’s central insight is that attention is not a mental force. It is not effortful grasping. It is a kind of waiting, a receptivity you have to make room for. As the ego lets go, the real appears: the world that is not being pathologically contained by your own fantasies and imaginings. And for Weil this has an unavoidable religious tone, because full contact with the real just is the apex of experience. Her formulation captures it perfectly with:

> _“Absolutely unmixed attention is prayer.”_

Murdoch builds directly on Weil. In&nbsp;_The Sovereignty of Good_&nbsp;(1970) she writes that moral change comes from an attention to the world whose natural result is a decrease in egoism, through an increased sense of the reality of, primarily, other people, but also of other things. Her famous example is the mother-in-law, M, who learns to re-see her daughter-in-law, D. At first M finds D pert, brusque, tiresomely juvenile, a girl her son has "married beneath." M behaves impeccably throughout and never lets her real opinion show, so nothing in her outward conduct ever changes. What changes is her attention. Suspecting herself of being snobbish and jealous, she looks again, and over time D is discovered to be&nbsp;_"not vulgar but refreshingly simple, not undignified but spontaneous, not noisy but gay, not tiresomely juvenile but delightfully youthful."&nbsp;_No new facts entered the world. Attention reconfigured salience: what matters, how the situation makes sense. And the reconfiguration was toward reality, not away from it.

Murdoch’s conceptual grammar here is Platonist, and I think it is good grammar we should use more in the field of psychedelics. We do not invent the ranking of goods from nowhere. We are invited to it. This is how nearly all of human history has understood human experience: reality extends an invitation, and we participate in a field of affordances that includes moral affordances. As she puts it:

> _“Good is the magnetic centre towards which love naturally moves.”_

That last point about invitation matters, because it turns attention from a momentary act into something that forms the person doing the attending. How you pay attention is, more or less, how you inhabit the world. It is the basis of your experience, and so it is a good way to think about the self: your being is how you attend. Murdoch again:

> _“Our ability to act well when the time comes depends partly, perhaps largely, upon the quality of our habitual objects of attention.”_

And she ties this to&nbsp;_metanoia_, the change of being, which “is not brought about by straining and will-power, but by a long deep process of unselfing” (Murdoch, 1992). That is Weil’s letting-go again: through unselfing you grasp the real, and you are in a process of continual transformation as you try to align yourself with the good.

There is a genuine puzzle here, though, and L. A. Paul (2014) states it sharply. How do you know what you are transforming into, or whether you should? The transformation changes the one being transformed. The old self cannot calculate the new life, because the change alters both what you know and who is doing the knowing. Agnes Callard (2018) has, I think, the right answer: aspiration. The old self can still aspire. You glimpse a good you can only partly see, and you walk toward it even without fully understanding it, and you come to understand it more the more you practice and embody it. We can glimpse a good before we can inhabit it; the Good is participated in before it is fully understood. In walking toward it, you become the kind of person able to see it more fully. The bridge between your actual self and your future self is enacted in the world. It cannot be inferred in your head.

All this talk of attention might sound individualistic. It is not. Attention is communal, because we learn to attend from others, and we calibrate how we see the world against how others see it. Again, this is standard cognitive science, not woo. Rietveld and Kiverstein (2014) describe skill acquisition as an education of attention that&nbsp;_“crucially involves other practitioners who selectively introduce the novice to the right aspects of the environment.”_&nbsp;And there is a substantial literature on how our sense of what to walk toward is shaped by exemplars. We move toward persons who embody goods, not toward abstract principles; we learn the good by imitating admired models (Zagzebski, 2017; Henrich, 2015). Neuroplasticity does not tell us what a person becomes. It only tells us the person is more shapeable. What shapes the opened state is the ecology of attention into which it returns. And the timescales make this unavoidable: the plasticity a session opens persists for weeks or months afterward (Cole-Turner, 2025, citing Reardon, 2023).

"Setting" in the clinical sense covers only the session itself. The reorganization consolidates outside the model, in the ordinary social environment the person goes back to. What operates at that timescale is not a controlled setting. It is a culture: its values, its practices, its relationships. That is not in the substance. That is a way of life. This is where religion comes in, understood functionally. One of the proposed etymologies of religion is religio, from re-ligare, to bind. Vervaeke and Mastropietro (2025) describe relevance realization itself as "a binding between us and the world," and it is this binding that religion exists to intensify and direct. That is what religion is supposed to be: the enhancement of this coupling. In the talk I emphasized the communal aspect because it was easiest to present in the time I had, but ritual plays a large role here, as do symbols and narrative. The important point is that it all comes as a package: a tradition trains attention toward a telos and then helps you along the way as much as it can, discerning, stabilizing, and ordering the disclosure.

Here is what I think is missing from so much philosophy of psychedelics. We care enormously about transformation in the psychedelic context. Why do we not care about it in everyday life? That is largely what religion is about, if you look at it from this frame, which I think is historically accurate. Huston Smith saw this back in 1964, in one of the classic papers of the field:

> _“Drugs appear able to induce religious experiences; it is less evident that they can produce religious lives.”_

If you are secular and the phrase “religious life” puts you off, substitute “virtue-oriented life.” It is close enough for the argument. My prediction is that what I call thick ecologies of attention, meaning a telos, ritual, exemplars, community, and practices of discernment, will outperform an experience-focused frame, and will even outperform the experience-plus-integration frame as it currently exists. Because current integration is mostly thin: talk about the experience a bit, maybe try to meditate. We need to treat the whole thing as a package, which is exactly what religions do.

This connection has some empirical support. The Schutt et al. (2024) systematic review, covering 34 studies and nearly 20,000 participants, links psychedelic use to long-term spiritual growth: meaning, connection, spiritual faith, increased religious or spiritual practice. Gezon’s (2024) ethnographic fieldwork on integration groups suggests that community and “social efficacy” matter for processing and stabilizing psychedelic experiences, though the evidence there is qualitative. In Griffiths et al. (2018), enduring changes at 6 months were associated with both the acute mystical experience and continued meditation or spiritual practice. And in experienced meditators, psilocybin supported insightfulness in a retreat context, with effects linked to open monitoring meditation (Singer et al., 2024). The experience matters, and what surrounds the experience matters, and the surrounding structure is where the leverage is.

Importantly, if we accept that a neutral stance is not an option as we previously covered, then where no good is explicitly proposed, one gets supplied by default. The market is also an ecology of attention. It differs from religion only in what occupies the apex. You can see the default filling in across the psychedelic space. Microdosing trains attention toward performance: the mystical with transformation explicitly removed, use without reverence. The retreat and therapy market sells the session without the ecology that would hold it: thin, episodic, for-profit. The pathology model truncates the telos to baseline, so the person is reorganized just enough to function. Traditions aimed at saints and buddhas; the clinic aims at getting people “back to work.” Aspiration is deleted from the picture. And in Byung-Chul Han’s (2017) terms, psychopolitics completes the circuit: the self optimized for labor, with even mystical experience harnessed as performance enhancement, and the market as de facto ultimate concern. The choice is never whether our worlds get reorganized. The choice is by what.

So this is what I mean by the sacred as participatory disclosure. A meaningful world is a field of ranked affordances, and cognition is the ongoing search for the right grip on that field. Attention trains this grip at the personal scale; religion trains it at the cultural scale. The sacred is its deepest transformation. The sacred is not a supernatural object added to the world. Nor is it a private feeling inside the skull. It is a&nbsp;_meta-meaning function_: participatory knowing globalized.

It is the judgement upon which&nbsp;_other judgments_&nbsp;are made, your base orientation toward everything, the ranking of the field of goods and of your orientation toward the other. A world disclosed as meaningful, morally demanding, and worthy of reverence beyond use. And in disclosure, in&nbsp;_alētheia_, reality shines into our framing and at the same time withdraws beyond it, never exhausted by our grasp. If the earlier cognitive-science material was about grip, then the sacred, understood as participatory disclosure, is the ultimate cognitive grip. The optimal grip of optimal grips. It names what all that plasticity is&nbsp;_for_.

I also think this frame has practical bite for psychedelic science, in at least two places. First, the rate of non-response, which is not discussed nearly enough. We tend to shrug: some people get better, some do not, that is how it is. To some degree, yes. But if the disclosure depends on the whole ecology of attention rather than the molecule, maybe we can improve it. Second, the rate at which the effects diminish. Durability is exactly what a thick ecology, rather than a one-off experience, would be expected to buy you. Transformation is what we prize in psychedelics. The traditions have been in the transformation business for millennia. It would be strange not to learn from them.
