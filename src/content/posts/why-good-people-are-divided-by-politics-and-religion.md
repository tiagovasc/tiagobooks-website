**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_The Righteous Mind_’ by_ Jonathan Haidt**

The book begins by investigating the origins of morality and attempting to answer how it differs across cultures, how it often stems from gut feelings of disgust or disrespect, and how it stretches far beyond the simple understanding of harm. Haidt lays down three basic frameworks regarding the origin of moral knowledge: the nativist view, the empiricist view, and the rationalist view. The latter was the dominant paradigm when Haidt entered the field of social psychology, yet he explains why this framework is incorrect using solid arguments and research, some of which he authored himself. Ever since I began studying psychology, and specifically cognitive biases, I have come to realize that the view I once held of myself and human beings as rational agents has been effectively demolished. Haidt calls this the "rationalist delusion," a term I find quite fitting given how prevalent the misconception remains in scientific and philosophical circles. This flattering view of ourselves can only be maintained if one is completely oblivious to the evidence of how truly flawed our cognition is. We are indeed capable of reasoning, but it is rarely unbiased reasoning. Instead, it serves to justify our pre-existing beliefs, often without us even realizing it. Haidt provides a strong case for viewing morality as based on intuitions which are then defended post-hoc by reason. This is essentially a Humean model with a social add-on. The mind is divided into two parts using the metaphor of a rider, representing conscious controlled cognition, and an elephant, representing automatic intuitive cognition. While we identify as the rider, we evolved to serve the elephant. Even when our reasoning is poor, our moral intuitions remain unchanged because social and political judgments depend on the elephant’s intuition. Our moral thinking is much more like a politician searching for votes than a scientist searching for truth.

The text then explores Moral Foundations Theory, which constitutes the bulk of Haidt’s life work alongside Jesse Graham, Richard Shweder, and others. The core insight is that morality is broader than just harm and fairness, though it is often hard for us to see beyond those two pillars because they dominate our specific social circles. Haidt points out that people who grow up in Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) societies are statistical outliers rather than the norm. We tend to frame the world as a collection of separate objects and individuals, whereas other cultures often focus on relationships and context. Haidt identifies six distinct aspects or "taste receptors" of morality: Care, Fairness, Loyalty, Authority, Sanctity, and Liberty. Each evolved for a specific evolutionary purpose, and different political frameworks are essentially different combinations of these ingredients. Liberalism is dominated almost exclusively by Care and Fairness, while conservatism utilizes a more balanced menu of all six foundations, which appears to be the norm outside of WEIRD cultures. Each moral matrix creates a "team" that one belongs to, and it becomes very difficult for people to see outside of their own matrix. These beliefs likely have a root in a form of modularity, which are innate receptors that bias us, though they do not determine us, toward a certain team.

Finally, Haidt presents a thesis on the importance of groups, morality, and evolution. As he memorably puts it, human beings are 90% chimp and 10% bee. He provides four main arguments for group selection, a concept that remains controversial in the field. These arguments include the idea that major transitions produce superorganisms, that shared intentionality generates moral matrices, that genes and cultures coevolve, and that evolution can occur much faster than commonly thought. He calls our special ability to work in groups the "Hive Switch," which is the 10% that makes us unique compared to chimps. We have the capacity to transcend self-interest and work for something larger than ourselves. This is largely based on Emile Durkheim’s idea of _Homo duplex_, which suggests a division between the profane, where we exist as individuals, and the sacred, where we exist as part of the whole. This aspect of our nature is undeniable, and Haidt provides good arguments to support it, eventually tying our "bee nature" to religion and its role in social binding rather than just theological belief. It is truly a fantastic book and a must-read for anyone interested in understanding politics, morality, or human beings in general. While it is not a dense technical manual, it certainly gives you a comprehensive look at how anthropology and social science relate to the modern political landscape. As Haidt concludes, morality binds and blinds. It binds us into ideological teams that fight each other as though the fate of the world depended on our side winning each battle, but it blinds us to the fact that each team is composed of good people who have something important to say.
