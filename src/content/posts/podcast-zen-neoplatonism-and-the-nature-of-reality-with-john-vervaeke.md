Raised in a fundamentalist Christian family he later left in therapy, cognitive scientist John Vervaeke could have become one of religion's most effective debunkers. Instead, he became its unlikely defender. In this conversation, he traces the path from religious trauma through Socrates, Plotinus and Zen practice to his current identity as a Zen Neoplatonist. Religion, on his account, is less a belief system than a comprehensive way of orienting ourselves towards reality, one that science itself quietly presupposes.

The conversation moves through the limits of the first generation of cognitive science of religion, the relation between truth and relevance, and the historical formation of the God of the West. John explains how Zen's practice of emptying and Neoplatonism's search for synoptic integration correct one another, why traditions develop through what he calls semiotic symbiosis, and how a person can belong deeply to one tradition without closing themselves to others. We end with the danger of becoming a guru, the practices John uses to resist audience capture, and what he means by God.

### **Who Is John Vervaeke?**

John Vervaeke is a cognitive scientist at the University of Toronto, where he teaches psychology, cognitive science and Buddhist psychology. He is best known for _Awakening from the Meaning Crisis_ and his work on relevance realization. He founded the Vervaeke Foundation and the Lectern teaching platform, and describes himself as a Zen Neoplatonist. I’m also lucky enough to be his student.&nbsp;

### **Top Takeaways**

- Science and religion need not be enemies. Vervaeke treats both as ecologies of practices for reaching the depths of reality, while arguing that the usual conflict compares good science with bad religion and good religion with bad science.

- Religion is not primarily a list of propositions about a supernatural being. It is a comprehensive orientation towards meaning, one that shapes how different forms of meaning connect across a life.

- Science depends on presuppositions it cannot prove without already using them. The assumption that intelligibility tracks reality, for example, cannot be justified from a position outside intelligibility itself.

- Zen and Neoplatonism correct one another. Zen breaks and empties frames; Neoplatonism builds them towards a synoptic view. Together they can open a person so that nothing is excluded while emptying them so that nothing is enclosed.

- Resisting the role of guru requires structures, not good intentions. Vervaeke describes the peers, students, organisations and personal relationships he relies on to remain answerable to others.

### Memorable Quotes

> “Neoplatonism phenomenologically opens me so that nothing is excluded, and Zen empties me so that nothing is enclosed, and they both lead me into pure non-duality, pure relationality.”

> “We should stop comparing good science to bad religion, and the fundamentalists should stop comparing good religion to bad science. We should compare good science to good religion, and bad science to bad religion.”

> “God is ultimate reality, primordial reality, that affords us the deepest sense of realness, the wisest orientation, the most consistent and maturing transformation, and thereby enlivens all of our sense experience and our conceptual space.”

### **Key Insights**

**The cognitive science of religion began by trying to explain religion away.** Vervaeke argues that its first generation smuggled in an unexamined naturalism and treated religious people as cognitively defective. A more mature field would study the ways religion coordinates meaning without assuming in advance that its object is unreal.

**Meaning joins truth and relevance.** We do not pursue every truth equally, only those that matter within a context, yet we also want our sense of relevance to be corrected by truth. Neither can be determined from a position wholly outside the other.

**Traditions develop through semiotic symbiosis.** Religions are not sealed historical essences. They merge, borrow, contest and transform one another. Vervaeke traces the God of the West through El, YHWH and Greek philosophy, treating this history as part of sacredness rather than evidence against it.

**A home tradition can function like secure attachment.** Deep belonging need not become a fortress. On Vervaeke's model, loving one tradition securely can free a person to encounter others without either collapsing their differences or treating them as enemies.

**Credibility combined with the power to provide meaning creates a danger.** Vervaeke describes his public position as an unusually good setup for a cult. His response is to insist on students and colleagues rather than followers, remain accountable to scientific communities, and cultivate people and organisations able to correct him.
