What does it take to bring scientific explanation and first-person experience back together? Philosopher Julian Kiverstein has spent his career on exactly that gap, with a foot in continental philosophy, cognitive neuroscience, and a psychiatry department in Amsterdam.&nbsp;

The conversation begins with the hard problem of consciousness, which he reframes by starting from consciousness rather than physics, and traces the gradual emergence of sentience from the simplest life. It then unpacks intentionality and his “skilled intentionality” framework, the critique of artificial intelligence inherited from Hubert Dreyfus, and a detailed treatment of Gibson’s affordances and relational realism.&nbsp;

Kiverstein explains why the computational theory of mind confuses a useful model for reality, drawing on Whitehead and the concept of information. The episode closes by scaling these ideas to human flourishing, mental illness, psychedelics and meditation, and a candid personal reflection on living in one’s head while studying the body.

## Who Is Julian Kiverstein?

Julian Kiverstein is Assistant Professor of Neurophilosophy at the University of Amsterdam and a senior researcher in psychiatry at Amsterdam UMC. Working across phenomenology, 4E cognitive science, and the free energy principle, he is best known for the Skilled Intentionality Framework and “A Rich Landscape of Affordances,” both developed with Erik Rietveld.

## Top Takeaways

1. The hard problem of consciousness is mis-posed. Instead of starting from dead matter and trying to extract consciousness, we should start from lived experience and ask how nature, properly understood, could already include it.

2. Cognition is skilled engagement, not computation. The mind is directed at the world through embodied skills and affordances, and the same account extends all the way from reaching for a cup to conversation and abstract thought.

3. Models are not the things they model. The brain can be usefully described as computational or Bayesian, but confusing that abstraction for reality is a fallacy of misplaced concreteness that leaves out what minds fundamentally are: living beings concerned for their own existence.

## **Memorable Quotes**

> _“Instead of trying to start from a physical universe and then get consciousness out of that, we reverse the situation and start from consciousness and then see how to get the physical universe out of consciousness.”_

> _“We don't say that when we model weather, there's weather happening inside of the model. In the same way, we shouldn't say that when we model the brain, the model has the properties of the system we are modeling.”_

> _"How could somebody so interested in embodiment be so much in their head? It was this big divide between the ideas I have about what it is to be human and the life that I led."_

## Key Insights

**The hard problem starts in the wrong place.** Kiverstein argues that beginning from a disenchanted, mathematized universe and then hunting for consciousness guarantees failure. Phenomenology inverts this: start from lived experience, the explanandum, and ask how science fits inside it.

**Sense-making is not yet sentience.** Following Evan Thompson, even bacteria do sense-making, relating to sugar and acid with rudimentary valence. But subjectivity proper emerges later, with nervous systems that coordinate sensing and moving into a single point of view.

**Subjectivity is a dynamic singularity.** When a creature like a jellyfish must coordinate distributed cells to move, sensing and acting converge on one body. That convergence, in Susan Hurley’s phrase a “dynamic singularity,” is the birth of both a nervous system and a point of view.

**Neutral monism dissolves the dichotomy.** Drawing on phenomenology and William James’s radical empiricism, Kiverstein favors a reality that is neither mental nor physical but prior to that divide, a metaphysics in which the hard problem looks far less intractable.

**Affordances are relational, not dispositional.** Against Turvey’s view of affordances as object-side dispositions, Kiverstein holds that affordances are relations between an organism’s skills and its environment, grounding a relational realism rather than a mind-independent one.
