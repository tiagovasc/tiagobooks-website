**_A book review of&nbsp;‘_When Breath Becomes Air_’ by_ Paul Kalanithi**

Warning: This review contains spoilers.

This is a memoir from Paul Kalanithi, a neurosurgeon who was diagnosed with stage IV metastatic lung cancer. The narrative begins by recounting his life story and the path that led to his position as a neurosurgeon, a section I found incredibly enjoyable and worthwhile on its own. However, the bulk of the book, and what it is truly about, is his diagnosis and how he deals with it. I believe stories like these always have value, teaching us a great deal if we know how to learn from them. This one, in particular, is mind-blowing due to Paul’s incredible personality and journey, aspects that are greatly enhanced by his fantastic writing.

What grabbed my attention immediately was how similar Paul was to me regarding certain questions he was trying to answer, yet I was dumbfounded at his approach to answering them. While I have read a fair bit on medicine, the existential perspective he explores within it is unique and fascinating. What I have always searched for in philosophy, he somehow found in medicine, something I had never even conceived of. His encounter with death in his profession, and how that relates to his life perspective, was described in detail and was a pleasure to read. However, it was almost odd to experience because it felt so personal, as if I were engaging in a conversation that was too intimate or deserving of greater privacy. It is a direct look into a soul that most people aren’t willing to share, or if they are, they often lack the articulation to do so.

His love for philosophy and literature, and how it blends with his medical practice, makes this memoir distinct. I am sure there are many interesting stories from neurosurgeons and many biographies about dealing with cancer, but Paul’s combination of interests and his talent for writing make this book precious beyond measure. Even cancer aside, it is a wonderful description of what medicine is truly about. Seeing Paul’s dedication to it and how much it meant to him is awe-inspiring. The book offers a perspective that is hard to explain, as it represents a type of knowledge that cannot be fully articulated but dives directly into the deepest aspects of life. Beyond this knowledge, which almost feels like a sin to define so simply, his story is inspirational beyond comprehension in how he deals with his own death. The way he accepted his mortality and shifted his life according to what his health made available to him seems like unreachable wisdom to most of us.

It is a fantastic read for anyone interested in neurosurgery and oncology, but the core message can appeal to anyone because it is ultimately about death, and therefore about life. It asks what it means to live, what makes life worthwhile, and how to die. It is a must-read for everyone, and it feels like a blessing that his story was recorded and described by himself in such detail. Even though I am not a very emotional person, I could not help but cry when reading it.
