Your mind is not in your skull. It is the activity of a living body in constant traffic with its environment, and it does not stop at the edge of the nervous system. The brain plays a crucial role. But it is one organ in a larger system that takes in the body and the world, and that whole system is the smallest unit that thinks.

This is the claim of enactivism, the strand of cognitive science that grew out of Francisco Varela’s work and now runs through the 4E literature (cognition as embodied, embedded, enacted, extended). Its founding move is to deny that we sit inside our heads representing a pre-given world. A living being and its world instead bring each other forth through participation. I have opposable thumbs, so the objects around me show up as graspable; a creature built differently meets those same objects as something else entirely, as food or obstacle or nothing at all. The world an organism inhabits is cut to the shape of the body inhabiting it. There is no view from nowhere onto a finished reality, only some particular organism, in some particular situation, making sense.

Take this seriously and a row of comfortable assumptions begins to give way: about what knowledge is, about why our best scientific methods stumble on the things we most want to understand, about whether an insight that feels true can be trusted, and about whether truth itself survives once there is no neutral standpoint to check it from.

Every realm arises in relation. None stands alone. (Yama holding the wheel)

The reigning assumption is that to know is to hold a true proposition: the capital of France is Paris, a cat has four legs. John Vervaeke calls the overvaluing of this single mode propositional tyranny. Propositional knowing is the late arrival, the most recent and most derivative kind, and it rests on three older ones. Procedural knowing is skill, the know-how in riding a bicycle, which no quantity of propositions about balance will ever supply. Perspectival knowing is what it is like to occupy a situation, its felt salience. Participatory knowing is the most basic and the most ineffable: knowing by being, the way a living thing co-identifies itself and its world simply through being the kind of thing it is. Remove the older three and the propositions have nothing left to stand on. And participatory knowing is not a snapshot. It is a history. You became the knower you are through participation, and that history enters every new situation with you.

Once the unit of knowing is a whole person enmeshed in a world and a history, the standard machinery of psychology starts to misfire. Most of our statistics quietly assume ergodicity, a habit imported from classical physics: the average across many systems and the average across one system over time will agree, because the system carries no memory. Throw one die a hundred times, or a hundred dice once, and the distribution is the same. People are not dice. We learn, our history is constitutive of what we are, and even the act of measuring us alters us. For complex, person-specific phenomena the assumption is simply false, and much of what we throw away as noise is in fact signal. The repair is to invert the order of operations: rather than aggregate first and study the average, study each person’s trajectory and only then look across people for shared dynamics. What generalizes is seldom the outcome. It is the shape of the change.

A good example is the psychedelic experience, where the temptation is to treat the molecule as the active ingredient, the way paracetamol is: same input, same effect, the situation averages were built for. And paracetamol earns that treatment, because it acts on a phenomenon of low complexity. A psychedelic experience is the opposite case. The same compound at the same dose can open one person and traumatize the next, because what does the work is the lived experience the chemistry occasions, inside a particular context, for a particular person. The drug destabilizes a system; what that system reorganizes into depends on everything around it. This is why set and setting were never soft add-ons. They are where the transformation happens.

This cycle of destabilization and reorganization is the shape of change that does generalize. A system held in rigid patterns is loosened, and then settles into a new configuration. The same form shows up at every scale, and the scale is what matters. A small aha on a maths problem reorganizes one corner of your understanding and leaves the rest standing. Flow reorganizes how you meet a single activity while you are inside it. A mystical experience reorganizes the whole, the way you fit your world rather than any one task within it, which is why people return from it changed in a way that solving an equation never changes them.

What differs across these is not the mechanism but how far through the system the reorganization reaches. And it does not always settle well. The felt sense that something has clicked, call it fluency, is a poor detector of truth. Conspiracy theories are intensely fluent. They explain everything and feel like insight, yet they are usually false, cutting the person loose from lived reality even as the click grows stronger. Fluency takes its value from its context: the same click that marks insight in one life marks entrenchment in another. Whether the new order is wisdom or self-deception is a different question from how good it feels, and the feeling cannot answer it.

Each of those moves pushes knowing in the same direction: outward from the proposition, downward from the felt click, into the whole life of the person doing it. This turns the inquiry around. The first half asked how we know. What is left to settle is what we are answerable to, and what kind of reality holds us once we stop treating it as a finished thing standing over against us.

What we are answerable to has an older answer than the matching of statements to facts. A person can live truly or falsely, can be faithful to something or be a fake, and that kind of truth is visible. It supplies the test fluency could not: the test of a reorganization is what it does to a life. Self-deception has to keep whatever threatens it out of view, so a dishonest life perceives less; living honestly widens what is allowed to show up, which makes it a way of knowing the world more accurately, in conduct rather than in assertion. Knowing, being, and doing stop being separate territories, which is what participatory knowing meant all along: how you live is not downstream of what you know, it is part of how you know. You do not first secure the truth and then act on it. You enact a world, with others, and how you participate shapes what shows up in it.

What kind of reality this leaves us in is the harder question. If how you participate shapes what shows up, it can sound as though anything goes and every enacted world is as good as any other. It does not follow, and the reason matters. The enactive tradition takes its idea of groundlessness from Madhyamaka Buddhism: reality is empty of intrinsic, standalone existence, which is a long way from being empty of reality. Nothing final sits underneath, no substance into which everything resolves; there is participation all the way down, and we are among its participants.

And participation pushes back: not every world can be enacted, because bodies fail, projects collapse, and a sense-making that loses contact with what it is making sense of stops being viable. That is also why no method gets the last word. Rigor survives, but it asks for a humility about tools, a discipline of remembering that the one in your hand, the randomized trial included, is a perspective you have taken up, useful here, harmful elsewhere, and never the thing itself. What groundlessness rules out is nihilism. We are world-makers under constraint, our care and our suffering wholly real at the scale we live on, and the absence of a metaphysical floor takes none of the weight out of them.

Enactivism has shaped my own thinking a great deal, especially over the last year, and much of that I owe to Hüseyin Beyköylü, a cognitive scientist at Leiden University, with whom I’ve had some of the most enlightening conversations I’ve had on any of this. One of them is now a podcast episode, “Enactivism, Buddhist Epistemology & the Cosmic Play,” which works through the arguments and evidence behind everything above, but with more detail and extra connections, such as the cognitive continuum linking flow to mystical experience, neurophenomenology as an alternative to brain-only models of mind, the idiographic studies he ran at legal psychedelic retreats, and a long, difficult exchange on truth and the cosmic play, where I pushed back hardest, and which became one of my favorite conversations of my life.

If the claim that the mind reaches past the skull, and that meaning is something we participate in rather than invent, is one you want to see argued out properly, this is the conversation for you.
